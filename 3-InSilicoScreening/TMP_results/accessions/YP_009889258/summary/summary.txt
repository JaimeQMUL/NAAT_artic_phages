Concise Summary:
Two novel lytic phages were characterized for biocontrol potential against pathogenic bacteria. Phage propagation and initial bacterial culture were conducted at 37 °C. Antimicrobial activity assays were performed at 25 °C, while phage stability testing utilized 42 °C to mimic the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) temperature. The phages were isolated from sewage water and tested for stability across a range of pH values (2 to 7) under simulated gastric, bile salt, and intestinal fluid conditions at 42 °C. Encapsulation in sodium alginate was employed to enhance stability, particularly against acidic stress (e.g., pH 2.4 in simulated gastric fluid), which rapidly inactivated free phages. The study demonstrates the phages' efficacy under simulated gut conditions, suggesting potential for oral administration, but the primary thermal focus is on stability at 42 °C rather than adaptation to extreme temperatures.

Environmental Context:

Concise Summary:
The study investigated biofilm formation and essential oil efficacy against the pathogen across a range of temperatures, specifically 4 °C, 14 °C, 23 °C, and 37 °C. Biofilm formation was found to be significantly influenced by temperature, with enhanced formation observed at 4 °C, 23 °C, and 37 °C, and the highest overall potential noted at 37 °C. Biofilm degradation efficacy of tested essential oils was highest at 14 °C and 37 °C, and lowest at 23 °C. The isolates were sourced from chicken feces and appendix, suggesting an environmental context related to poultry products. The data strongly support temperature as a critical factor modulating biofilm characteristics and antimicrobial efficacy.
