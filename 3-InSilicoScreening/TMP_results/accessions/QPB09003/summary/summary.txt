Paper: PMC13211687.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:
- Most phages targeting bacterial isolates from food animals remain stable within the range of 4–60 °C.
- Phages generally exhibited greater stability at lower temperature ranges (≤37 °C).
- The gut temperature is maintained around 37 °C, which phages tolerate and easily pass through.
- Phage titer declined when stored at −20 °C and −80 °C for 90 days.

- Inactivation of most phages was observed at ≥70 °C, potentially due to the denaturation of capsid proteins and the degradation of nucleic acids.
- The thermal tolerance of phages varies between studies, necessitating prior assessment for standard formulation and storage.

- The study analyzed samples from freshwater aquaculture environments, specifically associated with three types of aquaculture ponds (grass carp, crayfish, and crab ponds). The samples analyzed included water, sediment, and intestinal contents.

Concise Summary:
This metagenomic study characterized the resistome and mobilome across different freshwater aquaculture modes (grass carp, crayfish, and crab ponds) using water, sediment, and intestinal samples. The analysis identified the intestinal microbiome as a primary hotspot for antibiotic resistance gene (ARG) and mobile genetic element (MGE) accumulation, particularly in one specific aquaculture species. High-risk ARGs were found to be significantly enriched in the gut compared to other compartments. Furthermore, the study noted the co-localization of ARGs, MGEs, and virulence factor genes within prophage sequences, suggesting phage-mediated co-dissemination of resistance and virulence traits. The analysis was conducted at both contig and MAG levels, revealing that a significant proportion of ARG-carrying MAGs were multidrug-resistant (MDR). No direct information regarding the thermal physiology, optimal growth temperature, or temperature adaptation of the associated microbes was presented.
