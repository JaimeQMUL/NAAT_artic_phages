Abstract

Objectives

This study comprehensively evaluates the in vitro antibacterial activity of eravacycline against a wide range of significant Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria collected across China.

Methods

A total of 1250 non-duplicate clinical isolates representing 12 genera and 21 species were collected from 40 hospitals between 2017 and 2019 across China. Minimal inhibitory concentrations were determined using the broth microdilution method in accordance with the standards set by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute.

Results

Eravacycline demonstrated potent in vitro activity against most tested bacterial species. The MIC90 values ranged from 0.25 to 2 mg/L for Enterobacterales, except for Proteus vulgaris (4 mg/L). According to the FDA breakpoints, Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae exhibited susceptibility rates of 98.9% and 70.9%, respectively. Eravacycline achieved an MIC90 value of 1 mg/L against Acinetobacter baumannii, which was the lowest among all agents tested. For Gram-positive isolates, eravacycline displayed MIC90 values of 0.5 mg/L for MRSA and methicillin-resistant CoNS (MRCNS), 0.03 mg/L for vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium and 0.03 mg/L for Streptococcus pneumoniae, surpassing or equalling tigecycline and markedly outperforming doxycycline.

Conclusions

This large-scale multicentre surveillance study demonstrated that eravacycline exhibited potent in vitro activity against major multidrug-resistant Gram-negative and Gram-positive pathogens, indicating that it could be a promising therapeutic option for treating infections caused by these strains.

Introduction

The extensive and inappropriate use of antibiotics has led to the rapid selection of multidrug-resistant pathogens, leaving clinicians with limited therapeutic options.1 A particularly concerning trend is the global dissemination of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales, carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa and carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, which are frequently associated with high morbidity and mortality.2,3 A similar concern is the persistence of multidrug-resistant Gram-positive pathogens, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and vancomycin-resistant Enterococci, which remain major causes of both hospital- and community-acquired infections.4,5 According to the WHO global antimicrobial resistance surveillance system, the resistance rates of imipenem and meropenem for carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae were 10.63% and 12.34%.6 Methicillin-resistant S. aureus remains a leading cause of bloodstream and skin infections, with the resistance rates approximately 50% in 15 European countries7 and over 40% in some states of America.8 In China, data from the China Antimicrobial Surveillance Network (CHINET) demonstrated a similarly worrisome trend. The resistance rate of K. pneumoniae to carbapenems increased from below 5% in 2005 to over 20% in 2017, and A. baumannii resistance rate to carbapenems increased from below 40% in 2005 to over 75% in 2019 nationwide.9 Notably, methicillin-resistant S. aureus isolation rates surpassed 40% in some regions, including Tibet, Shanghai and Jiangsu.10

Eravacycline, a fully synthetic fluorocycline antibiotic, represents a significant advancement over other traditional tetracyclines. Approved by the US Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency in 2018 for treating complicated intra-abdominal infections, it exhibits a unique mechanism of action by binding to the 30S ribosomal subunit, thereby inhibiting bacterial protein synthesis when administered.11,12 In contrast to earlier tetracyclines, eravacycline demonstrates potent antibacterial activity against a broad range of multidrug-resistant pathogens with typical tetracycline resistance mechanisms, including efflux pump overexpression and ribosomal protection.13 In recent years, several studies worldwide have evaluated in vitro activity of eravacycline. However, the number and diversity of clinical isolates tested, as well as the range of comparator agents assessed, have been relatively limited. We conducted this large-scale multicentre study to comprehensively evaluate the antibacterial activity of eravacycline against isolates collected from CHINET work between 2017 and 2019.

Materials and methods

Clinical isolates

A total of 1250 non-duplicate clinical isolates were collected between 2017 and 2019 from 40 hospitals across 25 provinces, municipalities and autonomous regions, relying on the CHINET. The isolates were primarily recovered from blood, pleural and peritoneal fluids, and sputum samples, including 182 Escherichia coli (87 ESBL-negative, 85 ESBL-positive and 10 carbapenem-resistant strains), 203 K. pneumoniae (61 ESBL-negative, 16 ESBL-positive and 126 carbapenem-resistant strains), 14 Klebsiella oxytoca, 67 Enterobacter cloacae, 43 Citrobacter freundii, 34 Morganella morganii, 27 Proteus mirabilis, 21 Proteus vulgaris, 56 A. baumannii (10 carbapenem-susceptible, 1 carbapenem-intermediate and 45 carbapenem-resistant strains), 125 Haemophilus influenzae (50 β-lactamase-negative and 75 β-lactamase-positive strains), 43 Moraxella catarrhalis strains, 120 S. aureus (57 MRSA and 63 MSSA strains), 51 CoNS [31 methicillin-resistant CoNS (MRCNS) and 20 methicillin-susceptible CoNS (MSCNS) strains], 50 Enterococcus faecalis, 59 Enterococcus faecium (14 vancomycin-resistant and 45 vancomycin-susceptible strains) and 155 Streptococcus pneumoniae (30 penicillin-susceptible strains, minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) ≤0.06 mg/L, 19 penicillin-intermediate strains, MICs = 0.125–1 mg/L and 106 penicillin-resistant strains, MICs ≥2 mg/L, interpreted according to oral penicillin V CLSI 2025 breakpoints). Species identification was confirmed by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (bioMérieux, France).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The MICs of eravacycline and comparator agents were determined using the broth microdilution method according to the recommendations of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M07.14 The following antibiotics were included as comparators: tigecycline, minocycline, doxycycline, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, ceftazidime, cefepime, imipenem, meropenem, amikacin and colistin. The MICs interpretation for eravacycline and tigecycline followed the FDA15 (Table 1) or EUCAST breakpoints (Table 2).16 For all other comparator agents, the MICs were interpreted using the CLSI breakpoints.17  E. coli ATCC 25922, E. coli ATCC 35218, P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853, S. aureus ATCC 29213, E. faecalis ATCC 29212, S. pneumoniae ATCC 49619, H. influenzae ATCC 49766 and H. influenzae ATCC 49247 were used as the quality control strains for antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Results

In vitro activity of eravacycline and comparator agents against Gram-negative bacteria

Eravacycline had excellent activity against 182 E. coli isolates based on the FDA breakpoints, including ESBL-negative [MIC50/90s, 0.125/0.25 mg/L; 100% (87/87) susceptible], ESBL-positive [MIC50/90s, 0.125/0.5 mg/L; 97.6% (83/85) susceptible] and carbapenem-resistant strains [MIC50/90s, 0.25/0.5 mg/L; 100% (10/10) susceptible], followed by amikacin [100% (87/87), 96.5% (82/85) and 100% (10/10) susceptible, respectively] and tigecycline [100% (87/87), 98.8% (82/85) and 100% (8/10) susceptible, respectively] (Table 3). However, according to the FDA breakpoints, eravacycline demonstrated moderate activity against 16 ESBL-positive and 126 carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae isolates [both MIC50/90s of 0.5/2 mg/L; 68.8% (11/16) and 63.5% (80/126) susceptible, respectively] (Table 4). All ESBL-negative and ESBL-positive E. coli and K. pneumoniae strains (100%) were susceptible to carbapenems (including ertapenem, imipenem and meropenem) without any resistant isolates identified. Although ceftriaxone (both 100% susceptible), ceftazidime [95.4% (83/87) and 100% (61/61) susceptible] and cefepime [95.4% (83/87) and 100% (61/61) susceptible] exhibited good activity against ESBL-negative E. coli and K. pneumoniae strains, they had limited activity against ESBL-producers with 0%, 48.2% (41/85) and 17.6% (15/85) susceptible for E. coli, and 0%, 37.5% (6/16) and 0% susceptible for K. pneumoniae, and exhibited negligible activity against carbapenemase-producing pathogens with the susceptibility rates of 0%, 10% (1/10) and 0% for E. coli, and 0%, 1.6% (2/126) and 0% for K. pneumoniae, respectively (Tables 3 and 4). According to the FDA breakpoints, eravacycline exhibited good activity against K. oxytoca, E. cloacae and C. freundii with the susceptibility rates of 92.9% (13/14), 85.1% (57/67) and 86.0% (37/43), respectively (Table 5), while showing poor activity against M. morganii [38.2% (13/34) susceptible], P. mirabilis (0% susceptible) and P. vulgaris [19.0% (4/21) susceptible] (Table 6). Among 27 Enterobacterales isolates with tigecycline MICs > 2 mg/L, only 3.7% (1/27) remained susceptible to eravacycline.

Discussion

To date, this nationwide multicentre surveillance study has provided the most comprehensive evaluation of the in vitro activity of eravacycline against a wide range of significant Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria collected across China from 2017 to 2019. The number and diversity of clinical isolates included in our investigation were unprecedented among all published studies about the in vitro activity of eravacycline. As a novel fluorocycline antibiotic, eravacycline represents a significant advancement in addressing multidrug-resistant infections, particularly for complicated intra-abdominal infections where limited therapeutic options exist.18 Structurally, eravacycline is a fully synthetic derivative of the tetracycline class, featuring a fluorine atom at the C7 position and a pyrrolidinoacetamido group at the C9 position on the D-ring.19 These chemical modifications confer enhanced ribosomal binding affinity and stability against both major tetracycline resistance mechanisms (efflux pump overexpression and ribosomal protection), thereby broadening its antibacterial spectrum and improving potency compared to earlier tetracyclines.20 Previous global surveillance programmes have reported eravacycline MIC90 values generally 2- to 4-fold lower than those of tigecycline against Enterobacterales and A. baumannii,21 which is consistent with our study.

Consistent with these structural advantages, eravacycline has demonstrated potent in vitro activity against a wide range of Enterobacterales, A. baumannii, S. aureus, Enterococcus spp. and fastidious bacteria such as S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae and M. catarrhalis.22,23 Our study revealed that eravacycline displayed robust in vitro activity against Enterobacterales, with MIC90s of 0.25–2 mg/L against almost all species, except for P. vulgaris (4 mg/L), and MIC90s of 0.5–2 mg/L against multidrug-resistant Enterobacterales, which aligns with the research reported by Hawser.23 Notably, according to the FDA breakpoints, 98.9% (180/182) of E. coli isolates were susceptible to eravacycline, including 100% of ESBL-negative isolates (n = 87), 97.6% of ESBL-positive isolates (n = 83) and 100% of carbapenem-resistant isolates (n = 10). Likewise, 70.9% (144/203) of K. pneumoniae strains were susceptible to eravacycline based on the FDA breakpoints, including 86.9% of ESBL-negative strains (n = 53), 68.8% of ESBL-positive strains (n = 11) and 63.5% of carbapenem-resistant strains (n = 80). These susceptibility rates were higher than those reported by Liao et al. in Guangdong24 and Zou et al. in Zhejiang.25 This discrepancy may be attributable not only to geographic variation and differences in study settings, but also to differences in carbapenemase gene types25,26 and efflux pump expression.26–28 Additionally, variations in isolate numbers and the proportion of multidrug-resistant strains in this study may also have contributed to this discrepancy. For example, the relatively small numbers of carbapenem-resistant E. coli (n = 10) and ESBL-positive K. pneumoniae (n = 16) isolates may affect the stability and robustness of results. M. morganii, P. mirabilis and P. vulgaris exhibited low susceptibility to eravacycline. This may be attributable to several mechanisms, including overexpression of the AcrAB efflux pump,26,29 the dissemination of the eravacycline-inactivating enzyme gene tet(X4)29,30 and mutations in 16S rRNA or other binding site proteins.29,31 Furthermore, for most Enterobacterales in this study, the susceptibility rates to eravacycline were lower than those to tigecycline. This difference is likely attributable to the FDA susceptible breakpoints ≤0.5 mg/L for eravacycline and ≤2 mg/L for tigecycline.

Compared with Gram-negative isolates, Gram-positive bacteria appeared to be more susceptible to eravacycline, with MIC90s of 0.06–0.5 mg/L for Staphylococcus spp. (including MRSA and MRCNS), 0.03–0.125 mg/L for Enterococcus spp. (including vancomycin-resistant E. faecium) and 0.015–0.03 mg/L for S. pneumoniae (including penicillin-resistant strains). Eravacycline exhibited MIC90s of 0.5 mg/L, 0.5 mg/L and 0.03 mg/L for MRSA, MRCNS and vancomycin-resistant E. faecium, respectively, outperforming tigecycline and doxycycline by 2- to 32-fold, which aligns with its stability against tetracycline-specific efflux systems [encoded by tet(A) and tet(K)], a limitation observed in earlier tetracyclines.32 Furthermore, eravacycline maintained potent activity against S. pneumoniae, including penicillin-resistant isolates, with MIC90 values of 0.03 mg/L, 4-fold and 64-fold lower than those of tigecycline and doxycycline. Such efficacy against typical respiratory pathogens positions eravacycline as a potential candidate antimicrobial agent for hospital-acquired pneumonia.

Although there are no breakpoints for eravacycline provided by either the FDA or EUCAST against A. baumannii, it is noteworthy that eravacycline exhibited the lowest MIC90 values (1 mg/L, 0.25 mg/L, 0.06 mg/L and 0.03 mg/L, respectively) among all the tested agents against relevant bacteria species. However, compared with some previous studies,23,33,34 the number of CRAB (n = 45) isolates in our study was relatively small, which is a limitation in our study.

An important limitation of our study is that molecular characterization of carbapenem resistance mechanisms was not conducted for carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales isolates. However, unlike β-lactamase inhibitor combinations such as ceftazidime–avibactam, the in vitro activity of eravacycline is less likely to be affected by strains producing different carbapenemases. Although we were unable to determine the proportion of carbapenemase-producing isolates and to define the distribution of specific carbapenemase types, such as KPC, NDM and OXA-48-like enzymes, this limitation was unlikely to substantially affect the in vitro activity of eravacycline against CRE isolates. In addition, the in vitro activities of some novel agents, including ceftazidime–avibactam, meropenem–vaborbactam, imipenem–relebactam, cefiderocol and aztreonam–avibactam, were not evaluated in our study, which may limit a comprehensive comparison between eravacycline and other novel agents against carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales.

Another limitation is that although eravacycline was primarily indicated for complicated intra-abdominal infections (cIAIs), the in vitro MIC data of isolates recovered from blood or sputum samples remain valuable for evaluating the potential antimicrobial spectrum of eravacycline. Nevertheless, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to the clinical treatment of non-cIAI infections.

Conclusions

Eravacycline had potent in vitro activity against ESBL-positive and carbapenem-resistant E. coli, ESBL-negative K. pneumoniae, K. oxytoca, E. cloacae, C. freundii, carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis, MRSA, MRCNS, vancomycin-resistant E. faecium and penicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae, indicating that it could be a promising therapeutic option for treating infections caused by these strains in healthcare facilities.
