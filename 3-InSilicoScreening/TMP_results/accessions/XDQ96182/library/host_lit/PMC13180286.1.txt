Background

The oral microbiome evolves across the lifespan, with alterations influenced by both general and oral health conditions. In older adults, frailty and oral hypofunction are common and may contribute to these microbial changes. This systematic review evaluated changes in the oral microbiota of older adults, frail individuals, and those with oral frailty or hypofunction (e.g., fewer teeth), and explored how these conditions relate to one another.

Methods

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across MEDLINE (PubMed), Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. The last search was performed on 16 November 2025. Studies reporting the effects of aging, frailty, or oral frailty/hypofunction on oral bacteria or microbiome were included. All eligible studies were descriptively analyzed. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale.

Results

Thirty-four studies were included, of which 16 used conventional microbiological methods and 18 used 16S rRNA sequencing. Findings on microbial diversity (alpha and beta) associated with aging were inconsistent; however, significant compositional changes were observed in frailty. At the phylum level, Bacillota decreased and Bacteroidota increased, and at the genus level, Streptococcus, Veillonella, and Haemophilus decreased, whereas Prevotella increased in older adults compared with younger adults. These patterns were reversed in frail individuals and older adults with edentulism or fewer teeth compared with healthy older adults.

Conclusions

Frailty and having fewer teeth appear to exert a stronger influence on the oral microbiome than healthy aging. While older adults may develop a microbial community distinct from that of younger adults, frailty is associated with a microbial imbalance, demonstrated by a reduction in taxa important for biofilm formation and an increase in pathogenic taxa. Similar patterns are observed in individuals with fewer teeth, though evidence linking other aspects of oral hypofunction remains limited.

Clinical significance

Oral microbiome alterations may be associated with frailty. However, current evidence is not yet sufficient to support their use as diagnostic biomarkers. Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether these associations are correlational or causal, which is essential for future biomarker development and microbiome-targeted interventions.

Background

The oral microbiome changes across the lifespan and has the capacity to return to a stable state of equilibrium [1]. These changes occur in additional response to general health conditions such as digestive system diseases, cancers, cardiovascular diseases, Alzheimer’s disease, diabetes, and rheumatoid arthritis [2], as well as oral hygiene and oral health conditions such as caries, periodontitis, implantitis, oral mucositis, and cancer [3–5]. In addition, external factors such as diet, alcohol intake, and smoking influence the oral microbiome. Disruption of this balance, known as dysbiosis, is characterized by reduced microbial diversity, loss of beneficial species, and expansion of pathogenic species [6].

As people age, frailty commonly develops, characterized by a reduction in strength, endurance, and physiological reserves across the neuromuscular, metabolic, and immune systems [7]. The deterioration in functionality affects not only physical health but also oral health [8, 9], leading to oral frailty (OF) and, subsequently, oral hypofunction (OH). OF represents an intermediate stage between robust function and its decline, characterized by fewer teeth and reduced eating and communicating function [10]. OH is characterized by impairments in oral hygiene, salivation, occlusal force, tongue-lip motor function, tongue pressure, masticatory performance, and swallowing ability [11, 12].

Interestingly, the gut microbiome profile of individuals with frailty differs from that of non-frail older adults [13, 14]. Studies further indicate that alterations in the gut microbiome may contribute to the development of frailty, suggesting a bidirectional relationship between the gut and frailty [15, 16]. Given the ability of oral bacteria to transfer to the gut, changes observed in the gut may also occur earlier in the oral cavity [17]. Therefore, it can be postulated that frailty may influence the oral microbiome in a similar bidirectional fashion.

Previous systematic reviews focusing on 16S rRNA sequencing studies have described age-related shifts in oral microbial diversity and changes in dominant taxa, but the available evidence remains inconsistent [18–20]. A systematic review by Sarafidou et al. reported that some studies identified associations between oral microbiome profiles and frailty [21]. To date, however, no systematic review has examined the relevance of these microbial changes to OH that may influence the oral microbiome, despite evidence showing that the risk of OH increases with age [22] and that OH is correlated with physical frailty [23–25].

With the emergence of additional studies, this review aims to evaluate changes in the oral microbiota of older adults, frail individuals, and those with OF/OH, and explore how these conditions relate to one another. Moreover, we integrate findings from conventional oral microbiology methods with those from high-resolution molecular biology approaches to bridge existing evidence gaps, thereby strengthening the foundation for future investigations of the oral microbial community.

Methods

This systematic review was carried out and reported in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [26, 27]. The completed PRISMA checklist is provided (see Additional file 1). The protocol of this review was registered with PROSPERO (the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) under the registration number CRD42024628795.

Search strategy and selection process

The PECO (Population, Exposure, Comparison, and Outcome) framework was used to formulate the review question and construct the search strategy. The strategy was refined following a pilot search to address any identified issues. The search was performed on 16 December 2024. The details of all search terms are provided in Table 1.

Search results from each database were imported into Rayyan (https://www.rayyan.ai), an online platform for facilitating systematic review procedures. The artificial intelligence automatically detected and removed duplicate records as the initial step, followed by manual removal of undetected duplicates by reviewers. Two investigators (K.S. and T.F.) individually screened titles and abstracts within the platform. Following this step, studies underwent full-text review, and then eligible studies were selected for inclusion. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and unresolved disagreements were decided by a third reviewer (M.S.)

When full-text articles were not available or lacked sufficient detail, the reviewers contacted the corresponding author to provide more details. The reference cross-checks from the previous reviews and manual searches were performed to identify studies that were not discovered by the search strategy. In addition, an updated search was performed on 16 November 2025 to retrieve studies published after the initial search.

Data collection process and data items

Data were collected by K.S. and subsequently checked for accuracy and validity by T.S. For studies using conventional methods, extracted data included participant characteristics and sample size, sampling types, detection techniques, main outcomes, and major findings. For sequencing-based studies, additional methodological details were collected, including targeted 16S rRNA variable regions, sequencing platforms, and reference databases used. Moreover, main findings related to alpha-diversity, beta-diversity, and relative abundances of taxa were extracted for synthesis. When the required details were lacking, the reviewers contacted the corresponding author to obtain additional information.

Study risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias for the included association studies was simultaneously assessed by two investigators (K.S. and T.F.) using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [28]. The assessment comprises three domains, including selection, comparability, and outcomes, with total scores ranging from 0 to 10. Scores of 0–3 indicate high, 4–6 moderate, and 7–10 low risk of bias.

Summary measures and synthesis of results

A qualitative synthesis was conducted for all included studies, and findings were descriptively summarized. A meta-analysis was not performed due to substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies, including differences in sample types, sequencing approaches, analytical pipelines, and reported outcomes [29–31].

Results

Study selection

Three databases were searched [MEDLINE (PubMed): n = 4643; CENTRAL: n = 936; Web of Science: n = 873] and detected 6452 records. Duplicate records were automatically removed by the software for 658 records. A total of 5794 records were screened for title and abstract, resulting in 5700 irrelevant records. The list of 94 related records was sought for retrieval; all records were successfully retrieved and underwent further full-text screening. A list of 72 records was excluded by consensus between two reviewers due to ineligible study design (n = 46), ineligible participant age (n = 16), review articles (n = 8), irrelevant study outcomes (n = 1), and unclear reporting of participants’ age without response from authors (n = 1) [32]. Finally, 22 records related to the inclusion criteria were included. Additionally, 12 records were identified through a cross-check of the references and an updated search. A final list of 34 records was qualified for data extraction and final analysis. The study selection process is demonstrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Study characteristics

Conventional methods

Sixteen cross-sectional studies employed conventional microbiological methods [33–48]. Fourteen of these were published between 1991 and 2015, with only two published in 2023 [35, 37]. Eight studies aimed to compare bacterial species between different age groups. One study compared older adults with and without frailty in relation to pneumonia-associated bacteria [46], and another examined older adults with and without oral frailty [35]. Seven studies compared older adults with and without components of oral hypofunction, such as edentulism, reduced number of teeth, swallowing problems, or reduced tongue pressure.

There was considerable heterogeneity in the location of sampling within the oral cavity. Most studies used unstimulated saliva to represent the oral microbiome (seven studies), followed by supragingival plaque (six studies). Oral rinse and tongue plaque were each used in three studies, while stimulated saliva was used in two studies. Subgingival plaque was used in four studies, focusing on periodontal pathogens. Other studies mainly focused on cariogenic bacteria, including Streptococcus mutans and lactobacilli (five studies). A few studies reported on opportunistic pathogens or pneumonia-related bacteria (four studies). In terms of analytical methods, bacterial culture was the most common technique (used in 50% of the studies), followed by DNA-specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR), DNA hybridization probes, quantitative PCR (qPCR), and adherence assays. 16S rRNA sequencing methods

Seventeen cross-sectional studies utilized high-throughput sequencing technologies and were published between 2015 and 2025 [49–66]. Eleven of these studies aimed to assess age-related differences in the oral microbiome: six studies compared three age groups, three studies compared two age groups, and two studies compared six or seven age groups. Three studies compared middle-aged (45–64 years) and older adults (65–84 years), two studies compared older adults with long-lived older adults (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 85 years), while the remainder compared younger (25–44 years) and older adults. Two studies examined differences between older adults (65 years or older) with and without frailty, while three studies evaluated the association with frailty from national cohorts (the United Kingdom, the United States of America, and Canada). Although no studies directly compared older adults with and without oral frailty/hypofunction, two studies focused on their components, such as edentulism, reduced dentition, or xerostomia.

Given the potential influence of oral and systemic health on microbiome composition, participant exclusion criteria were explored. Five studies did not report any exclusion criteria. Three studies included at least one exclusion criterion, such as recent antibiotic use (within three months) or the presence of pathological lesions at the sampling site. The remaining studies applied multiple criteria, typically excluding participants who had used antibiotics within 1–12 months, systemic diseases, less than 6–26 remaining teeth, periodontal disease, or were denture wearers.

Again, we noted heterogeneity in the sampling location. The most used sample type was unstimulated saliva (eight studies), followed by oral rinse (five studies), stimulated saliva (three studies), and tongue plaque (two studies). Buccal mucosa, supragingival, and subgingival plaque were used in each study. Seventeen studies employed 16S rRNA gene sequencing, while one used shotgun metagenomic sequencing. Among the 16S rRNA studies, five targeted the V3–V4 region, four targeted V1–V3, three targeted V4, and one each targeted V1–V2, V3, V4–V5, and full-length 16S rRNA (V1–V9). Most studies used the Illumina MiSeq platform for sequencing. Reference databases varied across studies: four studies used the Human Oral Microbiome Database (HOMD), four studies used SILVA, three studies used Greengenes, and two studies used expanded HOMD. Findings from conventional methods

Three studies by Beighton et al. [33], Percival et al. [44], and Fure et al. [38] using saliva samples, both unstimulated and stimulated, reported significantly higher levels of Streptococcus mutans and lactobacilli in older adults compared to younger adults, suggesting an increased caries risk in older populations. The impact of denture use on the oral microbiome yielded conflicting results. While Beighton et al. and Percival et al. found no significant differences in Streptococcus mutans and lactobacilli levels between denture-wearing older adults and younger adults, Marsh et al. [41] and Fure et al. reported significant increases in these bacterial counts among denture users.

In studies investigating periodontal pathogens, Savitt et al. [45] observed that older adults had significantly higher levels of Porphyromonas gingivalis, but significantly lower levels of Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, compared to younger adults. In contrast, Haffajee et al. [39] found no significant differences in periodontal pathogens between older adults with well-maintained periodontium and periodontally healthy younger adults. Two studies by Fernandes et al. [36] and Kishi et al. [40] demonstrated significantly higher levels of periodontal pathogens in dentate older adults compared to edentulous individuals, indicating the continued relevance of periodontal microbial load in individuals with natural teeth.

Senpuku et al. [46] reported that frail older adults were found to harbor higher levels of pneumonia-associated bacteria, including Staphylococcus spp., Pseudomonas spp., and Candida albicans. Similarly, Chen et al. [35] found that individuals with oral frailty exhibited a greater proportion of pneumonia-associated bacteria, including Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, and Serratia marcescens. Notably, both studies also reported increased levels of Enterobacterales in individuals with frailty and oral frailty. Studies by Tada et al. [47] and Ortega et al. [43] reported that older adults with swallowing difficulties had significantly elevated levels of opportunistic pathogens, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, emphasizing the increased risk of pneumonia in individuals with frailty, oral frailty, or swallowing dysfunction. Major findings of individual studies are summarized in Table 2.

Findings from 16S rRNA sequencing methods

Alpha-diversity

Alpha-diversity encompasses different indices (e.g., Observed OTU, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson) that weight richness and evenness differently and can respond differently to preprocessing and taxonomic resolution. Therefore, the direction and size of age- or frailty-associated effects may depend on the chosen metric, and measures may not show a positive linear relationship. Findings on alpha-diversity were inconsistent across studies comparing older adults with younger or middle-aged adults, with variability also influenced by sample type and taxonomic resolution used in the analysis. Detailed alpha-diversity findings and the indices used for measurement are summarized (see Additional file 3).

Four studies reported decreased alpha-diversity (reflecting both richness and evenness) in older adults compared to young adults [54, 55, 59, 66], three found no significant differences [56, 62, 64], and one observed an increase in diversity [58]. When comparing older adults with middle-aged adults, one study reported a reduction in diversity [59], while another found no significant difference [53]. In a study by Guo et al. [51], which included three age groups of older adults aged 65–80 years, no significant change in diversity was observed. Interestingly, studies by Wu et al. [62] and Ji et al. [52] found that long-lived older adults exhibited reduced alpha-diversity compared to normal older adults.

Regarding frailty, Ogawa et al. [58] reported a significant reduction in diversity at the phylum level among frail older adults. However, no significant differences were observed at deeper taxonomic levels (genus and species) in studies by both Ogawa et al. and Larson et al. [54]. In contrast, edentulism, having fewer than 20 teeth, and the presence of dry mouth were consistently associated with reduced alpha-diversity across multiple studies [50, 64].

Beta-diversity

Beta-diversity is similarly method-dependent, with results varying according to the distance metric used (e.g., Bray–Curtis versus weighted or unweighted UniFrac), whether analyses emphasize abundance or presence/absence data, and how compositionality and normalization are handled. As a result, studies may detect (or miss) group separation depending on the chosen dissimilarity measure and ordination or statistical testing approach. Detailed beta-diversity findings and the metrics used for measurement are summarized in (see Additional file 4).

Beta-diversity results were also mixed when comparing older and younger adults. Five studies reported significant differences in community composition [54–56, 59, 61], while two studies found no significant differences [62, 63]. Similarly, when comparing older adults to middle-aged adults, one study reported a significant difference [53], and another reported none [59]. Interestingly, Schwartz et al. [59] found significant differences between older and young adults but no difference between older and middle-aged adults. When comparing among three age groups of older adults aged 65–80 years [51], as well as older adults versus long-lived older adults, there were no significant differences in beta-diversity [52, 62].

More consistent differences were observed between frail and non-frail older adults. Studies by Ogawa et al. [58], Yang et al. [65], and Larson et al. [54] reported significant shifts in microbial community composition associated with frailty. Additionally, Gazdeck et al. [50] reported significant differences between edentulous and dentate older adults.

Relative abundance of taxa

Distinct trends of relative abundance of taxa varied between two main comparison groups: (i) older adults versus younger adults, and (ii) older adults versus frailty, edentulism, or fewer than 20 teeth. At the phylum level, Bacillota was found to be less abundant in older adults [55, 63] but more abundant in frail [57] and edentulous individuals [50]. Similarly, Streptococcus (within Bacillota) followed this trend, with an additional study reporting higher abundance in long-lived older adults [52] and individuals with fewer than 20 teeth [64]. Veillonella (also within Bacillota) showed reduced abundance in older adults [55, 58] but increased abundance in frail individuals [57]. Likewise, Haemophilus (within Pseudomonadota) was found to be less abundant in older adults [56, 62] but more abundant in frailty [57].

In contrast, Bacteroidota was more abundant in older adults [55, 63] but less abundant in frail individuals [57]. Prevotella (within Bacteroidota) reflected this trend and was also found to be less abundant in edentulous older adults [50]. Interestingly, Prevotella and Tannerella (both within Bacteroidota) were more abundant in older adults but less abundant in long-lived individuals [52]. Some genera demonstrated opposing abundance trends in frail versus edentulous individuals. For example, Veillonella (within Bacillota) and Actinomyces (within Actinomycetota) were more abundant in frail older adults [57] but less abundant in edentulous older adults [50]. In addition, when comparing among three age groups of older adults aged 65–80 years, there were no significant differences in abundance taxa [51].

A few taxa showed similar trends across both comparison groups. Selenomonas (within Bacillota) was more abundant in both older adults [58, 66] and frail individuals [57]. In contrast, Fusobacterium (within Fusobacteriota) and Campylobacter (within Campylobacteriota) were less abundant in older adults [55, 58, 66] and in frail individuals [57]. Additionally, Capnocytophaga (within Bacteroidota) and Fusobacterium showed consistently lower abundance in frail individuals [57] and those with fewer than 20 teeth [64]. Other relative abundance taxa findings with inconsistent patterns across studies are presented (see Additional file 5).

Due to the limitation of species-level detection by metagenomic analysis of 16S rRNA sequencing and inconsistent findings between the studies, species-level results were omitted from the summary [67].

Age-associated patterns in alpha-diversity and taxa

Several studies that included participants spanning from childhood to older adulthood investigated associations between microbial taxa and age. Four studies using different sample types found a positive association between alpha-diversity and increasing age [49, 60, 61, 63], with the exception of one study by Yang et al. [65], which did not support this trend. Notably, Yang et al. also found a positive association between number of teeth and alpha-diversity (Shannon and Simpson). In contrast, three studies reported a negative association between frailty and alpha diversity [49, 60, 65].

At the genus level, four taxa were consistently associated with aging across multiple studies: Veillonella [49, 60], Granulicatella [61, 63], Prevotella [51, 52, 61], Alloprevotella [49, 61], and Haemophilus [60, 61] showed negative associations with increasing age. While Comamonas [60, 61] showed a positive association with age. Other genera with inconsistent findings across studies are summarized (see Additional file 6).

Risk of bias

The risk of bias among studies utilizing conventional microbiological methods was assessed as primarily low in 11 studies, while five studies were rated as having a moderate risk of bias [37, 40, 42, 45, 46]. Similarly, among studies employing 16S rRNA gene sequencing methods, 14 studies were assessed as having a low risk of bias, and four studies were rated as moderate [49, 58, 60, 65]. Discussion

Principal findings

This systematic review highlights that frailty, rather than aging alone, is closely linked to microbial dysbiosis. Although studies comparing older adults with younger or middle-aged adults regarding alpha- and beta-diversity remain inconclusive, differences in taxonomic abundance were observed, indicating that microbial changes occur with aging. During healthy aging, marked differences in microbial diversity or taxonomic abundance may not be evident until very advanced age (e.g., > 85 years), at which point a reduction in alpha-diversity may occur while beta-diversity remains relatively stable. In contrast, frail older adults exhibit significant changes in beta-diversity from healthy aging, despite non-significant changes in alpha-diversity. This pattern suggests that frailty may be associated with a restructuring of community composition (i.e., which taxa dominate and how individuals differ from one another) rather than a uniform change in within-sample richness or evenness.

Taken together, the oral microbiome of older adults may shift toward an adapted community consistent with the natural resilience of the oral microbiome [1]. In contrast, the microbiome of frail individuals tends to shift toward a microbial imbalance, demonstrated by a reduction in taxa important for biofilm formation and an increase in pathogenic taxa. This phenomenon may also be explained by the reduced ability to maintain oral hygiene, which facilitates the overgrowth of pathogenic taxa, while a reduced number of teeth results in the loss of oral niches. Furthermore, oral hypofunction, which commonly co-occurs with frailty, can further restrict eating ability, diminish natural self-cleaning mechanisms, and weaken protective oral defenses, collectively accelerating microbial imbalance.

Taxonomic abundance potentially associated with oral microbial dysbiosis

At the phylum level, similar to the gut microbiome, Bacillota decreases, while Bacteroidota increases with aging [68]. Interestingly, a reverse trend is observed in frail individuals, supporting the concept of dysbiosis. Additionally, frail individuals show reduced levels of Fusobacterium and Campylobacter, both of which play essential roles in biofilm formation, suggesting potential disruption of biofilm stability. In particular, Fusobacterium nucleatum is considered a key bridging organism in dental plaque maturation, and its depletion may therefore influence overall community organization [69, 70].

Studies found that Streptococcus and Veillonella, which typically decrease with age, show an increase in frail older adults. This shift could reflect poor oral hygiene and the development of a dysbiotic biofilm in this population. Streptococcus is a core early colonizer in the oral cavity, and Veillonella utilizes lactate produced by Streptococcus, suggesting a metabolic cross-feeding process in dysbiosis [71]. Interestingly, Singh et al. [72] found that Streptococcus was significantly increased in the oral microbiome of unhealthy compared to healthy aging cohorts. However, Streptococcus could also increase in older adults with edentulism or fewer than 20 teeth, regardless of frailty [50, 64, 73].

Moreover, frail older adults, especially those with fewer teeth, showed microbial shifts similar to those seen in individuals with edentulism. This highlights the potential overlap between frailty and edentulism, where the loss of oral niches and functional decline both contribute to microbial dysbiosis. Interestingly, Veillonella (within Bacillota) was higher when Fusobacterium (within Fusobacteriota) was lower in frail compared to non-frail individuals, indicating specific microbial signatures of frailty. Findings from Kageyama et al. [74] explained that older adults have two community types: type I, who are dominated with Veillonella and Prevotella, exhibit a higher risk of all-cause death and pneumonia-related death than type II, who are dominated with Fusobacterium and Neisseria.

Prevotella increases with aging but decreases in frail individuals, a pattern also observed in the gut microbiome [13]. The study exploring older adults with more than 26 teeth and no periodontal disease reported that their relative abundance and metabolome profiles resembled those of young adults with periodontal disease, yet remained distinct from periodontal-healthy young adults [56]. Supporting this, Kametani et al. [75] demonstrated that red complex species (P. gingivalis, T. forsythia, and T. denticola) play an important role in uniquely shaping the oral microbiome in a frail cohort, although periodontal disease developed only in those who harbor all of these species. In addition, a study found that a higher proportion of Prevotella in older adults was associated with oral frailty, particularly in those with subjective swallowing problems, compared to those with lower or middle levels [76]. Conversely, reduced Prevotella abundance has been linked to an increased risk of pneumonia [77].

Enterobacterales, opportunistic pathogens associated with pneumonia, has been reported in frail and oral frail older adults. These bacteria, often introduced from the respiratory tract, are known to pose a risk of aspiration pneumonia, particularly in individuals with dysphagia [78]. However, sequencing studies have not consistently shown the same overgrowth of Enterobacterales in frail adults. Instead, Haemophilus (within Pseudomonadota), a generally commensal organism in the oral cavity, showed higher abundance in these individuals, potentially reflecting oro-respiratory overlap and contributing to the development of aspiration pneumonia. Notably, Jiang et al. [79] found that caries-free older adults exhibited a more complex bacterial community, with an enriched proportion of Pseudomonadota and Haemophilus, compared to caries-active older adults.

At the cellular level, this potential dysbiosis, together with an increased abundance of oral pathogens, may contribute to the production of metabolites and virulence factors that aggravate senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) activity in senescent cells, thereby promoting systemic chronic inflammation (inflammaging) [80]. This proposed interaction is in line with the oral microbiome–SASP–aging axis and may be relevant to frailty as well as other age-related conditions [81].

Oral factors potentially contributing to oral microbiome changes

The inconsistency in alpha diversity results when comparing older adults to younger adults may be attributed to several confounding factors, particularly the number of remaining teeth. Studies that report reduced diversity in older adults often include participants with fewer teeth, resulting in fewer niches for oral bacteria, further promoting dysbiosis [82, 83]. For example, Yamamoto et al. [64] observed no significant differences in diversity between younger adults and older adults with more than 20 teeth, whereas those with fewer than 20 teeth had reduced diversity. Similarly, Nishimoto et al. [56] found no significant differences in diversity between older adults with more than 26 teeth and younger adults. Furthermore, Yang et al. [65] proposed a potential mechanistic pathway in which a reduced number of teeth is associated with decreased oral microbiome diversity, ultimately contributing to frailty.

The oral microbiome diversity in older adults with dry mouth was lower than those without dry mouth. These environmental changes impact the habitat of the oral microbiome and are reflected in the study using tongue plaque samples. Saliva plays an important role in the oral microbiome, such as providing nutrients, clearing microorganisms, and containing antimicrobial factors that manipulate the composition of microbes [84]. Many studies also support the difference between individuals with and without hyposalivation, as demonstrated in patients with xerostomia [85], hyposalivation and salivary pH [73], and Sjögren syndrome [86]. This finding supports the unstable outcomes from using saliva samples from the older adults, especially when salivary problems were not addressed in the previous studies.

Findings from studies using conventional methods show conflicting evidence regarding the impact of dentures on the oral microbiome, whereas a metagenomic study found a higher prevalence of pathogenic bacteria in individuals wearing removable partial dentures compared with dentate older adults without dentures [87]. Additionally, the number of natural teeth retained by denture wearers and the material of complete dentures have been shown to influence the oral microbiome [82, 88]. Overall, the effect of dentures on the oral microbiome remains inconclusive and needs further investigation.

Denture wearing often co-occurs with poor oral health and candidiasis, which is associated with a dysbiotic bacterial community. Candida spp. are of particular concern given the increasing spread of antifungal resistance, including C. auris, in nursing home settings [89]. Asakawa et al. [83, 90] reported an overgrowth of non-albicans Candida species in older adults, while Kraneveld et al. [91] found that microbiomes with a high Candida load were less diverse and showed distinct enrichment of Streptococcus. These findings parallel what is observed in frail, edentulous, and reduced-tooth populations in the current review. Although evidence remains mixed, the recurring association between dentures, pathogenic or opportunistic taxa, and reduced diversity underscores the importance of further research into how denture use, hygiene practices, and material type shape the oral microbiome.

Notably, the study by Sekundo et al. [73] in a centenarian cohort found that factors such as degree of disability, nursing care, diabetes, and smoking did not significantly impact the oral microbiome. However, maintaining good oral health, particularly through regular dental visits, had a positive influence on microbial diversity. These findings highlight the need for researchers to carefully select exclusion criteria when studying the oral microbiome in older adults. Compared with younger populations, older adults present more complex and variable oral and systemic conditions that can easily confound microbiome outcomes, making rigorous study design essential for answering research questions accurately.

Limitations

A key limitation of oral microbiome research is the substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies, including variability in pre-analytical and analytical procedures, which reduces reproducibility [92]. Currently, there is no gold standard or consensus protocol for oral microbiome study, which contributes to variability in study design, sequencing approaches, bioinformatic pipelines, reported outcomes, and the handling of confounding factors. Consequently, the comparison of findings from sequencing studies must be interpreted with caution [29–31].

Frailty is a time-dependent condition, and the lack of longitudinal evidence limits the ability to determine whether oral microbiome dysbiosis contributes to the development of frailty or whether frailty itself drives microbial changes. Therefore, well-designed longitudinal studies are essential to clarify the causal pathways between oral microbiome alterations and frailty progression. In addition, further investigation of other aspects of oral hypofunction should be examined to better identify oral factors associated with changes in the oral microbiome.

Implications for research

Due to inconsistent findings between studies using saliva samples, tongue samples may be more reliable for oral microbiome studies, as different tongue coatings share a similar overall microbial composition but differ in relative abundance, which can reflect the risk of certain diseases [93]. Moreover, the tongue microbiome closely mirrors the saliva microbiome in healthy individuals [94–96]. Therefore, using tongue samples may be a better choice for studying the oral microbiome of older adults, who frequently experience hyposalivation, helping to avoid this important confounding factor.

In addition, studies targeted different 16S rRNA variable regions, which substantially influence taxonomic resolution and detection accuracy. Evidence comparing the performance of these regions suggests that V1–V2 provides the more reliable coverage for the oral and nasal microbiome [97, 98], while full-length 16S sequencing can offer even broader resolution by capturing all variable regions [99]. Variation in the reference database further influences taxonomic findings. Suggested optimal taxonomic assignment depends on matching the sequencing region to an appropriate database, for example, Greengenes2 for V3–V4 or V4 regions, and HOMD for V1–V2 [100]. Overall, greater standardization of pre-analytical procedures, sequencing strategies, and bioinformatic pipelines is needed to improve reproducibility and comparability in oral microbiome research.

An additional methodological consideration is the availability of raw sequencing data from the included studies. Publicly accessible datasets would facilitate secondary analyses using uniform bioinformatic pipelines and standardized reference databases, which could reduce methodological heterogeneity and improve comparability across studies [101]. This approach may also enable pooled analyses and the application of quantitative dysbiosis indices, such as the Subgingival Microbial Dysbiosis Index (SMDI) and its extensions to saliva and tongue microbiomes, to better explore the relationship between periodontitis-associated microbiome dysbiosis and unhealthy aging [102, 103]. Future studies should therefore prioritize data sharing.

Although current evidence suggests possible associations between certain oral microbiome profiles and frailty or oral frailty, these observations remain preliminary. Given the heterogeneity of the available studies and the lack of longitudinal data, no clear microbial signature specific to or predictive of unhealthy aging can currently be identified. Higher-resolution microbiome analyses, ideally at the species or strain level, together with standardized study designs, may help clarify these associations. Furthermore, longitudinal studies are required to determine whether these microbial changes are simply correlated with frailty or play a causal role. Such evidence will be necessary before considering the development of microbial biomarkers or microbiome-targeted interventions.

Conclusions

Frailty and having fewer teeth appear to exert a stronger influence on the oral microbiome than healthy aging. While older adults may develop a microbial community distinct from that of younger adults, frailty is associated with a microbial imbalance, demonstrated by a reduction in taxa important for biofilm formation and an increase in pathogenic taxa. Similar patterns are observed in individuals with fewer teeth, though evidence linking other aspects of oral hypofunction remains limited.
