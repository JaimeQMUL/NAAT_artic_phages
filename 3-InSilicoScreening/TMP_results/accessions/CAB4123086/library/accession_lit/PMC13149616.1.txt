The reconstruction of metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) has improved our knowledge of how microbiomes perform biological and chemical processes in diverse ecosystems, including extreme environments. However, in Latin America, these ecosystems have received insufficient attention. In this study, we used shotgun metagenomics to reconstruct MAGs in Acos a high-altitude intermediate saline system in Cusco, Peru. Most of the MAGs detected were classified only at the phylum level, indicating significant phylogenetic novelty. Of particular note is the presence of two poorly characterized archaeal MAGs from the genus Methanonatronarchaeum, belonging to the phylum Halobacteriota. All reconstructed MAGs displayed a broad spectrum of metabolic pathways associated with the nitrogen and sulfur cycles, indicating metabolic versatility that allows them to cope with the harsh conditions of the saline environment. Both bacterial and archaeal MAGs are enriched in various metabolic processes related to the metabolism of amino acid and nitrogenous compounds; this could indicate a mechanism for adapting to osmotic stress. Among the genes detected, those involved in the degradation of the common herbicide atrazine were identified; this provides information on potential microbial mediation processes for the bioremediation of contaminated soils. Furthermore, and equally important, these habitats harbor a great diversity of viruses, many of which have unknown in current databases. Taxonomic classification revealed bacteriophages belonging to the class Caudoviricetes, specifically the families Myoviridae, Siphoviridae, and Podoviridae. Overall, our work provides high-quality MAGs that expand current knowledge of the diversity, function, and ecological dynamics of Bacteria, Archaea, and viruses in high-altitude intermediate saline environments.

Introduction

The advent of culture-independent methods, particularly next-generation sequencing technologies, has significantly improved our understanding of microbial diversity across environments and their roles as viral hosts [1–3]. Most of this microbial diversity comprises unknown taxa, highlighting that much more microbial diversity remains to be described. The data from these extraordinary sequencing efforts offer a promising source for biotechnological and biomedical innovation, highlighting the immense value of extremophile microbiome research [4, 5]. This value stems from the fact that these microbiomes are vast reservoirs of specialized metabolites and enzymes. These molecules have the potential to provide sustainable tools for mitigating climate change, such as novel biocatalysts for green chemistry or compounds that enhance crop resilience in arid environments [6].

In southeastern Peru, at approximately 2,852 m above sea level, the saline aquatic system known as the Acos salterns represents a high-altitude thalassohaline environment. These salt flats exhibit intermediate salinity during the austral summer (~ 19–22%), while localized hypersaline conditions develop due to evaporation and mineral precipitation [7]. The Acos system experiences strong daily temperature fluctuations typical of Andean high-altitude environments and is considered nutrient-limited, conditions that impose multiple physiological constraints on resident microorganisms. The site is publicly accessible and not designated as a protected natural area, suggesting limited direct anthropogenic disturbance. Previous studies in Acos have reported a high abundance of halophilic archaea within the class Halobacteria (phylum Methanobacteriota), particularly members of the genera Haloquadratum, Halobacterium, Halorubrum, and Haloarcula [7]. Together, the combined effects of altitude, salinity, pH, and nutrient limitation make the Acos salterns a valuable natural system for exploring microbial diversity and adaptation in high-altitude saline environments. These results are consistent with current knowledge on hypersaline microbiomes, where the archaeal class Halobacteria is the most abundant and predominant worldwide [8]. Furthermore, in a seasonal hypersaline environment located in the Chihuahuan Desert in México, the “Archean Domes”, Halobacteria lineages are among the most abundant across depths and highly prevalent over the years despite anthropogenic disturbance [9, 10]. The key metabolic strategies underlying the global prevalence of Halobacteria in saline environments are the accumulation of KCl to adapt the intracellular medium to high salt concentrations [11]. Furthermore, the biosynthesis and accumulation of osmotic solutes, such as trehalose and glycine, are important processes that allow cell viability in variable salinities [12].

On the other hand, bacteria have a cosmopolitan distribution in saline environments due to their high versatility and play key roles in local biogeochemical processes, such as organic matter degradation and nutrient cycling. Among the most prominent clades containing halophilic bacteria are the family Halomonadaceae (class Gammaproteobacteria, order Oceanospirillales) and the order Halanaerobiales (Bacillota, families Halanaerobiaceae and Halobacteroidaceae) [8, 13, 14]. Similar to halophilic archaea, halophilic bacteria either accumulate KCl intracellularly or produce/accumulate organic osmotic solutes, such as amino acids, amino acid derivatives, sugars, and sugar alcohols [14, 15]. In particular, the accumulation of osmotic solutes is one of the most widely used strategies that allows successful adaptation to a wide range of salt concentrations [16]. Furthermore, halophilic bacteria of the phyla Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota are particularly important in these environments, as they participate in processes such as nitrification, nitrogen fixation, and sulfur oxidation [17, 18].

Moreover, saline environments harbor a rich diversity of viruses, with a large proportion of viruses that have no homologs in existing databases, indicating the existence of uncharacterized viral species [19–21]. Haloviruses, viruses that have adapted to salt environments, are of special interest due to their impact on the structure and function of microbial communities, through processes such as cell lysis and horizontal gene transfer [22, 23]. Through the regulation of microbial diversity and the management of food webs, these viruses play a significant role in ecosystem dynamics [24, 25].

Finally, it is important to highlight the geographical gap that this study contributes to fulfilling, since most of our current understanding of microbiome diversity and function comes from developed countries, whereas major gaps persist in underdeveloped countries [26, 27]. In this regard, Latin America is an excellent example since there are huge gaps in the characterization of its microbial diversity, and in most cases, microbial studies are conducted with metabarcoding approaches, which strongly limit our understanding of the microbial metabolic adaptations [27]. This is unfortunate, as Latin America has unique environments that may harbor distinct microbiomes with unique metabolic adaptations, which could have biotechnological value. Hence, to fully understand global microbial diversity, increasing the number of sampling sites and employing functional approaches is mandatory.

In this study, we used shotgun metagenomics to reconstruct Metagenome Assembled Genomes (MAGs) from the intermediate saline Acos system. Our aim lies in elucidating the diversity and metabolic potential of bacteria, archaea, and viruses in this intermediate saline ecosystem. We sought the identification of new taxa and metabolic pathways, particularly those involved in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling, and potential virus-mediated interactions. We recovered 12 high-quality MAGs, most of which represent potential new species of bacteria or archaea. The archaeal and bacterial MAGs were characterized by a broad spectrum of metabolic pathways, highlighting their ecological versatility. Furthermore, we also detected a highly diverse viral community, whose sequences have no close homologs, highlighting their phylogenetic novelty.

Materials and Methods

Site Information and Sample Collection

Two environmental samples were collected from the Acos salterns, located in the district of Acos, province of Acomayo, Cusco, Peru (14°18′40″ S, 71°28′30″ W). The site is situated in a high-altitude saline system within the upper Apurímac River basin, characterized by semi-arid conditions, strong solar radiation, and pronounced daily temperature fluctuations (Fig. 1) [28]. The sampling site and procedures have been previously documented by our research team [7].

(B) Detail of the saline spring and evaporitic deposits at the Acos salterns, where environmental samples were collected. (C) Geographic location of the sampling site in the district of Acos, province of Acomayo, Cusco, Peru

DNA Extraction and Sequencing

All water samples were filtered through 0.22 μm Millipore filters to concentrate the microbial cells. DNA was extracted from the filters using ZymoBIOMICS DNA kits (MoBio, West Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and verified with a Qubit 4 fluorometer (Invitrogen). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Nextera V2.0 kit and sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform (150 bp, paired-end, 2 × 75 bases) at the Institute of Biotechnology, National Autonomous University of Mexico.

Quality Control and Taxonomic Profiling of Metagenomic Reads

The quality of raw sequencing data was assessed with FastQC (v0.11.8) [29]. Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were removed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) [30]. With the following parameters: ILLUMINACLIP: adapters.fa:2:20:10, LEADING:10, TRAILING:3, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20, MINLEN:36.

To identify the taxonomic composition of metagenomic reads from both samples, we performed taxonomic classification using Kraken 2 [31]. We classified the filtered metagenomic reads with the Kraken 2 standard nucleotide database, applying the default Kraken 2 taxonomic classification parameters.

The Kraken 2 output files were converted to BIOM format using the kraken-biom tool [32] to allow data aggregation at different taxonomic ranks. The BIOM files were exported to JSON and loaded into R to generate a phyloseq object [33]. Relative abundance values were estimated based on the proportion of reads assigned to each taxon in each sample.

The taxonomic profiles of the samples are summarized and graphically represented as stacked bar plots at the phylum and family levels to facilitate comparison of the distribution of classified reads among the samples. This provides an additional perspective on the dominant taxa in the samples and the overall variation among samples in the context of read-level taxonomic classification. The results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1A and B.

Metagenome-Assembled Genome (MAG) Binning

The high-quality reads were assembled using MetaSPAdes (v3.15.3) [34] in metagenomic mode (--meta). Assemblies were generated using multiple k-mer sizes (21, 33, 55 and 77), automatic coverage cutoff (--cov-cutoff auto), and default error-correction settings implemented in MetaSPAdes. The paired-end reads were assembled in a minimum contig length of 500 bp, by running up to 16 threads and a maximum memory of 250 GB. No further parameter optimization has been manually done.

Metagenomic binning was performed on the assembled contigs using three independent tools: MaxBin2 (v2.2.7) [35]. With default settings (minimum contig length 1000 bp, max_iteration 50, prob_threshold 0.9), MetaBat2 (v2.12.1) [36] with a minimum contig length of 2500 bp, and MetaCoag [37] (using the command `metacoag --assembler spades --graph /path/to/graph_file.gfa --contigs /path/to/contigs.fasta --paths /path/to/paths_file.paths --output /path/to/output_folder`). To improve bin quality and reduce contamination, bins were refined using Binning Refiner (v1.4.2) [38].

Genome-level taxonomic assignment and evolutionary relatedness of the recovered MAGs were assessed using the Microbial Genomes Atlas (MiGA) platform [39]. Refined bins were compared against reference microbial genomes using average nucleotide identity (ANI) and average amino acid identity (AAI) metrics, allowing classification at species and higher taxonomic ranks and evaluation of genomic novelty based on empirical similarity thresholds.

Viral Identification and Annotation

Metagenome assemblies were screened for viral contigs and auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) using VIBRANT (v1.2.1) [40]. Viral contigs were inspected for potential contaminants and analyzed with CheckV (v1.0.3) [41] to assess completeness and quality, thereby removing false positives and microbial contaminants. Functionally, viral contigs were annotated against KEGG and PFAM databases using VIBRANT’s default settings. Only AMGs flanked by phage genes and with consistent annotation in both KEGG and PFAM were retained for further analysis [42]. Taxonomic identification of non-false-positive AMGs was performed using Viptree (v1.1.3) [43].

Taxonomic Classification and Phylogenomic Analysis

Taxonomic assignment and phylogenetic placement of MAGs were conducted using the GTDB-Tk toolkit (v2.3.2) [44], which detects 53 archaeal and 120 bacterial marker genes using HMMER [45]. The domain containing the most marker genes determined the initial taxonomic classification. Domain-specific markers were aligned, concatenated, and trimmed with the appropriate bacterial or archaeal mask from the GTDB [39]. Phylogenetic trees were visualized using iTOL [46].

To refine species-level assignments and assess genome relatedness, phylogenomic analysis was also conducted using the Type (Strain) Genome Server (TYGS) under the Genome BLAST Distance Phylogeny (GBDP) approach [47]. The closest phylogenetic relatives were identified based on intergenomic distances calculated with the GBDP formula, using distance formula d5 as described by Meier-Kolthoff et al. (2013) [48], with 100 distance replicates per genome. Digital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH) was calculated using formula d4, and species-level assignments were validated based on the standard dDDH cut-off value of > 70% and G + C content differences of < 1%. Additionally, Average Amino Acid Identity (AAI) was calculated as a complementary approach, particularly for MAGs lacking a close species-level relative.

Functional Annotation and Metabolic Profiling

Functional annotation of metabolic genes relevant to ecology and biogeochemistry was performed utilizing several freely available Hidden Markov Model (HMM) databases: metabolic-hmms (https://github.com/banfieldlab/metabolic-hmms), FOAM [49], TIGRFAMS [50] and Pfam V36.0 [50]. For comparative purposes, reference genomes of both archaea and bacteria were included. Archaeal references comprised three Methanonatronarchaeum genomes: M. thermophilum AMET1 (GCF_002153915.1), Methanonatronarchaeum sp. AMET-Sl (GCF_029854155.1), and Methanonatronarchaeum sp. AMET6-2 (GCF_023016325.1). Bacterial references included Thiohalophilus thiocyanatoxydans ASM436673v1 (GCF_004366735.1) and Nitrosomonas aestuarii ASM304658v1 (GCF_003046585.1). Annotated genes were mapped to KEGG orthologs and normalized concerning the total number of coding sequences per genome. Hierarchical clustering was performed in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, 2021) [51] using the complete linkage method, implemented in the hclust function.

To assess whether the absence of mcrA in Methanonatronarchaeia MAGs could reflect a lineage-associated feature rather than solely incomplete genome recovery, we conducted a comparative phylogenetic analysis of 105 reference mcrA sequences from publicly available archaeal genomes (see Supplementary Data S1 for the list of reference mcrA sequences used in this analysis). Amino acid sequences of homologous mcrA proteins were aligned using mafft (v7.490) [52] with the --auto option. Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic inference and substitution model selection were performed using IQ-TREE (v3.0.1) [53] based on the Bayesian Information Criterion. Branch support was assessed using 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. The resulting phylogeny is shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

Results

Taxonomic Profiling of Metagenomic Reads

Both samples were dominated by the phylum Pseudomonadota, which represented about 50% of the community in A1 and 45% in A2 samples. The next most abundant phyla were Methanobacteriota (16–18%), Actinomycetota (11–15%), and Bacillota (13–14%), collectively accounting for roughly 70–75% of the total relative abundance. Additional phyla detected at moderate abundances included Bacteroidota (about 2.8–3.0%), Myxococcota (0.7–0.8%), Cyanobacteriota (0.5–0.7%), Thermodesulfobacteriota (around 0.6%), Rhodothermota (around 0.6%), Nitrososphaerota (around 0.5%), and Planctomycetota (0.3–0.4%), with most showing similar proportions between samples. Prevalent phyla with low relative abundance were Gemmatimonadota, Nitrospirota, Acidobacteriota, Deinococcota, Campylobacterota, Verrucomicrobiota, Spirochaetota, Balneolota, Chloroflexota, Synergistota, Kiritimatiellota, Chlorobiota, Thermoplasmatota, Fusobacteriota, Thermoproteota, and Thermotogota.

At the family level, both samples were dominated by Staphylococcaceae, Halomonadaceae, Streptomycetaceae, and Haloferacaceae, each accounting for approximately 7 to 13% of the total community. Additional families with relatively high abundance included Pseudomonadaceae (about 4%), Marinobacteraceae (around 2.5%), Haloarculaceae (2.5–2.7%), Natrialbaceae (around 2%), and Enterobacteriaceae (2–2.4%), which together accounted for a substantial proportion of the total reads in both samples. Numerous other families were detected at low relative abundances (~ 0.5–2%), such as Burkholderiaceae, Idiomarinaceae, Lysobacteraceae, Xanthobacteraceae, Bartonellaceae, Natronomonadaceae, Flavobacteriaceae, and Microbacteriaceae, with generally comparable values between samples.

Obtaining and Quality of the metagenome-assembled Genomes (MAGs)

Twelve MAGs were recovered with completion levels ranging from 75.7% to 91.9% and contamination levels from 0% to 4.3% in the intermediate saline-hypersaline lake at Acos (Table 1). MAG A2_3, which had the highest completion level at 91.9%, was classified within the phylum Pseudomonadota, class Gammaproteobacteria, order Pseudomonadales, and family Cellvibrionaceae, with its genus and species unclassified. Phylogenetic analysis using Microbial Genomes Atlas (MiGA) [39] identified Abyssibacter profundi and Anaeromyxobacter dehalogenans as its closest genomes; however, dDDH and AAI values were well below the species definition threshold (Table 1).

MAG A1_1 was classified at the genus level as Nitrosomonas within the family Nitrosomonadaceae. According to MiGA, its most similar genomes were Abyssibacter profundi and Anaeromyxobacter dehalogenans, but low dDDH and AAI similarity indicated these genomes were markedly different, suggesting that these two are candidates for new species. MAG A2_1, belonging to the phylum Nitrospirota, was most similar to Candidatus Nitronereus thalassa, while MAG A2_2, from the phylum Pseudomonadota and family HTCC2089, was most closely related to Mangrovibacter phragmitis. MAG 2_4, belonging to the order Burkholderiales, family Nitrosomonadaceae, was most closely related to Nitrospira multiformis. In those cases, comparative genomic metrics supported their classification as novel taxa.

Several other MAGs also showed high levels of divergence. MAG A2_7, from the phylum Desulfobacterota, was most closely related to Syntrophotalea acetylenivorans. MAG A2_9 was most closely related to Abyssalbus ytuae in the phylum Bacteroidota (class Flavobacteriia). MAGs A2_8 and A1_5 were most closely related to the genus Thiohalophilus, specifically Thiocyanatoxydans. Although remained unresolved at the species level based on genome-wide similarity metrics.

MAG A2_5 was affiliated with the species Chromohalobacter canadensis and showed the highest level of genomic similarity among all the MAGs, the dDDH values were still below the species threshold.

The archaeal MAGs A1_10 and A2_13 were classified within the lineage Methanonatronarchaeia of the phylum Halobacteriota (see Fig. 2). MiGA identified Actinarchaeum halophilum and Methanocrinis alkalitolerans as the most similar reference genomes; however, the AAI and dDDH values were sufficient to classify them as novel.

Metabolic Potential of Archaeal and Bacterial MAGs

Archaeal MAGs

Functional annotation of archaeal MAG A1_10 and A2_13 revealed a broad spectrum of metabolic pathways, emphasizing their ecological versatility (Fig. 3). The most abundant metabolic pathway was the purine pathway (5.98% of all predicted metabolic pathways), indicating active nucleic acid synthesis and energy metabolism. Methane metabolism (4.41%) was also prominent, suggesting the potential for methanogenesis or similar processes important in anaerobic environments. Sulfur metabolism (4.28%) and carbon fixation (3.52%) were also well represented, indicating the ability to utilize diverse energy and nutrient sources.

Fig. 3 Kegg profile for Archaeal MAGs. Heat map of the proportion of predicted KEGG orthologs (KO). The heatmap shows the functional annotation for Archaeal MAG. The color bar represents the relative abundance of KEGG orthologs (KOs) within each genome, measured as a percentage of the total number of coding sequences. The scale ranges from 0 to 10%, indicating the proportion of KOs relative to the genome’s coding sequences

Other notable metabolic pathways included pyruvate metabolism (3.08%), glycine/serine/threonine metabolism (2.71%), and nitrogen metabolism (2.64%), which together contribute to robust energy production and essential nutrients cycling. In contrast, metabolic pathways such as lipoarabinomannan biosynthesis, caffeine metabolism, photosynthesis, and mucin-type O-glycan biosynthesis were only minimally represented.

Such functional patterns are further supported by the comprehensive gene lists in the genomes of these archaea, as provided in the supplementary dataset (Table S3). Both archaeal genomes appear to contain a complete set of functional genes involved in information processing (DNA replication, transcription, and RNA processing), as well as several enzymes involved in carbon metabolism. Additionally, the co-occurrence of genes related to methane metabolism, sulfur transformations, and carbon fixation in these genomes indicates a degree of metabolic flexibility in the lifestyles of these archaea, suggesting that these microorganisms can utilize different metabolic pathways to generate energy depending on the environmental conditions they encounter.

Bacterial MAGs

Bacterial MAGs exhibited a diverse metabolic repertoire, reflecting their adaptation to intermediate saline conditions. ABC transporters were highly represented in MAG A2_5 (4.63%) and A2_3 (2.95%), suggesting a strong capacity for nutrient uptake and environmental sensing. Alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism were represented in the different MAGs, with A2_5 again showing the highest abundance (2.95%).

Energy metabolism was also robust, with the citrate cycle (TCA) being most abundant in A2_7 (3.71%), indicating an aerobic respiratory potential. Degradation of fatty acids was notable in A2_3 (2.88%), suggesting the utilization of lipids as energy sources. Carbon fixation pathways were particularly strong in A2_7 (3.52%), indicating autotrophic capabilities.

MAGs also showed potential for biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, including production of siderophores and type II polyketide products as well as biodegradation pathways, such as benzoate degradation (1.23% in A2_2) and atrazine degradation (0.34% in A2_5), indicating a possible role in bioremediation and adaptation to local pollutants (Fig. 4 and supplementary file S2).

These patterns are further supported by the complete gene inventories provided in the supplementary material (Tables S3), where bacterial MAGs encode a wide array of genes responsible for transport, environmental sensing, and metabolic versatility. Notably, the high abundance of genes associated with ABC transporters, two-component regulatory systems, and quorum sensing underscores an elevated capacity for nutrient uptake and rapid physiological responses to changing environmental conditions. The presence of complete gene sets for central carbon metabolism (glycolysis, TCA cycle, and fatty acid degradation), along with pathways for secondary metabolite biosynthesis and xenobiotic degradation, highlights the ecological plasticity of these bacterial lineages. This functional breadth indicates that bacterial MAGs play a central role in organic matter turnover, nutrient cycling, and potential bioremediation processes within intermediate saline environments.

Functional Insights: Key Genes and Metabolic Pathways

Purine and Sulfur Metabolism

Metagenomic analysis revealed the presence of numerous key genes involved in purine biosynthesis, rescue, and degradation, such as inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (guaB), guanylate kinase (guaC), ribonucleotide reductase (nrdA, nrdE), and hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (hprt). Genes for de novo synthesis and nucleotide salvage pathways were widespread, indicating microbial strategies for survival in nutrient-poor environments. Several sulfur metabolism genes (cysC, sat/met3) were also identified, suggesting a tightly linked regulation of the purine and sulfur cycles.

Methanogenesis and Alternative Metabolic Pathways in Archaea

Despite the detection of many key enzymes for methanogenesis, such as formylmethanofuran dehydrogenase and coenzyme F420 hydrogenase, the absence of the alpha subunit of methyl-coenzyme M reductase (McrA, K00399) is striking, as this enzyme is essential for this metabolic pathway. This absence is significant, as McrA catalyzes the final step of methanogenesis in methanogenic archaea [54]. The absence of McrA suggests that these archaea utilize alternative strategies for carbon and sulfur metabolism or represent non-methanogenic lineages. This could be due to incomplete genome restoration events or that these microorganisms have undergone a metabolic adaptation (see Discussion) (Fig. 5 and Figure S2).

Other detected enzymes, including formate dehydrogenase, pyruvate oxidoreductase, and enzymes involved in the TCA cycle, confirm an active energy metabolism not strictly linked to methane production. Genes involved in serine biosynthesis and formaldehyde fixation underscore the metabolic versatility of these archaea.

Nitrogen Metabolism and Glutamate Synthesis

Numerous genes supporting the nitrogen cycle were present, including glutamate dehydrogenases (gdhA), glutamate synthases (gltB, gltD, gltS), assimilatory nitrate reductase (nasB), nitrite reductase (nirB, nirD), nitrate reductase (narG, narZ, nxrA), and nitrous oxide reductase (nosZ). Genes for the reduction of nitric oxide and hydroxylamine were also found, indicating the capacity for multiple nitrogen transformations and a complex nitrogen cycle in the community (Fig. 5).

Viral Diversity, Taxonomic classification, and Functional Potential

Metagenomic analysis revealed a remarkable diversity of viral contigs from the Acos intermediate saline system, with many viral sequences having no close homologs in public databases, emphasizing the novelty of the local biome.

The taxonomic classification of the viral genomes was performed using VIBRANT and Viptree. This resulted in the identification of a broad range of viral groups predominantly associated with tailed double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) bacteriophages from the class Caudoviricetes, and the families according to their morphology Myoviridae, Siphoviridae, and Podoviridae (Fig. 6). Several viral contigs clustered within unclassified or novel viral lineages, further supporting the presence of unique viruses in these intermediate salinity environments.

The tree displays the taxonomic relationships between viral sequences identified in this study and reference phages. Novel and unclassified clades are highlighted, underlining the presence of previously undescribed viral lineages in the intermediate-salinity system

Phylogenomic analysis of the viral sequences (Fig. 6, proteome dendrogram) illustrates the relatedness of the identified viral contigs to known reference phages and highlights the clustering of multiple contigs in novel clades, suggesting the presence of previously undescribed haloviruses.

Functional annotation of the viral genomes revealed a wide range of genes involved in host infection, virion assembly, DNA replication, packaging, and immune defense. In particular, genes encoding structural proteins (e.g., the large head subunit gpT, capsid proteins, chaperones for tail assembly), lytic enzymes (peptidase S49, protease for head maturation), and regulatory elements (DNA methyltransferases, transcriptional regulators of the XRE family) were strongly represented (Fig. 7).

Fig. 7 Functional annotation of complete viral prophages. Summary of the major functional categories and auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) identified in viral genomes. The annotation highlights genes involved in virion assembly, infection, lysis, DNA modification, and putative novel functions

Notably, the functional inferences from the viral genomic data align with the distribution and functional annotation of the identified auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) in various contigs (Fig. 7). The AMGs identified in this study were not limited to single viral lineages but were distributed across various phylogenomic groups, suggesting that the acquisition of metabolic auxiliaries may not be an isolated event in the Acos viral community. The identification of AMGs related to nitrogen, sulfur, or carbon metabolism in phylogenetically distinct viruses further supports the hypothesis of community-level metabolic modulation by viruses in the intermediate saline habitat.

Furthermore, about one-third of viral genes matched hypothetical or unknown proteins, highlighting the unexploited functional diversity within the Acos viral community. Beyond this unknown fraction, several viral contigs encoded auxiliary metabolic genes associated with nitrogen and sulfur metabolism, carbon processing, and cellular stress responses. Such AMGs are often considered tools by which viruses reprogram host metabolism during infection, maintaining metabolic activity and redox balance under environmentally stressful conditions, such as elevated salinity. Additionally, the detection of AMGs related to the metabolism of aromatic compounds, along with bacterial pathways for xenobiotic degradation in the same environment, suggests that viruses may indirectly influence host-driven biogeochemical and bioremediation processes, including atrazine degradation. The detection of viral AMGs and xenobiotic degradation pathways in the same environment does not support a model of direct viral degradation but indicates the involvement of viruses in ecosystem-level functions by reshaping host metabolic networks during infection.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into the microbial and viral communities of an intermediate saline system in Acos, Cusco, Peru, highlighting both the taxonomic novelty and metabolic versatility of organisms thriving under extreme conditions [7, 28]. Our work brings high-quality MAGs that extend current knowledge on the diversity, function, and ecological dynamics of Bacteria, Archaea, and viruses in intermediate salinity environments.

The structure of the microbial communities in both samples reflects the findings in the MAGs, with the phylum Pseudomonadota predominating in both, representing about 50% of the community in sample A1 and 45% in sample A2. The next most abundant phyla were Methanobacteriota (16–18%), Actinomycetota (11–15%), and Bacillota (13–14%), collectively accounting for approximately 70–75% of the total relative abundance. Additional phyla detected at moderate abundances included Bacteroidota (about 2.8–3.0%), Myxococcota (0.7–0.8%), Cyanobacteriota (0.5–0.7%), Thermodesulfobacteriota (around 0.6%), Rhodothermota (around 0.6%), Nitrososphaerota (around 0.5%), and Planctomycetota (0.3–0.4%), with most showing similar proportions between samples. Phyla with low relative abundance included Gemmatimonadota, Nitrospirota, Acidobacteriota, Deinococcota, Campylobacterota, Verrucomicrobiota, Spirochaetota, Balneolota, Chloroflexota, Synergistota, Kiritimatiellota, Chlorobiota, Thermoplasmatota, Fusobacteriota, Thermoproteota, and Thermotogota (Figure S1).

At the family level, both samples were dominated by Staphylococcaceae, Halomonadaceae, Streptomycetaceae, and Haloferacaceae, each accounting for approximately 7–13% of the total community. Other families with relatively high abundance included Pseudomonadaceae (about 4%), Marinobacteraceae (around 2.5%), Haloarculaceae (2.5–2.7%), Natrialbaceae (around 2%), and Enterobacteriaceae (2–2.4%), which together accounted for a substantial proportion of the total reads in both samples. Numerous other families were detected at low relative abundances (about 0.5–2%), such as Burkholderiaceae, Idiomarinaceae, Lysobacteraceae, Xanthobacteraceae, Bartonellaceae, Natronomonadaceae, Flavobacteriaceae, and Microbacteriaceae, with generally comparable values between samples (Figure S2).

The bacterial MAGs were distributed across seven taxa, most of which were at the phylum level, including Pseudomonadota, Nitrospirota, Bacteroidota, and Desulfobacterota. One at the family level, Cellvibionaceae, one at the genus level, Nitrosomonas, and one at the species level, Chromohalobacter canadensis. These results align with those from other hypersaline environments in Latin America, such as the Archean domes and a microbial oasis in the Chihuahuan Desert [9, 10]. Also, the taxonomic profile is highly consistent with other hypersaline environments, such as hypersaline lakes and microbial mats in Shark Bay [55, 56]. In turn, the two Archaeal MAGs detected in this study belong to the clade Methanonatronarchaeia, within the phylum Halobacteriota, which are prevalent members in hypersaline environments worldwide [8]. The detection of Methanonatronarchaeia MAGs is particularly noteworthy since this group of archaea is still poorly represented in genome databases, with only a handful of complete genomes available [57, 58]. A notable result of this study is that most MAGs were only identified at the phylum level, suggesting the presence of basal phylogenetic novelty in the Acos system. Such a result is in line with Rodriguez-Cruz et al., 2024 [9], who found extensive new basal lineages of Archaea and Bacteria in a hypersaline environment in the Chihuahuan Desert. Overall, these results highlight the unique microbial diversity present in Latin American environments and encourage the establishment of a more in-depth research program to profile Latin American microbial biodiversity across all its biomes.

Metabolic profiling of the archaeal MAGs revealed a broad range of functional capabilities, highlighting purine, methane, sulfur, and nitrogen metabolism, strengthening the hypothesis that these archaea have adapted to utilize a wide range of energy and nutrient sources. Interestingly, despite the presence of a key for methanogenesis, the absence of the McrA subunit suggests alternative metabolic pathways for carbon and sulfur, or possibly incomplete methanogenesis. This is consistent with recent findings indicating metabolic plasticity and non-canonical energetic pathways in extremely halophilic archaea [57, 59, 60]. Such metabolic versatility could provide a competitive advantage in fluctuating hypersaline or resource-limited environments. Interestingly, archaeal MAGs have the metabolic potential to display a wide range of nitrogen-related processes, such as purine and nitrogen metabolism, as well as processes related to glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism. These results could indicate the presence of metabolic strategies to produce and accumulate compatible solutes to cope with osmotic stress, which is a common selective pressure in hypersaline or intermediate saline environments [8, 14, 61].

Bacterial MAGs exhibited similarly diverse metabolic profiles, characterized by an abundance of transporters, robust TCA cycling, fatty acid degradation, carbon fixation, and pathways for the biodegradation of aromatic compounds and pollutants. These results suggest a functionally rich community involved in important ecosystem services such as organic matter turnover, nutrient cycling, and even bioremediation [53, 54]. Our results show the potential for atrazine degradation. Atrazine is a common herbicide used for weed control in farmlands. Its persistent use leads to soil contamination [62, 63]. Current knowledge indicates that the most common Bacteria capable of fully degrading atrazine to ammonia are Arthrobacter sp. FM326, Pseudomonas sp. AACB, and Bacillus subtilis [64]. The discovery of these new MAGs expands the range of bacterial taxa that could be performing this degradation function. Further experimental studies are needed to properly evaluate the metabolic capacity of these Bacteria to degrade atrazine. However, our results highlight the value of microbiological surveys in extreme environments in Latin America.

Our study also reveals a remarkable diversity of viruses, many of which are highly divergent from the known reference genomes. Most of the viral contigs found belong to the tailed dsDNA bacteriophages (Caudovirales: Myoviridae, Siphoviridae, Podoviridae), but numerous sequences belong to unclassified or putative novel viral clades. This observation is consistent with previous reports from hypersaline systems, which are considered hotspots for viral novelty [19–21].

The functional annotation of viral genes revealed the presence of structural proteins, lysis enzymes, DNA modification systems (including restriction modification evasion), and integrases, suggesting that horizontal gene transfer and lysogenesis play an important role in shaping microbial community dynamics. One-third of viral genes encode hypothetical proteins, indicating considerable “viral dark matter” and emphasizing the need for further functional characterization [65–67].

The identified auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) suggest viral involvement in nutrient cycling, metabolic flux, and possibly modulation of host metabolism and adaptation. This emphasizes the central role of viruses as ecosystem engineers in extreme environments, influencing host fitness, community structure, and biogeochemical processes [68–70].

The coexistence of metabolically versatile Bacteria and Archaea, along with a diverse and dynamic virome, likely contributes to strengthening ecosystem resilience and preserving functional redundancy as adaptive mechanisms in the face of environmental stress. The results presented here support the idea that intermediate salinity systems such as Acos are valuable model ecosystems for studying adaptation, horizontal gene transfer, and the emergence of metabolic innovations [71, 72].

The lack of complete methanogenic metabolic pathways in the dominant archaea, combined with the enrichment of genes for alternative C1 and sulfur metabolism, may indicate a shift in community metabolic strategies driven by environmental constraints. Such shifts could have important implications for the methane cycle and greenhouse gas dynamics in saline inland waters [59].

Viruses are increasingly viewed as active regulators of microbial metabolism rather than merely passive agents of mortality. The detection of viral AMGs associated with nitrogen, sulfur, and carbon metabolism in the Acos system suggests that viruses may reprogram host metabolism to optimize replication under resource-limited and high-salinity conditions. This viral-mediated metabolic reprogramming could affect major biogeochemical processes identified in this study, such as nitrogen transformation and sulfur cycling.

Additionally, the presence of viral AMGs alongside bacterial atrazine degradation genes indicates a possible indirect influence of viruses on bioremediation potential by modifying host metabolic efficiency or gene expression. Although direct functional validation is needed, similar virus-mediated impacts on host metabolism have been reported in other extreme and saline ecosystems.

Limitations and Future Directions

While this study provides an overview of the Acos microbial ecosystem, it still has some limitations. The incompleteness of some MAGs, particularly for genomes lacking genes with essential functions such as McrA, suggests the need for greater sequencing depth and improved assembly methods, which would enable the revelation of additional metabolic capabilities. Furthermore, many viral sequences remain unclassified, reflecting the need to expand reference databases and functional assays. Future studies should integrate metatranscriptomic and metaproteomic approaches to directly assess microbial and viral activity, link MAGs to environmental parameters, and decipher the ecological consequences of the observed functional diversity. In addition, experimental validation of key metabolic pathways, particularly those involved in methane and sulfur cycling and atrazine degradation, would considerably improve our understanding of ecosystem functioning in hypersaline and intermediate-saline habitats.

Conclusions

Our results show that the microbial and viral communities in the medium salinity Acos system have a highly diverse metabolic repertoire. Our results highlight the phylogenetic novelty of microbes inhabiting the Acos system, which harbor promising strategies for bioremediation of soils contaminated with atrazine. The reconstructed genomes reveal distinct pathways for carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling, suggesting flexible strategies for energy production and nutrient utilization under extreme conditions. The absence of canonical methanogenesis genes indicates novel metabolic adaptations of the Archaea. These results emphasize the central role of microorganisms in metabolism.
