Highlights

• Administration of the bacteriophage cocktail at a high MOI reduced APEC-induced morbidity and mortality by 20-30% in laying hens, validating its potential as an antimicrobial alternative.

• The oral route (drinking water) demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy compared to intramuscular injection, resulting in the complete absence of macroscopic lesions and a noticeable reduction in median histopathological scores, particularly in the spleen.

• The administered phage cocktail achieved effective lytic activity against APEC and was recovered across multiple tissues without causing collateral damage or altering the general bacterial structure of the cecal microbiota.

Avian colibacillosis, caused by avian pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC), results in high morbidity and mortality and reduces egg production in domestic poultry. Increasing antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and restriction on antibiotic use necessitate alternative disease control strategies. We evaluated the efficacy of a phage cocktail (Tequatrovirus, Sashavirus, and an unclassified Caudoviricetes) at a multiplicity of infection > 100, administered for 4 days in drinking water (DW) or as a single intramuscular (IM) treatment, in preventing experimental APEC infection (108 CFU given intravenously) in specific–pathogen–free laying hens (25–27 weeks old, n=10 per experimental group). Phage treatment reduced mortality from 20% to 0 and morbidity by 30%. Birds receiving phages in DW showed a marginal trend toward reduced weight loss (p = 0.063) than those in IM or APEC–only groups at 4 days post–infection. Macroscopic lesions were present in 6.7–11.7% of organs in APEC–only and IM groups but were absent in DW–treated hens. In the DW group, median histopathological scores were lower, with the most notable reduction (p = 0.006) in the spleen. Across organs, concentrations of recovered phages were similar between DW and IM groups (p = 0.227); however, in the DW group, 1.3−2.1 log10 PFU/g of phages were detected in all tissues except blood. Based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing, neither IM nor DW phage treatments altered the overall general bacterial structure or population in the cecum. We conclude that this phage cocktail, administered via DW, lysed APEC without disrupting gut microbiota, improved overall bird health and welfare, and protected internal organs from APEC invasion.

1 INTRODUCTION

Avian pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC) is the primary cause of colibacillosis, with deleterious effects on poultry production globally, including broilers, turkeys, laying hens, and ducks (Kathayat et al., 2021). APEC is a subgroup of extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli that causes systemic disease, manifesting as airsacculitis, pericarditis, salpingitis, coligranuloma, egg peritonitis, perihepatitis, cellulitis and arthritis; it is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in poultry, with high mortality (up to 53.5%) in young chickens (Mellata, 2013). It also decreases meat and egg production (by up to 20%) and increases carcass condemnation rates (Kathayat et al., 2021). This disease primarily affects laying hens (commonly termed E. coli peritonitis syndrome; EPS). Predominant disease–causing APEC serogroups include O2, O78 and O117, with O78 being the most prevalent (Jamali et al., 2024). Predisposing factors include stress, immunosuppression, and concurrent Mycoplasma, or viral infections (Collingwood et al., 2014).

Colibacillosis in poultry is mostly controlled using antibiotics (Landoni and Albarellos, 2015), both prophylactically and metaphylactically. However, 95–100% resistance has been reported in APEC isolates to a number of medically important Category I and II antibiotics, and > 46% resistance to Category III antibiotics (Jamali et al., 2024). Furthermore, due to increasing antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in zoonotic pathogens (such as E. coli) and the risk of transmission to humans, Chicken Farmers of Canada (CFC) has phased out preventive use of Category I and II, and III antibiotics (CFC, 2020).

Compounding this challenge, regulatory changes in Canada now mandate spacious, welfare–enhancing hen housing systems (CFC, 2020); this improves animal welfare (Hartcher and Jones, 2017), but may increase susceptibility to pathogens, including APEC (Fulton, 2017). Hens infected with APEC often die, despite antibiotic treatment. Vaccines provide only limited protection against serologically unrelated pathogens (Ghunaim et al., 2014). Probiotics are promising, but generally require several days to establish their protective effects (Christensen et al., 2021). Consequently, there is an urgent need for effective alternative control strategies.

Bacteriophages (phages) are viruses with a highly specific tropism, infecting only particular bacterial species or strains, with minimal or no disruption to commensal and beneficial bacteria (Salmond and Fineran, 2015), which are pivotal for maintaining health and preventing disease in animals and humans (Shi et al., 2022a). Phages are approved for decontaminating foodborne pathogens such as E. coli O157 on meat or fresh produce in Canada, USA, Europe, and Australia (Bumunang et al., 2023; Moye et al., 2018) and for veterinary use in the European Union (Yang et al., 2023).

To address the growing AMR crisis, virulent or lytic phages are a proposed alternative for treating antimicrobial–resistant bacterial infections in humans, livestock, and aquaculture. Anti–APEC phages have been identified and characterized by our group and others, demonstrating strong lytic activity in broth culture (Karami et al., 2024; Kazibwe et al., 2020; Koratkar et al., 2024; Sattar et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2023; Wintachai et al., 2024). Their therapeutic potential has been further confirmed in vivo in young chicks and broilers (Huff et al., 2013, 2005; Jhandai et al., 2024).

However, a critical knowledge gap remains regarding the application of these phages in layers. Differences in physiological status, genetics, and immune function mean that APEC pathogenesis and disease manifestation in adult layers can differ substantially from those observed in young chicks and broilers (Abdelhamid et al., 2024; Alber et al., 2021; Ask et al., 2006; Landman et al., 2013). Consequently, a comprehensive assessment of the therapeutic and biocontrol outcomes of phage therapy is specifically needed in laying hens, which represents a vital and distinct segment of commercial poultry production. Encouragingly, a prior study demonstrated positive results in 10–week–old pullets (young female layers) challenged with APEC O78, a single dose of phage cocktail (containing a Myovirus and a Siphovirus) administered orally or by spray reduced morbidity and mortality by 43 and 25%, respectively (Oliveira et al., 2010).

In addition to the need for data in adult layers, the optimal delivery route for phage therapy against APEC remains unclear. While methods like intramuscular and intratracheal injection often yield more consistent efficacy than oral administration (Huff et al., 2013, 2005; Kittler et al., 2020, Tsonos et al., 2014), they are impractical for large–scale poultry operations. Environmental spray is feasible for surface decontamination; however, this route has its own drawbacks: it can cause interruption of the flock activity during application, and a very large phage load may be required to ensure phages reach the intended site of infection. Administration through drinking water (DW) is, by far, the most practical and scalable method for mitigating bacterial infection in large poultry flocks. However, technical challenges remain concerning the bioavailability and stability of phages under the harsh, acidic conditions of the avian gastrointestinal tract (Dąbrowska, 2019; Ryan et al., 2011) necessitating further investigation into DW delivery efficacy in this context.

Previous studies on phages in poultry have focused on clinical outcomes such as morbidity and mortality. A detailed histopathological evaluation, however, is essential to detect early–stage pathological changes in internal organs, providing a more sensitive and objective measure of therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, the broader ecological impacts of APEC phage administration on the gut microbiota are largely unexplored, although such effects have been documented for Salmonella phages (Huang et al., 2022; Kosznik–Kwaśnicka et al., 2022). Understanding these factors is essential for accurately evaluating anti–APEC efficacy of phages and refining their preventative use for practical application. To address these critical gaps, we aimed to systematically evaluate phage delivery in DW, combining clinical assessment with in–depth histological and microbiome analyses.

Previously, we characterized the host range and lytic activity of vB_EcoM_ASO78A (Tequatrovirus), vB_EcoS_AVIO78A (unclassified Caudoviricetes), and vB_EcoS_ASO78B (Sashavirus; Smith et al., 2023). ASO78A had a broad host range, lysing multiple strains of APEC O1, O2 and O78. In contrast, ASO78B and AVIO78A were highly virulent against O78 strains but did not lyse serogroups O1 and O2 strains tested. Furthermore, preliminary comparative assays indicated that a three-phage cocktail demonstrated enhanced anti-APEC activity against clinical O78 strains compared to individual phages or two-phage combinations in broth culture (Girum et al., 2021). These findings prompt us to assess the cocktail's potential for controlling bacterial infection in vivo. We hypothesized that delivering phage cocktail via DW would effectively lyses APEC O78 in vivo, preventing laying hens from developing colibacillosis and sustaining organ damage, while not compromising the cecal microbiome. Our objectives were to: 1) evaluate the efficacy of the phage cocktail in preventing APEC infections in adult layers, including clinical endpoints, macroscopic and microscopic organ lesions and bacterial recovery; 2) compare preventive outcomes of intramuscular (IM) versus oral (DW) administration of phages; 3) determine the survival of phages in the circulatory system and internal organs; and 4) characterize the effects of phage administration on the cecal microbiome.

2 Materials and methods

2.1 Bacterial strain and media

The APEC strain ECL24324 (O78; resistant to ampicillin and gentamicin and containing five virulence genes, i.e., iroN, ompT, hlyF, iss, iutA) isolated from pericarditis lesions in a 34–week–old laying hen was obtained from the Escherichia coli Reference Laboratory, Université de Montréal, QC, Canada and used in this study. Overnight cultures of APEC strain ECL24324 were prepared by inoculating a single colony into 10 mL of tryptic soy broth (TSB) (Sigma, Oakville, ON, Canada), followed by incubation for 18–20 h at 37°C. To enhance pathogenicity, APEC strain ECL24324 was inoculated into 25–27–week–old White Leghorn specific pathogen free (SPF) laying hens (average weight = 1240 ± 138 g; purchased from the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) and re–isolated from the bone marrow (BM) of a diseased hen. This BM isolate was used for both the infection trial and phage efficacy studies.

2.2 Preparation of bacterial inoculum

An overnight culture of the BM isolate was inoculated into TSB and then incubated at 37°C at 180 revolutions per minute (rpm) for 1.5–2 h until a mid–log culture was obtained (OD600 = 0.5−0.6, ∼108 Colony forming unit (CFU/mL). The culture was then centrifuged at 4700 × g for 5 minutes at 4 °C, and the pellet was resuspended in pre–chilled fresh TSB after being washed once with TSB to remove residual growth medium. Desired bacterial concentrations (109, 108, 107.5 and 107 CFU/mL) were prepared in cold TSB. Bacterial concentrations were confirmed by plating appropriate dilutions, prepared in sterile phosphate buffer saline (PBS, pH = 7.4 ± 0.1), onto MacConkey agar supplemented with 10 µg/mL gentamicin (Dalynn Biologicals, Calgary, AB, Canada), followed by overnight (20–24 h) incubation. Prepared bacterial resuspensions were transported on ice to the Veterinary Science Research Services (VSRS), Spy Hill campus, University of Calgary, for inoculation.

2.3 Bacteriophage propagation and titration

The three bacteriophages (ASO78A, ASO78B, and AVIO78A) used in this study were initially isolated from sewage and fecal samples from commercial egg layer farms (Smith et al., 2023). Phages were propagated and enumerated as described previously using the host strain APEC O78 ECL24324.

2.4 Time killing assay

To identify the optimal phage formulation and multiplicity of infection (MOI) for subsequent in vivo efficacy studies, time–kill assays were conducted. Mid-log–phase APEC O78 cultures (∼108 CFU/mL) were exposed to single phages (ASO78A, ASO78B, and AVIO78A), 2-phage combinations (ASO78A+ASO78B, ASO78A+AVIO78A, and ASO78B+AVIO78A) and 3- phage cocktail (ASO78A+ASO78B+AVIO78A) at MOIs (100 to 0.00001) and incubated at 37°C. APEC growth was monitored by measuring optical density at 600 nm (OD600nm) at 1-h interval for 24 h using a Synergy Neo2 Multimode Microplate Reader(Agilent Technologies, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). Each treatment was tested using three biological replicates, with each biological replicate measured in technical duplicate. Growth curves were plotted to visualize phage-mediated suppression and growth dynamics. Percent inhibition across 24 h was calculated relative to the untreated bacterial control. In addition, overall growth inhibition was quantified by calculating the area under the growth curve (AUC) for each replicate using the trapezoidal method (Spritzler et al., 2008). Lower AUC values indicate stronger and more sustained bacterial suppression over the 24 h experimental period.

2.5 Phage preparations for administration in laying hens

To purify and concentrate phages for IM injection, 30 mL of phage research laboratory stock (PRLS, ∼1010 plaque forming units (PFU)/mL) was transferred into 30 kDa centrifugal filter units (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) and centrifuged at 4000 × g at 20°C for 50 minutes. Concentrated phages were transferred to 15–mL Falcon tubes and resuspended in lambda diluent (10 mM Tris–Cl, 8 mM MgSO4; pH 7.5) at 5 ×1011 PFU/mL. Phages were titrated using the soft–agar overlay technique (Smith et al., 2023). When the target MOI for each phage was 100, phage ASO78A failed to propagate to a titer ≥ 1010 PFU/mL and ultrafiltration was unsuccessful. Phage ASO78A (7.05 × 109 PFU/mL) was therefore added as a filtered phage stock, whereas ASO78B (1.14 × 1012 PFU/mL) and AVIO78A (2.2 × 1011 PFU/mL) were concentrated as described above, suspended in lambda diluent, and mixed (1 mL of each) to obtain the phage cocktail.

For DW delivery, the 3 filtered phage stocks were mixed in equal PFU ratios to create a cocktail concentrate. This mixture was dispensed into 4 separate sterile aliquots (one per day of treatment) and stored at 4°C. Immediately prior to administration, the daily aliquots were diluted into 3 L of regular tap water to achieve a final concentration of 3 × 108 PFU/mL. Based on an estimated water intake of ∼100 mL per hen per day, this provided a dose of 1010 PFU per phage (total 3 ×1010 PFU) resulting in a target MOI of 100 for each individual phage relative to the challenge dose. Water was withdrawn for 2 h prior to phage administration to encourage rapid uptake. Water consumption was recorded every 24 h, and phage viability in the water reservoirs was confirmed daily by plaque assay to verify stability.

2.6. Experimental design

The SPF laying hens (25–27–weeks–old White Leghorn, average weight = 1,225 ± 141 g for the infection trial and 1,255 ± 133 g for the phage efficacy study) were purchased from the CFIA and kept at the VSRS, Spy Hill campus, University of Calgary. They were randomly allocated into experimental groups, with each group housed in separate floor pens to prevent cross–contamination. Hens had ad libitum access to water and feed and were maintained under controlled conditions, including a 12 h:12 h light–dark cycle, room temperature of 25°C ± 3, and 50% relative humidity. Upon arrival, hens were given an acclimation period of 4 or 7 days (DW group and all other groups, respectively) before being handled for experiments. The study was reviewed and approved by the Veterinary Science Animal Care Committee (VSACC), Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, University of Calgary, AB, Canada (Protocol AC20–0008). The experiment consisted of two trials: establishment of an infection trial and a phage efficacy study.

2.6.1 Study 1: Establishment of infection model

To establish an infection model, three trials were conducted using different concentrations of APEC and varying observation intervals. In each trial, SPF laying hens were randomly assigned to an experimental infection group (n = 5 per disease group) and a healthy control group (n = 3 per trial). Experimental groups were intravenously (IV) inoculated with 1 mL of a mid–log phase culture resuspension of the APEC bone marrow strain at doses of 10⁷ (low dose; LD), 10⁷․⁵ (Intermediate dose; ID), 10⁸ (high dose; HD), or 10⁹ CFU/mL (very high dose; VHD), respectively. In contrast, the healthy control group was injected IV with sterile TSB (1 mL). On 7 day post infection (dpi) or at a humane endpoint (cumulative clinical score of 3 [Table S1] or ≥ 20% body weight loss), hens were euthanized by isoflurane overdose, followed by cervical dislocation (Ask et al., 2006).

2.6.2 Study 2: Phage efficacy study

To evaluate the prophylactic efficacy of the phage cocktail, SPF laying hens (n = 35) were randomly allocated into four groups: one healthy control group (n = 5) and three experimental groups (n = 10 each). The healthy control group received sterile TSB (1 mL, IV) and lambda diluent (200 µL, IM). Both phage–treated groups were challenged with APEC (1 mL, 10⁸ CFU/mL, IV) followed by phage treatment. The DW phage group received the phage cocktail in DW on four consecutive occasions prior to APEC challenge (3 × 10⁸ PFU/mL, MOI ≥ 100; 3 L per 10 hens). The IM phage group received a phage cocktail (4.56 × 1011 PFU/mL, µL per hen) at an MOI of 100, administered 2 h prior to APEC challenge via the IM route (Fig. 1). In addition, the APEC–only group received lambda diluent (200 µL, IM) (Atterbury et al., 2007; Huff et al., 2004). Hens were euthanized at 4 dpi or at a humane endpoint, as described above (Ask et al., 2006; Mellata et al., 2003).Fig. 1 Experimental design for healthy control, APEC-only, and phage-treated groups in specific-pathogen-free (SPF) laying hens challenged with an APEC bone marrow strain.

2.6.3 Efficacy parameters

Clinical signs: Animal welfare was monitored by observing clinical signs (i.e., depression, breathing problems [open–mouth breathing or difficulty in breathing], changes in comb color, body weight loss, and reduced movement). Severity of each sign was scored on a scale of 0–3, where 0 indicates no signs and 3 indicates severe signs. Hens scoring 3 for any category were identified with an asterisk (*) to be euthanized at a humane endpoint. Clinical signs with corresponding scores are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Body weight and mortality: Hen body weight was measured at 2–day intervals to monitor weight loss following APEC infection and/or phage administration. Mortality was recorded to determine severity of APEC infection and efficacy of phage therapy in preventing death.

Relative organ weight: After euthanasia, the liver, lungs, heart, spleen, ovary, and oviduct were collected and weighed to determine relative organ weight fraction (organ weight/hen weight) to identify any change in relative organ weight due to APEC infection.

Macroscopic and microscopic lesions: Pathological lesions in these organs were observed visually and noted (0: normal, 1: mild, 2: moderate, 3: severe; Table S2). For histopathological examination, thin slices (5–10 mm) of each organ were collected carefully in tissue cassettes, fixed in 10% buffered formalin (ThermoFisher Scientific, Alberta, Canada) and submitted to the Diagnostic Service Unit (DSU), University of Calgary to obtain 3–5 µm paraffin–embedded tissue slides stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E). Microscopic lesion scoring was done as follows: 0: normal, 1: mild, 2: moderate, and 3: severe (Table S3) (Huff et al., 2004). Histopathology of the ID group for the disease trial was not performed due to time constraints.

APEC recovery from organs and blood: After examining organs for colibacillosis lesions, they were carefully collected in sterile Nasco– Whirl-Pak™ (ThermoFisher Scientific, Canada) and transported to the Advancing Canadian Water Assets (ACWA) Microbiology Laboratory at the University of Calgary for bacteriology and phage analysis. Blood samples from 1 dpi and euthanasia day were collected and 10–fold serially diluted in PBS followed by plating onto MacConkey agar supplemented with 10 µg/mL gentamicin (Dalynn Biologicals, Calgary, Alberta) to obtain an APEC count, inhibiting commensal E. coli growth.

The surfaces of organs (heart, liver, spleen, and lungs) were decontaminated using a Bunsen burner and 70% ethanol. The outer layer was excised using sterile instruments to access inner, uncontaminated tissue. Then, a 1–g portion of the decontaminated organ was placed into a gentleMACSTM M tube (Miltenyi Biotec, California, USA) containing 2 mL of PBS with 0.1% Triton X and processed using the ‘protein_01_01’ program for 1 minute, with a gentle MACS dissociator (Miltenyl Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). The resulting organ homogenate was serially diluted in PBS and plated onto MacConkey agar supplemented with 10 µg/mL gentamicin. After incubating at 37°C for 24 h, presumptive pink colonies were isolated and enumerated to determine CFU/g of tissue.

To recover APEC from the oviduct, ovary, and bone marrow, two sterile cotton swabs (15.2 cm, Puritan, Maine, USA) were used. Using sterile instruments (scissors and forceps), the outer layer of each organ was removed, and swabs were taken from the inner layers of ovary, oviduct (isthmus, magnum and uterus [Note that the avian “uterus” is part of the oviduct and is distinct from the mammalian uterus]) (Scanes, 2020) and bone marrow. One swab was inoculated in 5 mL TSB supplemented with 10 µg/mL gentamicin and incubated at 37°C for 18–20 h. The other swab was resuspended into 5 mL of PBS, followed by plating onto MacConkey agar supplemented with 10 µg/mL gentamicin and incubated at 37°C for 24 h.

To confirm identity of isolates recovered from organs, genomic DNA was obtained from isolated pink colonies using the boiling method; extracted DNA was stored in TE buffer (10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0; ThermoFisher Scientific, Alberta, Canada) at –20°C. Simplex PCR was used to confirm the O78 serogroup, whereas multiplex PCR was used to detect the presence of five virulence genes (iroN, ompT, hlyF, iss and iutA) (Ryan et al., 2011) using a Qiagen Hot Star Kit (Markham, Ontario, Canada) (primer sequences are listed in Table S4a; Chalmers et al., 2017; Johnson et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2014).

Phage recovery from organs: Number of phages present in the organs was determined as described (Tsonos et al., 2014). Briefly, 0.7 mL of organ homogenate, as obtained above, was mixed with lambda diluent (0.5 mL), followed by centrifugation (11,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C) and filtration through 0.8/0.2 µm filter units (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Filtrates were enumerated for phages as described above.

2.7 Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata/SE 17.0 for Mac (Stata Corp. 2021, College Station, TX, USA), and R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data from various infection trials were pooled and analyzed. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non–normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median along with first (Q1) and third (Q3) quartiles. For all statistical models, significance was determined at p < 0.05.

Time kill assay: Time–kill assay data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects model to account for repeated measurements over time. OD600nm measurements were square-root transformed before statistical analysis to meet the assumptions of parametric mixed-effects models. The mixed model was used to generate pairwise comparisons between different phage treatments across MOIs and within a given MOI, as well as pairwise comparisons between different MOIs within each phage treatment. Phage treatment, MOI, time, and their interactions were included as fixed effects, with replicate treated as a random effect and a compound symmetry covariance structure applied to model within-replicate correlations. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using Tukey’s method, and statistical significance was defined using standardized thresholds (p < 0.05). Superior phage efficacy was defined as complete inhibition of bacterial growth (i.e., OD600 ≤ 0.01) (Niu et al., 2021).

Replicate-level AUC values for the phage cocktail were calculated using the trapezoidal rule and summarized as mean ± SD. Pairwise comparisons between MOIs were performed using t tests with pooled variance and Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05, and results were summarized using compact letter displays (CLDs).

Clinical score: Individual scores of four clinical signs (depression, breathing difficulty, changes in comb color, and difficulty in movement) were summed to calculate a total clinical score for each hen. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare total clinical score across groups, followed by a post–hoc analysis using Dunn's test adjusted for multiple comparisons with Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995; Dunn, 1964). Additionally, a Spearman rank–order correlation analysis was done to assess associations between scores of the four clinical signs. Considering the weak to moderate correlation coefficients, each clinical sign score was also analyzed individually as an outcome variable, using the same method as for total score.

Mortality rate: Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted to assess hen mortality and a Mantel–Cox test was performed to compare survival curves across treatment groups.

Body Weight (BW): A mixed–effect multilevel linear regression model was fitted to determine effects of dose on body weight loss by 7 dpi. Baseline body weight was assessed as a potential confounder in this and all other regression models and was retained in the model(s) when its removal changed the main coefficient(s) by > 20% (Dohoo, I., W. Martin, and H. Stryhn, 2010). In the phage efficacy trial, 3 linear regression models were used to determine effects of phage treatment groups on cumulative weight loss by 1, 2 and 4 dpi. Residual plots were visually inspected to verify model assumptions, including normality and homoscedasticity.

Relative organ weight: Effects of treatment groups on relative organ weight (fraction) were assessed using β regression models (Ferrari and and Cribari–Neto, 2004; Heinz and Schemper, 2002) for each organ, adjusted by baseline weight. Average marginal effects of treatment groups on the conditional mean relative organ weight (fraction) compared to the reference group were estimated at the mean baseline body weight as post–estimation of the β regression model, using the ‘margins, dydx’ command in STATA, and results were visualized using the ‘marginsplot’ command.

Macroscopic and histopathological scores: First, a binary variable was created for each organ to indicate the presence or absence of a lesion, regardless of severity. For each hen, the proportion of organs affected by a macroscopic lesion was calculated by dividing numbers of organs with lesions by total number of organs assessed. Similarly, proportions of organs with histopathological score ≥ 1 was calculated. These proportions of affected organs were then used as outcome variables to evaluate impacts of treatment groups on the extent of organ lesions. A fractional logit regression and firth logistic regression model was used to analyze proportions of affected organs across treatment groups and organs respectively, separately for macroscopic lesions and histopathological lesions. Second, macroscopic and histopathological lesion scores between groups, separately for each organ, were compared using a Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by a post–hoc analysis using Dunn's test with BH adjustment for multiple comparisons. Finally, associations of disease groups and phage groups with actual macroscopic lesion scores and microscopic lesion scores of the organs were assessed using mixed–effect ordinal logistic regression models and an ordinal logistic regression model, respectively. Final model coefficients were exponentiated and odds ratios (OR) along with 95% confidence interval (CI) adjusted by baseline weight were presented along with the corresponding p–values.

APEC recovery and count from organ homogenate: APEC recovery was analyzed in 2 stages. First, a binary variable was created, reflecting presence or absence of bacteria. To assess effects of treatment groups and tissue types on the odds of APEC presence, a Firth logistic regression model was employed using Firth's penalized likelihood method to address small sample size and potential separation issues (Firth, 1993; Heinze and Schemper, 2002), with final model coefficients exponentiated to obtain OR. Second, actual APEC counts were log10–transformed and modelled using mixed–effect linear regression and the model coefficients, adjusted by baseline body weight, were presented along with the 95% CI and corresponding p–values. The model intercept was set at the average baseline body weight (grams) to facilitate interpretation.

APEC recovery from swab samples: The association of treatment groups and swab tissue type with presence or absence of APEC from swab samples was assessed using Firth logistic regression, separately for infection and phage efficacy study.

Phage count: Phage recovery was analyzed in 2 stages. First, a binary variable was created reflecting presence or absence of phages and analyzed using a mixed–effect logistic regression model. Second, actual phage counts were log10–transformed and modelled using mixed–effect linear regression and model coefficients, adjusted by baseline body weight, were presented along with the 95% CI and corresponding p–values. The model intercept was set at the average baseline body weight (grams) for meaningful interpretation.

2.8 DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene amplification

Cecum samples from phage–treated and non–treated groups were collected in Nasco Whirl-Pak™ bags (ThermoFisher Scientific, Canada), transported to the ACWA laboratory, and immediately stored at −80°C. Cecal contents were processed, and DNA was extracted using a PowerSoil® DNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen, USA). DNA quality (260/280 ratio) and concentration were measured using a NanoDrop ND–1000 spectrophotometer (Isogen Life Science B.V., Veldzigt), and samples were subsequently diluted to 50 ng/µL with elution buffer (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) before submission to Genome Québec (Montréal, Canada).

Library preparation and sequencing

To amplify the V4 region, primers 515F and 806R (Table S4b; Clavijo et al., 2022) were used, the resulting amplicon libraries were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq PE 250 bp, with a total sequencing output of ∼10 million read pairs using the paired–end protocol (250 bp paired–end reads). Quality verification and summarization of raw reads were performed using FastQC 0.11.9 and MultiQC 1.12 (Ewels et al., 2016). Sequence trimming was performed with Trimmomatic 0.39 (Bolger et al., 2014) to remove primers and low–quality read sections with settings HEADCROP:10, SLIDINGWINDOW:5:20, LEADING:20, and TRAILING:20. Further statistical analyses were performed in R 4.1.0 (R Core Team, 2023). Trimmed paired reads were additionally filtered with DADA2 1.22.0 (Callahan et al., 2016) using filterAndTrim with default settings and then merged. Chimeric sequences were removed from the merged sequence set with DADA2 removeBimeraDenovo. Taxonomic classification of 16S sequences was done with DADA2 IdTaxa using the SILVA 138 database (Quast et al., 2013) to construct an amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) table. Phyloseq 1.38.0 (McMurdie and Holmes, 2013), vegan 2.5–7 (Oksanen et al., 2020) and DESeq2 1.34.0 (Love et al., 2014) were used for statistical analysis and plots visualized with ggplot2 3.3.5 (“Wiley Interdisciplinary Reviews,” n.d.).

Alpha (α) diversity of the dataset was assessed with the Shannon diversity index and richness (observed ASVs) using vegan and plotted. The ASV table was filtered for ASVs present (count ≥ 2) in at least 1% of samples to reduce noise in downstream analysis. In addition, one–way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate α diversity across treatments and timepoints. Microbial community structure was analyzed with vegan 2.6–4 (Oksanen et al., 2020) using β–dispersion and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 9999 permutations to determine differences in microbial community structure between treatment groups. To calculate beta (β) diversity, filtered ASV counts were normalized with size factors calculated with GMPR in DESeq2.

Pairwise sample distances were determined with the Bray–Curtis metric using phyloseq ordinate and visualized with detrended correspondence analysis (DCA). The phyla and genus with the highest abundance in each treatment and sample timepoint were identified with phyloseq and visualized. Significant (p < 0.05) differentially abundant genera were identified with DESeq2 by fitting a negative binomial model to each treatment vs. treatment comparison (“∼Treatment”). Significant genera were visualized with a heatmap.

3 RESULTS

3.1 Time kill assay to determine best phage and MOI for in vivo study

Time–kill assays were conducted to determine the optimal phage formulation and MOI for the in-vivo chicken study. Anti-APEC O78 was dependent (p < 0.001) on phage preparation, MOI, and exposure time, with the three-phage cocktail (ASO78A+ASO78B+AVIO78A) consistently demonstrating enhanced efficacy (p < 0.001; Fig. S1). The optimal MOI for enhanced anti-APEC efficacy was 100 (p < 0.001). At MOI=100, the three-phage cocktail was capable of inhibiting 100% of APEC growth across sampling times (Mean OD600nm = 0.005 ± 0.006) outperforming (p < 0.001) single phage ASO78A (90% inhibition; Fig. S1a) and ASO78A+ASO78B (88% inhibition; Fig. S1a). Notably, even at MOI=0.00001, the cocktail inhibited growth by 52% (p < 0.0001) compared to bacteria only control while other treatments showed no effect.

AUC analysis was used as an integrative measure of phage efficacy to capture cumulative bacterial growth over the 24-h assay, thereby reflecting both the rate of bacterial killing and growth inhibition across all sampled time points. Lower AUC values indicate both accelerated APEC killing and sustained inhibition of regrowth. At each MOIs tested, the three-phage cocktail (ASO78A+ASO78B+AVIO78A) produced statistically lower (p < 0.05) AUC values than individual phages or 2-phage combinations, indicating superior time-integrated suppression of APEC O78 growth over the 24-h a period (Fig. S1b). Specifically, among the MOIs tested, the 3-phage cocktail at MOI = 100 yielded the lowest cumulative AUC (0.126 ± 0.060; p < 0.01). Consequently, the three-phage cocktail at MOI=100 was selected for further in vivo validation.

3.2 Disappearance of phage DW and estimated phage intake

To determine the approximate phage dose, water disappearance was measured every 24 h in the DW phage group prior to the APEC challenge. With a phage titre in the drinking water ranging from 3×107 to 108 PFU/mL, the estimated daily phage intake per bird was between 2.1×109 and 8×109 PFU (based on 10 birds per pen).

Whereas overall α– and β–diversity metrics were unaffected by phage treatment, substantial shifts in relative abundance of specific taxa occurred between groups. APEC infection broadly reduced beneficial genera, including Lactobacillus and Alloprevotella, whereas DW phage treatment appeared to partially preserve Lactobacillus abundance compared to APEC–only or IM phage groups. 4 DISCUSSION

Here, we demonstrated the potential for prophylactic use of a phage cocktail administered via DW or IM injection against APEC O78 infection in adult layers. Overall, pre–exposure to phages, particularly via DW, was more effective in reducing APEC burden and improving clinical outcomes, while also preventing the development of macroscopic and microscopic lesions. For poultry, administering antimicrobials through DW is routinely practiced for controlling both intestinal and extraintestinal bacterial infections including colibacillosis (Agunos et al., 2020). During the laying period, antibiotics are often avoided due to the risk of drug residues in eggs, leaving APEC–infected hens untreated and potentially culled. Preventive measures, such as proposed phage administration before an anticipated disease outbreak, are therefore crucial for enhancing hen health and welfare (Zia and Alkheraije, 2023).

Therapeutic outcomes of phage administration via the oral route were consistent with phage treatment reported by Oliveira et al. (2010). However, other studies have shown that oral administration was incapable of reducing APEC load in the broiler gut (Kittler et al., 2020) and did not improve clinical signs in affected broilers (Tsonos et al., 2014). The efficacy of phage delivery−particularly via the oral route−is critically determined by several interrelated factors, including optimization of administration parameters (timing, dosage, and frequency), the phage’s tolerance to gastric acid and survival through the digestive tract, and mitigation of interference from the resident gut microbiota; successful therapeutic results require consideration of these variables to ensure phages can reach to bacterial infection sites and execute lysis efficiently (Dąbrowska, 2019; Nang et al., 2023; Ryan et al., 2011). In this study, superior efficacy of phages in drinking water may be attributed to two factors. First, thrice–daily administration of phage cocktails through drinking water, with the last dose 2 h before APEC IV challenge, indicated that phages translocated across mucosal barriers, entered the bloodstream, and lysed APEC in various tissues and organs (Dąbrowska, 2019; Nang et al., 2023). This inference is supported by the recovery of phages from the heart, lungs, liver, and spleen of hens in the DW group, although at the low levels. Second, phages administered prior to APEC infection may have stimulated the chicken immune system, contributing to more efficient clearance of APEC (Górski et al., 2023). Information on phage–regulated immune function in avian cells is scarce. However, an oral phage cocktail for chickens modulated immune responses by producing interferon (IFN)–β and increased IL–10, an anti–inflammatory cytokine (Cianci et al., 2025). Such phage–induced modulation appears to enhance therapeutic efficacy against Salmonella enterica infection by preventing excessive inflammation and promoting controlled immune activation (Podlacha et al., 2024). This mechanism may similarly have supported more efficient clearance of APEC in our study. Key innate immune responses, such as those regulated by heterophils (avian neutrophils) and macrophages are crucial in fighting APEC (Alber et al., 2021). In fact, our ongoing investigation demonstrated that APEC phages alone are capable of stimulating nitric oxide production in the avian macrophage cell line (unpublished data), warranting further verification of immune involvement in an in–vivo model.

A critical challenge in oral phage therapy is ensuring consistent dosage under the constraints of mass administration. We targeted a daily dose of 3 ×1010PFU per hen; however, reflecting the behavioral variables inherent to flock-level medication, the estimated intake was 3−10 times lower. Crucially, the fact that significant protection was achieved despite this variable and lower consumption demonstrates the therapeutic robustness of the cocktail under practical application conditions. Furthermore, daily monitoring confirmed that phage titres remained stable, ruling out substantial loss due to adherence to plastic drinker surfaces.

We developed an E. coli peritonitis syndrome model in 25–27–week–old SPF layers by experimentally challenging them intravenously with ≥ 108 CFU/mL of APEC O78 strain. Although the IV route does not fully replicate natural exposure, it is a validated method for consistently inducing systemic colibacillosis and peritonitis in experimental poultry (Landman et al., 2013; Mellata, 2013). Model validity was demonstrated by mortality rate and systemic infection, characterized by typical gross organ lesions and bacterial recovery from a wide range of infected organs, consistent with reports from experimentally or naturally infected hens (Abdelhamid et al., 2024; Landman et al., 2013; Ozaki and Murase, 2009).

Notably, the high-dose infection model resulted higher mortality than the APEC only control in the phage trial, despite standardized experimental conditions. While the exact cause is uncertain, such variability is possible in in vivo APEC challenge models and may reflects a combination of bacterial virulence dynamics and host factors. APEC virulence is heavily dependent on large, transmissible plasmids (e.g., ColV, pAPEC-1), which can be unstable and prone to curing during storage or subculturing, leading to attenuated phenotypes (Mellata et al., 2010; Sprouffske et al., 2016). In addition, subtle variations in flock immunity, such as maternal antibody levels or stress resilience, can significantly alter susceptibility to infection between different batches of birds (Leitner et al., 1990). However. despite the lower mortality rate in the efficacy trial controls, control birds still exhibited severe clinical signs and high lesion scores consistent with fibrinous polyserositis, the hallmark of avian colibacillosis (Pourbaksh, et , 1997). Importantly, the DW phage cocktail significantly reduced these lesion scores and bacterial organ loads compared to controls. This suggests that the cocktail is robust enough to mitigate disease severity even in sub-lethal infection scenarios.

Regarding tissue pathology, considerable microscopic lesions were observed in the lung, liver, spleen, heart, and oviduct that appeared to be associated with specific clinical signs (e.g., increased respiration rate, open–mouth breathing, and depression). Information on APEC–induced histopathology is limited in laying hens. However, our observations on histopathological changes were similar to those from 3 dpi of 26–week–old commercial layer pullets intratracheally inoculated with APEC serogroup O1 (Abdelhamid et al., 2024). Understanding histological changes in organs from APEC–infected layers and their relationship to clinical signs would facilitate diagnosis of colibacillosis at an early stage and inform relevant management practices (e.g., treatment and isolation of presumptive APEC–infected hens) to reduce pathogen spread. Upon APEC infection, systemic infiltration leading to inflammation of organs, manifested as swelling, congestion, and fibrinoheterophilic exudate, occur rapidly. These events may lead to enlarged organ (heart, liver, lungs, and spleen), consistent with previous findings in broilers (Naghizadeh et al., 2019).

In contrast, with pre–addition of phages in DW, gross lesions at necropsy were absent and histopathological damage largely attenuated, although relative weight of several organs was changed. Especially, splenic enlargement was observed in phage-treated chickens, aligning with finding in broilers (Naghizadeh et al., 2019), suggesting a persistent or complex immune interaction. In addition, the observed ovarian and oviduct shrinkage may reflect the physiological stress of combating APEC infection. This stress response has been previously linked to the drop in egg production characteristic of colibacillosis (Kathayat et al., 2021) and was similarly observed in our low-dose infection model. We inferred that phages effectively killed APEC in vivo, minimizing its capacity for systemic damage. Perhaps phages modify cytokine production to promote anti–inflammatory effects in avian cells, enhancing bacterial clearance and protecting tissues. For example, oral administration of a phage cocktail in weaned pigs reduced inflammation and enhanced antioxidant responses due to decreased interleukin (IL)–1β, IL–6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)–α (Li et al., 2024). Data on effects of phage treatment on histopathological changes of internal organs are scarce. To the best of our knowledge, only one report demonstrated that intranasal administration of phage Bϕ–R2096, a member of Caudoviricetes, greatly reduced histological lung damage in mice infected with Acinetobacter baumannii (Jeon et al., 2019). In addition to clearing bacteria, the ability of phages to protect against tissue damage is crucial for maintaining organ function, thereby sustaining organ health and overall well–being. Despite its importance, this protective role of phages is poorly understood and warrants further study.

Surprisingly, IM phage administration only protected hens from acute death caused by APEC but did not improve other outcomes such as development of gross and microscopic lesions or bacterial recovery, although the difference was not statistically significant. This contrasts with previous successes of IM injection in broilers and quail (Huff et al., 2005; Naghizadeh et al., 2019a). Differences from past reports may relate to variations in preventive use of phage delivery in the present study, whereas others focused on treatment. We administered phages 1–2 h before introduction of APEC into the bloodstream, enabling phages to circulate and be ready to target APEC. On 1 dpi, in 80% of the hens, we recovered phages at an average level of 4 log10 PFU/mL from the blood, indicating they were circulating. Phage recovery from blood after IM administration was considerably higher than that reported from the above–mentioned broiler study and comparable to those reported for E. coli phage recovery from non–immune germ free mice (Geier et al., 1973). In a pilot trial, there were no clinical signs in laying hens inoculated either with ultrafiltered phages or filtered phages alone, as used for the broiler study (Huff et al., 2003, 2005). Thus, it is unlikely that phages caused harm. The inefficacy of IM inoculation of phages may be due to phage neutralization and inactivation by serum antibodies in the bloodstream, preventing them from binding to APEC (Naghizadeh et al., 2019b; Popescu et al., 2021). Notably, in contrast to phage recovery from all organs in the DW group, phages were only recovered from the spleen in the IM phage group. Perhaps fewer active phages were capable of reaching other organs before APEC invasion, further supported by the similar levels of APEC recovered from the IM and APEC–only groups.

Administration of phages, regardless of the delivery approach, did not affect the general population and composition of cecal microbiota of laying hens. Similarly, administration of phages targeting Salmonella (Clavijo et al., 2022) and Campylobacter jejuni (Richards et al., 2019) did not disturb the general structure of cecal microbiota in broilers. However, relative abundance of some genera varied between healthy and diseased hens, with or without administration of phages. The intestinal micro–ecosystem, comprising gut bacteria and their environment, is crucial for maintaining health, and disruptions in this balance can affect host–microorganism interactions. Although an exact link between gut bacteria and intestinal disease is not fully established, many intestinal diseases in poultry (e.g., due to Salmonella and Campylobacter jejuni) are associated with imbalances in gut bacteria (Shi et al., 2022b). Among the nine relatively abundant genera, APEC infection elevated relative abundance of Megamonas and Faecalibacterium, but decreased Lactobacillus, Alloprevotella, Ruminococcus torques, Prevotellaceae UCG–001, and Olsenella. In a previous study of chicks inoculated with APEC O78, there were changes in the genus composition of the fecal microflora, particularly decreases in Proteobacteria, Faecalibacterium, Lactobacillus, and Prevotella (Wang et al., 2022). In our study, Lactobacillus, the second most abundant genus, was 10–20% less abundant in APEC–infected hens, which may be reflected by reduced body weight across APEC–infected hens compared to APEC–free hens. Lactobacillus has previously been shown to be highly enriched in high body weight chickens (Zhang et al., 2023). Interestingly, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus in the DW phage group was higher compared to the APEC–only groups, suggesting that oral phage administration may protect beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus (Grubb et al., 2020).

Despite phages administered in DW protected hens from APEC infection, this study presents several experimental limitations. First, impacts on egg production were not determined. Future research should involve laying hens during peak egg production. Second, the experimental period lasted only 4 days post-infection to focus on APEC survival in organs during the acute phase. Subsequent studies should assess the protective effect of phages in natural infection and chronic disease models, addressing long-term effects of phage treatment on health and performance. Third, water was withheld for 2 h prior to dosing to promote uptake. This may have induced transient stress, meaning a contribution of stress to the therapeutic outcomes cannot be excluded (Wurtz et al., 2024). In addition, a key limitation of our study was the absence of a phage-only control group, which prevented assessment of the independent effects of phage administration on host physiology and microbiota. To ensure the safe use of phages in DW, a phage-only group should be included in future experiments. Regarding emergence and frequency of phage resistance, we are currently finalizing whole-genome sequencing data of APEC isolates recovered from the study and from broth culture (Gohar et al., 2025).

This study served as a proof-of-concept supporting the targeted efficacy. However, findings from these controlled SPF trials may not fully translate to commercial flocks facing complex microbial and environmental stressors. To determine field implications, the next steps involve optimizing dose and frequency of phage delivery as well as benchmarking protective efficacy in commercial settings, which also includes assessing phage stability in on-farm water sources. As with other phage applications, host specificity remains a concern for targeting diverse APEC serogroups. Therefore, the continuous expansion of phage libraries capable of lysing a broad range of serogroups using conventional isolation and genome editing is recommended (Choi et al., 2024; Ferriol-González and Domingo-Calap, 2021). Additionally, combining phages with other antimicrobial approaches, such as direct-fed microbials, may enhance the overall protective effect (Soffritti et al., 2025).

In conclusion, this study was the first to demonstrate protective effects of phages delivered in DW against development of colibacillosis in laying hens. Phages in DW effectively killed APEC without compromising the general gut microbiota, thereby improving the general health of the hens and protecting organs from APEC invasion. Delivering phages through DW was efficacious and feasible, holding great promise for control of antimicrobial-resistant bacterial infections such as colibacillosis in the poultry industry.
