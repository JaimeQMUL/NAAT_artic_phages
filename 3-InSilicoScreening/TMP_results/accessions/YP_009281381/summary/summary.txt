- **Thermal Stress During Formulation:** High temperatures (e.g., 100–170 °C) used in spray drying can compromise phage structural integrity and infectivity. 
- **Stabilization:** Excipients like trehalose are noted for their ability to preserve phage infectivity across various temperature conditions by forming a protective amorphous matrix. 
- **Storage Stability:** Long-term stability testing guidelines (ICH Q1A(R2)) involve accelerated (40 °C/75% RH) and long-term (25 °C/60% RH) conditions.
- **Temperature Sensitivity:** Phage viability is sensitive to thermal stress, with Myoviridae phages generally showing greater resilience than Podoviridae or Siphoviridae under identical processing conditions.

Environmental Context:
- The context is primarily related to the *stability* of the phages during processing (drying, storage) and *in vivo* conditions (e.g., lung environment), not the natural growth environment of the phages or their bacterial hosts.

Concise Summary:
The review focuses heavily on the physicochemical stability of bacteriophages when formulated for dry powder inhalation, rather than the optimal growth temperature of the phages or their bacterial targets. Thermal stress is a major concern during formulation, particularly during spray drying, where high inlet temperatures (100–170 °C) can denature capsid proteins and reduce infectivity. Stabilization relies on excipients like trehalose, which form protective amorphous matrices to maintain structural integrity. Storage stability is assessed under ICH guidelines (e.g., 25 °C/60% RH). The discussion notes that phage resilience varies by family, with Myoviridae generally being more robust than Podoviridae or Siphoviridae under thermal stress. No specific growth temperature range for the phages is provided; the focus is on preservation during processing and storage.
