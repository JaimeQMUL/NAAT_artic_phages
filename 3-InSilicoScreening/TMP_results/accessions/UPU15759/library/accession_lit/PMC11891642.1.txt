Abstract

The emergence of antimicrobial‐resistant foodborne pathogens poses a continuous health risk and economic burden as they can easily spread through contaminated food. Therefore, the demand for new antimicrobial agents to address this problem is steadily increasing. Similarly, the development of rapid, sensitive, and accurate pathogen detection tools is a prerequisite for ensuring food safety. Phage‐derived proteins have become innovative tools for combating these pathogens because of their potent antimicrobial activity and host specificity. Phage proteins are relatively free from regulation compared to phages per se, and there are no concerns about the transduction of harmful genes. With recent progress in next‐generation sequencing technology, the analysis of phage genomes has become more accessible, and numerous phage proteins with potential for biocontrol and detection have been identified. This review provides a comprehensive overview of phage protein research on food safety from 2006 to the present, a pivotal period marked by the certification of phages as Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS). Emphasizing recent advancements, we investigated the diverse applications of various phage proteins for biocontrol and detection purposes. While highlighting the successful implementation of these proteins, we also address the current bottlenecks and propose strategies to overcome these challenges. By summarizing the current state of research on phage‐derived proteins, this review contributes to a deeper understanding of their potential as effective antimicrobial agents and tools for detecting foodborne pathogens.

1 INTRODUCTION

In the post‐antibiotic era, the emergence of antimicrobial‐resistant foodborne pathogens poses a critical threat to food hygiene and human well‐being. According to a World Health Organization (WHO) report on June 7, 2021, 600 million cases of foodborne illnesses are reported annually (WHO, 2021). The WHO estimates that approximately 60% of global foodborne disease cases can be attributed to foodborne bacteria (WHO, 2015). Foodborne pathogenic bacteria can easily contaminate food, causing numerous diseases that have severe effects on human health and economic burden. As reported by the EFSA regarding 2022 zoonoses in the European Union, the pathogenic agents causing foodborne outbreaks include Salmonella spp. (17.60%), Bacillus cereus toxins (5.31%), Campylobacter spp. (4.42%), Staphylococcus aureus toxins (2.38%), Escherichia coli (including STEC and non‐STEC cases, 1.49%), Clostridium perfringens toxins (0.95%), Listeria monocytogenes (LM) (0.61%), Yersinia spp. (0.24%), and Vibrio spp. (0.16%) (European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) & European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), 2023). Considering that the causes of 46.14% of foodborne disease outbreaks remain unidentified, the observation that these nine foodborne pathogens accounted for 33.16% of all reported cases is particularly noteworthy. This proportion represented more than half of the remaining identified cases (53.86%), highlighting the critical impact of these pathogens on food safety. Foodborne illnesses induced by these organisms often result in acute symptoms, including gastrointestinal issues, such as diarrhea, vomiting, nausea, or abdominal cramps, as well as neurological symptoms, such as headaches, paralysis, or paresthesia (Imre et al., 2020). Therefore, ongoing surveillance of foodborne pathogens in the food chain, coupled with rapid detection and successful biocontrol, is a top priority for ensuring food safety. Although the advent of antibiotics has enabled the treatment of a broad range of bacterial infections, its success has not lasted long. Antibiotic resistance has emerged and spread rapidly owing to the overuse and misuse of antibiotics in the clinical and agricultural fields (Mann et al., 2021). In 2019, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) announced that over 2.8 million people in the United States are affected by antibiotic‐resistant bacteria, resulting in more than 35,000 deaths each year (CDC, 2019). Therefore, in 2017, the WHO recommended an overall reduction in the use of medically important antimicrobials in food‐producing animals and advocated the complete restriction of such antimicrobials for growth promotion. Experts in the US food and animal industry embraced these recommendations, given that the FDA enforced an actual ban on the utilization of medically important antibiotics for growth promotion through voluntary initiatives in 2017 (Scott et al., 2019). Hence, there is an urgent need for novel antimicrobial agents that can replace conventional antibiotics for the biocontrol of foodborne pathogens.

The preemptive and precise detection of foodborne pathogens is crucial for the prevention and mitigation of foodborne diseases. The current standard for detecting pathogens in food has traditionally been culture‐based methods, such as the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) horizontal method 21528‐2:2017, which is highly sensitive and time‐consuming (ISO, 2018). Another approach for diagnosing bacteria involves nucleic acid‐based techniques such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Although PCR is a powerful and high‐precision testing method, it requires expensive equipment and expertise. This growing demand for novel detection tools has led to the development of various rapid and accurate detection methods. Antibodies (Abs) are the most prevalent tools for rapid detection but face several challenges: Polyclonal Abs often exhibit cross‐reactivity and batch‐to‐batch variability. Moreover, monoclonal Abs typically result in a low production yield and modest affinity and are highly susceptible to changes in pH and salt concentration (Le Basle et al., 2020). The need to develop specific, stable, and affordable agents for pathogen detection remains unresolved. In this context, bacteriophages are gaining attention as alternatives to conventional antibiotics and Abs. Bacteriophages are the most abundant entities in nature, which exclusively infect and replicate within bacterial cells (Stone et al., 2019). Phages are generally classified into lytic and temperate phages based on their life cycles (Naureen et al., 2020). During the lytic cycle, phages replicate within host cells after infection and release progeny viral particles via bacterial lysis. In contrast, temperate phages integrate their DNA into the host genome, which may persist as plasmids (Chevallereau et al., 2022). Under conditions that threaten the survival of host cells, these phages can reenter the lytic cycle, leading to the release of progeny virions (Monteiro et al., 2019).

Many researchers have focused on two major properties of lytic phages that can be leveraged as antimicrobial agents: (1) their specificity in recognizing appropriate complementary receptors on bacterial hosts and (2) their lytic ability after a short viral life cycle, leading to the rapid eradication of the targeted bacteria (Strathdee et al., 2023). In addition, the simplicity of phage production and relatively low production costs make it ideal for practical applications in various fields such as food production and processing (Palma, 2023). For example, Listex P100 that contains bacteriophage P100 at a concentration of 2 × 1011 plaque‐forming units was certified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the US FDA in 2006 for use in cheese preservation, specifically to prevent Listeria contamination on the surface of cheese (Soni et al., 2012). Since then, studies utilizing phages against foodborne pathogens have been actively conducted, resulting in the approval of 17 phage products as GRAS by the FDA until December 2024 (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2024) (Table 1). Several clinical trials of phage therapy for the treatment of bacterial infections are currently ongoing worldwide, indicating the potential of phages in medical applications beyond food safety for antibiotic‐resistant bacterial infections (Cui et al., 2024). Furthermore, phages have served as clinical diagnostic tools and detection agents for foodborne pathogens owing to their host recognition ability and high specificity. Generally, phage‐based diagnostic techniques can be categorized into three groups: (1) phage amplification assays, which can only detect viable cells; (2) phages as recognition tools; and (3) quantification of intracellular components following phage‐induced lysis (Meile et al., 2020; Paramasivam et al., 2022). These methods include the utilization of natural phages or genetically engineered phages carrying reporter genes (e.g., green fluorescent protein [GFP], β‐Gal, Lux operon).

Building on the potential of phages to promote food safety, global regulatory approval for their use in the agro‐food sector is gradually increasing. However, phages are typically regulated by existing frameworks rather than by dedicated legislation. For example, phages fall under the GRAS certification in the United States, similar to other chemical compounds or protein‐based preparations, and they are categorized as feed additives or processing aids in the EU (M. J. Costa, Pastrana, et al., 2023; Jaglan et al., 2022). Many other countries, including Australia, New Zealand, Canada, Switzerland, and Israel, have also approved the use of phages for food safety purposes based on US regulatory guidelines (Jaglan et al., 2022; J. Li, Zhao, et al., 2022). These approaches overlook the distinct biological properties of phages, including their inherent self‐replicating nature and genomic flexibility, and raise concerns regarding their long‐term safety and genetic variability (Gutierrez et al., 2019; Jonczyk‐Matysiak et al., 2019; Torres‐Barcelo, 2018). Unlike single chemical substances, phages are dynamic living entities that interact with the environment in a complex manner. Therefore, tailored regulatory frameworks that specifically address the unique characteristics of phages are essential to ensure their safe and effective use in food safety.

In addition to regulatory challenges, phages exhibit certain functional drawbacks that must be addressed for their practical application in food safety. The emergence of phage‐resistant cells, narrow host ranges, and phage‐mediated transduction may hinder the efficiency of phage applications (Arya et al., 2020; Gabashvili et al., 2020; Stone et al., 2019). Although lytic phages possess a lower propensity for transduction than that of lysogenic phages, a risk of dissemination of virulence‐ or antibiotic‐resistance‐related genes remains (Marcelli et al., 2020; Wolput et al., 2024). One possible scenario is that mispackaging of host DNA into viral capsids during virion assembly could lead to transduction (Leclerc et al., 2022). The long‐term implications of these “mistakes” are yet to be fully understood. Furthermore, the limited binding spectrum, large size of the whole phage particles, and phage‐induced lysis could be bottlenecks for diagnostic applications (Ye et al., 2022).

The turning point came with the recent advances in next‐generation sequencing technologies, opening up tremendous opportunities for whole‐genome sequencing of various organisms (Hu et al., 2021). Among newly identified phage genes with high biotechnological value, phage‐derived proteins associated with lysis and host recognition have been extensively explored because of their potential utility. Moreover, their proteinaceous nature and straightforward mechanisms alleviate concerns related to the regulatory complexities and phage‐induced transduction, positioning phage‐derived proteins as practical and promising alternatives to intact bacteriophages to address food safety challenges (Anany et al., 2019). Therefore, numerous research groups have explored the therapeutic and diagnostic potential of phage proteins, and several review papers have summarized their findings (S. P. Costa, Nogueira, et al., 2023; Klumpp et al., 2023; Nazir et al., 2023; Topka‐Bielecka et al., 2021; Xu, 2021). However, most reviews have primarily focused on specific types of phage proteins such as endolysins or receptor‐binding proteins (RBPs) and have discussed their partial roles as antibacterial agents or pathogen recognition elements. In this review, we aimed to provide a comprehensive and in‐depth perspective on the diverse applications of various phage‐derived proteins, highlighting their dual potential for both biocontrol and pathogen detection in food safety. Additionally, we focused specifically on nine major foodborne pathogens that were selected based on their prevalence and impact on public health. Given the GRAS certification of phages for food preparation purposes in 2006 and the subsequent surge in related research, we reviewed studies published from 2006 onwards. Finally, we critically examine the key challenges that must be addressed and discuss recent approaches to overcome these limitations.

2 METHODS

A literature search using Google Scholar was performed to identify studies investigating the use of phage‐derived proteins for food safety. Boolean operators (e.g., AND and OR) were employed to refine the search results. Example queries, including “endolysins OR depolymerase AND Salmonella” and “phage tail AND detection,” were used to identify relevant studies. The works were screened using the following criteria: Selection focus: Studies focusing on phage‐derived proteins (e.g., endolysins, depolymerases, and tail proteins) used for the detection or control of foodborne pathogens were included. For biocontrol studies, only those conducted in food matrices or contact surfaces, targeting biofilm eradication, or involving in vivo experiments were considered.

Target pathogens: The included studies investigated one or more of the pathogens that included Bacillus cereus, Campylobacter spp., Clostridium perfringens, Escherichia coli, Listeria monocytogenes, Salmonella spp., Staphylococcus aureus, Vibrio spp., or Yersinia spp.

Publication criteria: Only peer‐reviewed studies published in English since 2006 were included in the analysis.

Studies discussing whole‐phage therapy or those addressing nonspecific pathogens were excluded. Works were selected by manually screening titles and abstracts to ensure their relevance. The selected studies were categorized based on protein type (e.g., endolysins, depolymerases, and tail proteins) and application (e.g., bacterial eradication and pathogen detection). The analysis focuses on summarizing key outcomes, evaluating challenges in practical implementation, and proposing future research directions in this field.

3 PHAGE‐DERIVED PROTEINS FOR BIOCONTROL PURPOSES

3.1 Introduction of phage‐derived degrading enzymes

Food contamination by bacterial pathogens poses a major threat to human health across the food production, distribution, and consumption chains. These pathogens often form biofilms on biotic and abiotic surfaces, causing recurrent foodborne disease outbreaks (Alvarez‐Ordonez et al., 2019; Gutierrez et al., 2016). The emergence of antibiotic‐resistant bacteria has raised concerns, underscoring the urgent need to develop alternatives to conventional antimicrobials. In this regard, phage‐derived enzymes that target foodborne pathogens and their biofilms have become the focus of research. These enzymes offer several advantages over conventional antibiotics, including high target specificity and a low risk of developing bacterial resistance (Shao‐Yeh et al., 2021). Delving into the dynamics of the phage's life cycle, several phage proteins participate in the degradation of bacterial components and the subsequent lysis of the host. In the first step of infection, phages recognize and bind to host receptors, such as outer membrane (OM) proteins, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), teichoic acids, fimbriae, or flagella located on the bacterial cell envelope (Rakhuba et al., 2010). Subsequently, virion‐associated lysins (VALs), also known as virion‐associated peptidoglycan hydrolases (PGHs), generate small holes within the peptidoglycan layer to facilitate the injection of phage DNA (Baldysz et al., 2024; Oliveira et al., 2018) (Figure 1a). These enzymes are occasionally observed in the tails, baseplates, or necks of phages that infect Gram‐positive or Gram‐negative bacteria (Latka et al., 2017). When bacterial surface polysaccharides such as capsular polysaccharides (CPS), exopolysaccharides (EPS), or LPS impede the phage's access to its receptor (Van Houdt & Michiels, 2010), phages employ their depolymerase, typically in the form of a tail spike protein (TSP), to digest these polysaccharides, creating gaps or channels in the cell envelope and thereby enhancing the ability of the phage to access bacterial cells (Knecht et al., 2019) (Figure 1b). Depolymerases are divided into two main groups based on their mode of action: (1) O‐glycosyl hydrolases (EC 3.2.1) require a water molecule for specific cleavage of the O‐glycosidic bonds within polysaccharide, and (2) lyases (EC 4) cleave the glycosidic bond through β‐elimination in uronic acids (Olszak et al., 2017). Based on their substrate specificity, depolymerases are categorized into PGH, endosialidases, endorhamnosidases, alginate lyases, and hyaluronate lyases (Drulis‐Kawa et al., 2015). The degradation ability of phage depolymerases enhances the susceptibility of bacterial cells to the host immune system by targeting the extracellular components responsible for bacterial virulence (Latka et al., 2017), thus acting as an adjuvant for serum‐mediated killing (Majkowska‐Skrobek et al., 2016; Oliveira et al., 2020; Olszak et al., 2017). Therefore, phage depolymerases have recently received considerable attention as effective anti‐virulence agents because of their promising antimicrobial potential against both Gram‐negative and Gram‐positive bacteria.

After the replication of phage particles inside the bacterial host, phages employ a specific enzyme called endolysin to enable progeny virions to burst out of the host cell (Abdelrahman et al., 2021). Endolysins are phage‐encoded PGH that cleave specific bonds within the peptidoglycan layer, resulting in host cell lysis (Fischetti, 2010) (Figure 1c). Most endolysins lack signal sequences, preventing their translocation through the inner membrane and limiting their access to substrates in murein (Fischetti, 2010). Consequently, endolysins accumulate in the cytosol in a fully folded state during the vegetative cycle (Aguiar et al., 2019). At a specific moment, a small membrane protein called holin forms patches in the cytoplasmic membrane, triggering membrane disruption and ultimately enabling the entry of endolysin into the peptidoglycan layer (Berry et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2003). Most endolysins from phages of Gram‐positive hosts have a modular configuration consisting of one or more enzymatically active domains (EADs) at the N‐terminus and a cell wall‐binding domain (CBD) at the C‐terminus, connected by a short, flexible linker (Oliveira et al., 2013). Endolysins from Gram‐negative host‐infecting phages are normally small globular proteins with an average molecular weight of 15–20 kDa and consist of a single EAD without a distinct CBD module (Briers et al., 2009). An EAD cleaves a specific bond within a peptidoglycan, whereas a CBD helps the EAD target its substrate by recognizing and binding to its receptors in the cell wall, including peptidoglycan (Matamp & Bhat, 2019). Based on the cleavage site in the murein layer, EADs can be subdivided into three main classes and six subclasses: Glycosidases break the β‐1,4 glycosidic bonds between N‐acetylmuramic acid (MurNAc) and N‐acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) in the peptidoglycan structure. In particular, N‐acetyl‐β‐d‐muramidases cleave bonds between MurNAc and GlcNAc; N‐acetyl‐β‐d‐glucosidases target bonds between GlcNAc and MurNAc residues; and lytic transglycosylases catalyze the non‐hydrolytic cleavage of the peptidoglycan (Matamp & Bhat, 2019).

Amidases, such as N‐acetylmuramoyl‐l‐alanine amidase, function as hydrolases that specifically target the amide bond in the peptide stem, situated between the MurNAc and L‐alanine in murein structure (Abdelrahman et al., 2021).

Endopeptidases can cleave peptide bonds within the interpeptide bridge or intrapeptide stem of the peptidoglycan structure (Matamp & Bhat, 2019). Examples of endopeptidases include l‐alanoyl‐d‐glutamate endopeptidase (VANY), c‐d‐glutamyl‐m‐diaminopimelic (DAP) acid peptidase, d‐Ala‐m‐DAP endopeptidase, and d‐alanyl‐glycyl endopeptidase (CHAP), among others (Abdelrahman et al., 2021).

To date, there are no reports of endolysin‐resistant bacteria (Blasco et al., 2020). Altering the structure of peptidoglycan, the target of endolysins, may be challenging because of its crucial role in bacterial survival. Peptidoglycan acts as a rigid support fixture to prevent bacteria from undergoing osmotic lysis (Mahalanabis et al., 2009). This lytic enzyme derived from phage, originally designed for internal lysis, can induce host lysis through external treatment when applied to Gram‐positive bacteria with exposed PG layers (Schmelcher & Loessner, 2016). Therefore, it is gaining attention as a promising biocontrol agent against foodborne pathogens in the food industry, agriculture, and various other clinical fields. 3.2 Food applications of phage‐derived degrading enzymes

3.2.1 Evaluation in food matrices

Although bacteriophages have become attractive antibacterial agents and offer a significant advantage in terms of their high host specificity, there are several limitations to the use of phages for food safety. First, the exact bacterial species or strains for eradication need to be known, and the appropriate phage preparations to target them are required (Hyman, 2019). Second, phages can transduce antibiotic‐resistance or bacterial virulence genes (Colavecchio et al., 2017). Third, limited information is available regarding interactions between phages and human hosts. Recently, Zamora et al. (2024) discovered that phages can penetrate the airway epithelial cells and induce diverse immune responses. Further investigation of these interactions is imperative to establish safety guidelines for reliable phage therapy. In addition to these practical hurdles, the lack of adequate regulations to ensure the long‐term safety of phages and consumer hesitation to purchase virus‐treated food further constrain the broad adoption of phages for food safety purposes (Mahony et al., 2020).

However, phage‐derived lytic enzymes, such as endolysins, depolymerases, and VALs, are free from the concerns associated with phage applications because of their proteinaceous nature, which makes them more suitable biocontrol agents in the food industry. During phage purification, the propagation of phages often necessitates the use of hazardous foodborne pathogens, which poses inherent risks and highlights the critical need for stringent purification procedures. Conversely, phage‐derived enzymes can be efficiently produced using nonpathogenic bacterial cells or plants, offering numerous advantages over whole‐phage preparations (Hammond et al., 2019; Islam et al., 2019; Lu et al., 2020; Sui et al., 2021). Indeed, an endolysin targeting C. perfringens produced by a plant host was first approved as a GRAS (GRN 802) in 2018 (Table 1). Currently, many approaches involve the direct application of these enzymes to foodborne pathogens with an emphasis on evaluating their lytic potential in various food matrices.

Common vehicles of foodborne disease outbreaks typically include dairy products, fresh produce, meat, and processed animal products (Lee & Yoon, 2021; White et al., 2022). Among the various food sources, milk and cheese provide a favorable environment for microbial contamination because of their high nutritional content, including sugars, proteins, fats, and other organic compounds; their low sodium concentrations; and their neutral pH (Fusco et al., 2020; Ibarra‐Sanchez et al., 2017). In this context, N. Li, Yuan et al. (2022) and Chang, Yoon et al. (2017) confirmed the successful antimicrobial activity of B. cereus and S. aureus‐targeting endolysins in milk samples. These studies demonstrate that low concentrations of endolysins are sufficient for significant bacterial reduction. Specifically, 46 µg/mL of endolysin achieved a 4.7‐log CFU/mL reduction of B. cereus cells, whereas 1.88 µM of endolysin combined with the essential oil‐derived compound carvacrol (6.66 mM) reduced S. aureus cells to undetectable levels. In parallel, Listeria poses a substantial economic burden to the dairy industry as it can survive under cold storage conditions (Chowdhury & Anand, 2023; Gonzales‐Barron et al., 2023). Based on these characteristics, Ibarra‐Sanchez et al. (2021) investigated the growth‐inhibitory effects of LysP100, an endolysin derived from GRAS‐certified phage P100, on LM in cheese. Their study confirmed the effective bacteriostatic ability of LysP100 in queso fresco over 28 days at 4°C, with complete sanitization achieved when combined with a nisin derivative. Similarly, Misiou et al. (2018) developed a combined method of pretreating PlyP825 (34 µg/mL) with high hydrostatic pressure processing at 400 MPa for pasteurizing LM in milk and cheese. This method resulted in an effective reduction of over 4‐log CFU/mL and CFL/g of viable LM cells in milk and mozzarella cheese at room temperature and even under cold storage at 10°C for 20 days.

In addition to dairy matrices, Salmonella spp., E. coli., and C. perfringens represent major threats to meat products (Bendary et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2020; Punchihewage‐Don et al., 2022), prompting investigations into endolysin‐based disinfection. Abhisingha et al. (2023) and Lu et al. (2023) assessed endolysin potential in poultry that is particularly related to Salmonella and C. perfringens outbreaks (Mora et al., 2020; Shaji et al., 2023). Both studies exhibited that each endolysin could significantly kill viable Salmonella and C. perfringens cells within chicken and duck meat, resulting in 1.6 and 3.2‐log CFU/g reductions, respectively. Y. Wang, Li et al. (2024) engineered an endolysin by fusing short peptides for the biocontrol of E. coli O157:H7, demonstrating its effectiveness in beef. This engineered endolysin exhibited outstanding pathogen removal efficacy, killing E. coli cells by 6 log and 4 log CFU/cm2 in combination with tea polyphenols in beef at 37 and 25°C, respectively. For pork applications, we compared the antibacterial efficacy of the staphylococcal phage STAP152 and its endolysin LysP152 at low and room temperatures. Notably, LysP152 exhibited better bactericidal activity at 4°C compared to that of the whole phages, achieving a reduction of over 1 log CFU/mL of S. aureus at a concentration of 70 µg/cm3 within 3 h, whereas STAP152 (MOI = 100) eradicated less than 1 log CFU/mL cells under the same conditions. In the seafood sector, Vibrio spp. are recognized as one of the most prevalent and dangerous causes of bacterial infections (Brauge et al., 2024; EFSA Panel on Biological Hazards (BIOHAZ) et al., 2024). H. Q. Ning, Lin et al. (2021) examined the applicability of Lysqdvp001, a Vibrio parahaemolyticus‐specific endolysin, in seafood. They demonstrated the broad applicability of the combination of Lysqdvp001 and ε‐poly‐lysine in Gadus macrocephalus (pacific cod), Penaeus orientalis (oriental shrimp), and oysters, resulting in reductions of 3.75, 4.16, and 2.50 log CFU/g, respectively.

Numerous studies have demonstrated the successful feasibility of phage‐derived proteins for direct use in a wide range of food products, such as meat, fresh produce, and dairy that are commonly associated with severe foodborne illness outbreaks (Interagency Food Safety Analytics Collaboration, 2023). However, the potential sensory effects of these proteins on food quality remain unclear. For example, enzymatic treatments may interact with food components such as proteins, fats, and carbohydrates, potentially leading to unintended alterations in taste, texture, color, and aroma (Pang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2018). Considering that sensory attributes significantly influence consumer preferences and purchasing decisions (Li, Wang, et al., 2024; Vivek et al., 2020), conducting controlled sensory analyses is essential to improve the commercial viability of biocontrol strategies in the food industry.

3.2.2 Evaluation on food‐contact surface

In addition to inappropriate food‐handling conditions, bacterial pathogens can indirectly contaminate food by forming biofilms on various contact surfaces such as those in food manufacturing plants or even directly on the food itself (Carrascosa et al., 2021; Rather et al., 2021). Biofilms are composed of extracellular polymeric substances that act as a solid barrier for bacterial survival, conferring tolerance to harsh external stimuli such as sanitizers, antibiotics, and physical treatments (Abebe, 2020; Ciarolla et al., 2022). This persistence makes biofilm removal challenging, leading to recurring microbial contamination in food‐production facilities and spoilage of food products, and this causes significant economic losses (da Silva et al., 2021; X. Liu, Yao, et al., 2023). Currently, researchers are actively exploring phage‐derived enzymes as anti‐biofilm agents for applications in common food‐contact materials, including stainless steel, plastics (e.g., polypropylene and polystyrene), and glass, to build safer food‐handling environments (Geueke et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2022). Notably, the nine pathogens highlighted in this review are capable of forming biofilms across diverse food‐processing facilities, further increasing contamination risks and complicating sanitation efforts (X. Liu, Yao, et al., 2023). Among these, S. aureus poses a serious threat to public health because of its multidrug resistance and strong biofilm‐forming capacity in both food and medical settings, emphasizing the urgent need to develop effective anti‐biofilm solutions (Ahmed et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2021). To address this challenge, Cha et al. (2019) evaluated the biofilm‐disrupting potential of LysCSA13 from S. aureus phage CSA13 on various food‐contact surfaces. The results revealed that LysCSA13 could remove preformed S. aureus biofilms at 2‐log and 3‐log CFU/coupon on stainless steel and glass, respectively. Chang, Kim et al. (2017) assessed the biofilm removal capacity of the staphylococcal endolysin LysSA11 against multidrug‐resistant S. aureus (MRSA) on food utensils. A 30‐min treatment with LysSA11 (1.35 µM) successfully eradicated biofilm matrices by approximately 4‐log CFU/cm2 on a polypropylene cutting board and a stainless‐steel knife. Other studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of various phage‐derived endolysins and their depolymerization against biofilms formed by B. cereus, E. coli, Salmonella spp., and Vibrio spp. biofilms (Hasan et al., 2024; Park & Park, 2021; Seol et al., 2024; Srinivasan et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021).

Although removing existing biofilms is essential, preventing biofilm formation is equally important for maintaining a safe environment. Guo et al. (2017) investigated the anti‐biofilm activity of the depolymerase Dpo42 from E. coli phage vB_EcoM_ECOO78 against E. coli clinical isolates. Although Dpo42 was unable to eliminate preformed biofilms of E. coli strains, it effectively inhibited biofilm formation in a dose‐dependent manner in 15 of 34 clinical E. coli isolates grown on polystyrene. Another promising strategy involves the combination of phage‐derived enzymes with traditional antibiotics to prevent biofilm formation. Kim et al. (2023) demonstrated the inhibitory potential of cotreatment of endolysin (LysPB32), depolymerase (DpolP22), and antibiotic (polymyxin B) against S. typhimurium biofilms on polystyrene surfaces. This combined method significantly reduced biofilm formation by S. typhimurium. Although the promising capabilities of phage proteins for biofilm eradication or prevention have been demonstrated in laboratory settings, further evaluation in real‐world environments is required to assess the applicability of these enzymes. In actual food‐processing settings, several factors may influence the enzymatic efficacy of phage proteins, including cleanliness of food‐processing facility surfaces, temperature fluctuations, humidity, and pH levels (Fellows, 2022; K. Yang, Shi, et al., 2022). Additionally, the current research bias toward evaluating the anti‐biofilm activity of phage proteins on polystyrene surfaces must be addressed by testing them on a wide variety of food‐contact materials. Further in‐depth studies examining the shelf stability and performance of these proteins under typical food‐handling conditions are crucial for their widespread implementation.

3.2.3 Evaluation in farm animal models

Zoonotic infections occur through the transmission of pathogens from animals to humans and represent a significant public health threat (Rahman et al., 2020). In livestock farming, high‐density rearing of animals in confined spaces creates an environment conducive to the rapid spread of bacterial infections, resulting in the widespread use of antibiotics as both preventive measures and treatments to maintain animal health and productivity (Kirchhelle, 2018; Manyi‐Loh et al., 2018). The indiscriminate and excessive use of antibiotics has accelerated antimicrobial resistance globally, leading to a growing public health crisis (Blaser et al., 2021; Vaughn et al., 2022). Furthermore, this trend is exacerbated in regions lacking regulatory frameworks, where the low cost and easy accessibility of antibiotics encourage their misuse (Roope et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2021). The most frequent bacterial zoonotic causative agents are Campylobacter spp., E. coli, Salmonella spp., S. aureus, and Vibrio spp. that cause numerous foodborne illnesses annually (Alomari et al., 2021; European Food Safety Authority & European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, 2021; Ziarati et al., 2022). The prevalence of these pathogens in food systems, coupled with the severity of antimicrobial resistance, requires innovative solutions to mitigate their impact, prompting the investigation of phage‐derived proteins for their antimicrobial effects in vivo. Miletic et al. (2015) and Waseh et al. (2010) investigated the antibacterial effects of Salmonella phage P22 depolymerase in chickens. Miletic et al. demonstrated that P22 depolymerase fused with elastin‐like polypeptide (Gp9‐ELP) reduced S. typhimurium colonization by 1‐log CFU/g, whereas Waseh et al. observed a 70‐fold decrease in Salmonella count in chicken cecum treated with P22 depolymerase compared to that in the BSA‐treated group following oral administration.

3.3 Challenges of using phage‐derived degrading enzymes in food applications

3.3.1 Impact of food matrices

In Section 3.2.1, we discuss the numerous findings that demonstrate the potential of phage proteins as novel antibacterial agents in complex food matrices. However, variability in the physicochemical properties of foods (e.g., pH, salt concentration, ionic strength, temperature, and physical topology) and food components (proteins, carbohydrates, fats, minerals, and vitamins) can influence the efficacy of phage proteins in real‐world applications (Nazir et al., 2023; Shannon et al., 2020). The presence of several proteases present in foods can also undermine the antimicrobial activity of phage enzymes. For practical use in the food industry, phage enzymes must endure high temperatures during food processing and harsh storage conditions. Therefore, analysis of the optimal biochemical conditions for endolysins is necessary prior to food application to establish criteria for selecting suitable food matrices. Furthermore, optimizing protein formulations to enhance their stability and activity under diverse conditions, such as heat treatment, freezing, and high‐fat environments, is essential.

3.3.2 Bacterial resistance risks

Unlike traditional antibiotics that typically inhibit bacterial metabolic pathways inside the cells, phage endolysins and VALs induce cell lysis by breaking down the cell wall (Gondil et al., 2020; Rahman et al., 2021). As the bacterial cell wall is essential for survival, modifying the peptidoglycan structure to evade endolysin action imposes a significant fitness cost on bacteria (Melnyk et al., 2015; M. Wang, Buist et al., 2022). Moreover, the rapid lytic activity of endolysins and VALs reduces the selective pressure, preventing bacteria from developing resistance through prolonged exposure. This fundamental difference may render these phage enzymes more resilient to bacterial defense mechanisms. Although no cases of endolysin or VAL resistance have been reported to date (Abdelrahman et al., 2021), this possibility remains. Given the known evasion strategies employed by bacteria against existing antibiotics, two potential mechanisms of resistance to phage‐derived peptidoglycan‐degrading enzymes could arise: (1) the production of endolysin‐binding or degrading enzymes, similar to β‐lactamases, and (2) the thickening or modification of the bacterial cell wall to avoid osmotic lysis by the these proteins (Bahr et al., 2021; Cui et al., 2021; Escobedo et al., 2023; Grishin et al., 2020; Hyo et al., 2013; Tooke et al., 2019; Wilson et al., 2020). Phage depolymerases that act on the surface of bacterial cells in a manner similar to that of endolysins are subject to adaptation concerns. Several studies have demonstrated that exposure to depolymerases for up to 24 h does not necessarily result in host resistance (Chen et al., 2022; Oliveira, Costa, et al., 2019; Oliveira, Mendes, et al., 2019). However, bacteria may develop countermeasures to evade their functions by altering the surface substrates targeted by depolymerases. Therefore, beyond single‐phase protein treatments, future strategies should explore the synergistic effects of phage enzymes in combination with other antimicrobial agents or treatments to enhance their effectiveness and reduce the risk of bacterial resistance.

3.3.3 In vivo stability of phage proteins

For effective treatment of bacterial infections in livestock models, comprehensive pharmacokinetic and immunogenicity analyses are essential to overcome the instability and short half‐life of phage proteins. Current approaches for evaluating the antibacterial efficacy of phage enzymes in animal models have reported residual activity lasting from a few minutes to several hours (Antonova et al., 2024; Jun et al., 2017; Loeffler et al., 2003). This rapid loss of proteins may be attributed to neutralizing Abs, renal clearance, and degradation by various proteases, presenting significant challenges in the treatment of systemic bacteremia (Fischetti, 2010; Seijsing et al., 2018). Designing suitable formulations, such as encapsulation and enzyme engineering, is critical for enhancing the stability of phage proteins and extending their therapeutic activity in vivo.

3.3.4 Challenges in obtaining phage‐derived enzymes

Among the various phage‐related antibacterial proteins, endolysins have garnered significant attention because of their rapid and straightforward modes of action (H. Liu, Hu, et al., 2023; Rahman et al., 2021). The modular structure and relatively small size of endolysins further enhance their potential for biotechnological applications (Lee et al., 2023; Zheng & Zhang, 2024). However, the commercialization of endolysins in the food industry is still in its early stages because most endolysin applications have focused on their therapeutic efficiency against bacterial infections in the clinical field (de Wit et al., 2019; Ho et al., 2022; Jun et al., 2017). Some phage endolysins exhibit low protein yields and solubilities, which are not ideal for industrial applications (Balaban et al., 2022). Moreover, identifying novel endolysins with desirable functionalities is resource‐intensive, involving the typical process of phage isolation, genomic analysis, enzyme production, and functional characterization (Lee et al., 2021), and success is not always guaranteed. Indeed, existing studies examining phage‐derived proteins are heavily biased toward certain bacterial species such as S. aureus, Salmonella spp., E. coli, and Listeria spp., whereas comparatively less work has focused on pathogens such as Campylobacter spp. or Yersinia spp. This imbalance may stem from the challenging culture conditions required for Campylobacter (Jackel et al., 2019) and the relatively low incidence of foodborne disease outbreaks caused by Yersinia (Simpson et al., 2020). To circumvent these challenges, Fernandez‐Ruiz et al. (2018) employed a metagenomic approach by leveraging genomic data from uncultured viruses to mine novel endolysins using bioinformatic methods. More intensive investigations are needed to identify the phage proteins that target Campylobacter spp. or Yersinia spp.

In contrast to that of endolysins, research investigating the antibacterial potential of VALs and depolymerases is relatively limited. This gap stems from the greater complexity involved in discovering VALs or depolymerases compared to endolysins. Although endolysins are typically secreted as individual proteins, facilitating their identification, VALs and depolymerases are often embedded within structural components such as phage tails, requiring more intricate genetic and bioinformatic analyses for discovery (Concha‐Eloko et al., 2024; Magill & Skvortsov, 2023). Recent technological advancements such as deep learning‐based genome mining tools are beginning to address these challenges and are discussed in detail in Section 4.3.

3.4 Recent approaches to overcome the limitations

3.4.1 Techniques to enhance the protein production

With the classification of the C. perfringens‐targeting endolysin as a GRAS for food preparation in 2018, ongoing efforts have focused on ensuring the safe utilization of endolysins. The efficient production and purification of endolysins remain essential prerequisites for the practical application of these antimicrobial enzymes. Currently, most studies employ E. coli expression systems to produce recombinant endolysins, but the heterologous expression of recombinant phage endolysins often encounters difficulties due to codon bias and issues related to protein toxicity (Kaur et al., 2018). The former problem can be addressed by introducing competent cells with additional tRNAs corresponding to rare E. coli codons (Krishnappa et al., 2023; Nariya et al., 2011; Yu et al., 2021) or by codon optimization of the target endolysin sequence (Deshotel et al., 2024; Escobedo et al., 2023; Orlando et al., 2020). The latter problem is more challenging because endolysins are originally bacterial cell‐specific lytic agents. In addition, E. coli expression poses immunogenicity issues owing to the LPS of host cells, which can act as endotoxins in the human body. To overcome these barriers, several studies have introduced novel expression systems, such as plant‐based expression systems and yeast systems. Kazanaviciute et al. (2018) were the first to report the successful expression and purification of six C. perfringens‐specific endolysins from edible plants, such as beets, spinach, and lettuce. These endolysins demonstrated lytic activity against a panel of food‐contaminating C. perfringens strains under various salt and pH conditions relevant to food preparation environments and effectively inhibited the growth of C. perfringens in cooked turkey. These plant‐derived endolysins, which are enzymatically active and free from endotoxins, were certified as GRAS by the FDA in 2018. Chun et al. (2020) used a yeast surface display system to express the staphylococcal endolysin LysSA11. Yeast cells displaying LysSA11 achieved a 5‐log reduction in viable S. aureus cells within 3 h, retaining enzymatic activity for 14 days under refrigerated conditions, which surpassed that of purified soluble LysSA11.

3.4.2 Strategies to improve the protein stability and lytic activity

The desire to produce more effective and stable phage lysins has led to a focus on protein engineering, which involves truncating or swapping domains from parental endolysins. In several instances, the lytic potential of endolysins was enhanced by CBD removal. CBD truncation of the staphylococcal phage endolysin LysK resulted in a 2‐fold improvement in its muralytic capacity (Horgan et al., 2009). Similar phenomena were observed in previous studies (Gaeng et al., 2000; Loessner et al., 1999; Loessner et al., 1998; Low et al., 2005). The function of the CBD is to recognize and bind to the appropriate target within the cell wall structure, thereby restricting the ability of the EAD to cleave the intended target by localizing the entire enzyme. For CBD‐independent endolysins, CBD can hinder the hydrolytic activity of EAD by increasing the overall enzyme size (Choi & Kong, 2023; Horgan et al., 2009). This may obstruct endolysin access to the peptidoglycan mesh layer, leading to decreased enzymatic activity (Demchick & Koch, 1996). The positive charge of endolysins is another key factor in the effective interaction between the enzyme and negatively charged targets, such as lipoteichoic acids (Low et al., 2011). Thus, an overall increase in the net positive charge of the catalytic domain through CBD truncation may play a crucial role in enhancing lytic activity. The structure‐based truncation of enzymes can improve protein solubility and enzyme activity. Maciejewska et al. (2023) revealed that the first N‐terminal 26 residues of the depolymerase from Klebsiella phage KP34 (KP34gp57) are not responsible for the overall catalytic activity of the enzyme. Consequently, these N‐terminal residues were deleted, leading to highly soluble forms of truncated depolymerases. Moreover, certain truncated derivatives of KP34gp57 exhibited enhanced enzymatic activity, with decreased minimal halo‐forming units ranging from 1.5 to 5.9 nM compared to the wild‐type depolymerase (23.4 nM).

In addition to truncation, the modular structure of endolysins allows domain swapping, which involves the exchange or rearrangement of domains, resulting in novel chimeric endolysins with desired antimicrobial features. These hybrid endolysins not only possess enhanced muralytic capacity (Diez‐Martinez et al., 2015; Eichenseher et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2017) and an extended bactericidal range (Dong et al., 2015; Haddad Kashani et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2021) but also exhibit improved solubility (Daniel et al., 2010) and strong thermal stability (Eichenseher et al., 2022; Swift et al., 2015) within food matrices and on contact surfaces. Fernandes et al. (2012) constructed chimeric endolysins by fusing the highly soluble enterococcal endolysin EAD with an S. aureus‐specific CBD, which has low protein solubility. Both hybrid endolysins, Lys168‐87 and Lys170‐87, exhibited high solubility and broadened lytic activity against MRSA clinical isolates and other Gram‐positive pathogens such as Streptococcus pyogenes. To generate highly thermostable endolysins, Swift et al. (2019) fused three different EADs from thermophile‐infecting phage endolysins with five CBDs from various C. perfringens phage endolysins. They created 15 novel chimeric endolysins that demonstrated improved thermal stability compared to the parental C. perfringens endolysins and effectively controlled C. perfringens strains. Similar approaches, termed domain shuffling, generate chimeric endolysins with high‐throughput outcomes through the random recombination of input domain libraries and subsequent screening. Son et al. (2020) constructed a random domain rearrangement library of four different staphylococcal endolysins and screened it to obtain an engineered endolysin by employing spanin‐based lysis of E. coli cells expressing the engineered endolysin. The hybrid endolysin Lys109, consisting of the CHAP domain from LysSA12 and amidase plus CBD from LysSA97, showed improved lytic activity toward S. aureus cells in milk and on stainless‐steel surfaces, along with enhanced thermal/pH stability and effective biofilm removal capacity. Choi et al. (2023) produced a novel chimeric endolysin targeting C. perfringens by shuffling the domains of the C. perfringens phage endolysin, LysCPD9, with those of the thermostable endolysin LysCPS2. This fusion protein, ClyY, comprising the EAD of LysCPD9 and a tandem repeat of the CBD from LysCPS2, exhibited higher lytic activity than its parental endolysin LysCPD9, demonstrating a remarkable bactericidal efficiency of 4‐log CFU/mL and 3‐log CFU/cm2 in milk and beef, respectively, in 2 h. These domain‐shuffling approaches have predominantly targeted S. aureus because extensive knowledge of staphylococcal endolysins provides abundant resources for enzyme engineering (Schmelcher & Loessner, 2016). Studies have largely focused on evaluating the antimicrobial efficacy of chimeric endolysins in milk. To enhance the practical applications of these engineered enzymes, their enzymatic capabilities in diverse food matrices should be extensively explored.

Mutagenesis is another strategy used to enhance the lytic activity and thermostability of phage lytic proteins. Zhang et al. (2015) developed a mutant endolysin by substituting two amino acids of endolysin from E. coli phage Bp7, designated as Bp7Δe. Bp7Δe was purified in soluble form and showed intense antimicrobial efficacy and expanded lytic range than its parental endolysin. Additionally, Mayer et al. (2011) found that structure‐based modification of the active‐site residues of the CD27L endolysin, which targets Clostridium difficile, resulted in increased listeriolytic activity. Heselpoth et al. (2015) introduced computation‐based direct mutagenesis of streptococcal endolysin PlyC using the FoldX and Rosetta algorithms. One of the mutants, with the substitution of the amino acid residue 406 from threonine to arginine, showed an increase in the denaturation temperature by 2.2°C and thermal kinetic stability by 16‐fold compared to the wild‐type. This enhancement can be attributed to a newly formed hydrogen bond between the 106th glutamine and substituted 406th arginine, which contributes to the overall thermal stability of the enzyme. Recent advances in in silico prediction of protein structure can provide rational mutation strategies for endolysins, generating endolysins with enhanced lytic and physicochemical stability.

The most recent approach for improving the stability and efficacy of endolysins involves the utilization of protein nanostructures. Dzuvor et al. (2022) added a specific peptide (P114) capable of autonomous assembling in response to pH reduction into endolysin P128, resulting in the formation of endolysin nanoparticles (P128NANO). P128NANO demonstrated superior lytic potency at concentrations 2–3 times lower than a single protein against methicillin‐resistant S. aureus strains, along with notable thermostability (up to 65°C). Similarly, Solanki et al. (2013) attempted to immobilize Listeria phage endolysin Ply500 using silica nanoparticles (SNPs) to enhance the overall protein stability of these listericidal nanocomposites (Ply500‐SNP). Consequently, Ply500‐SNP exhibited superior storage stability compared to the single endolysin, retaining over 95% of its initial log‐killing activity after 40 days at 4°C, whereas only 50% was retained by the single endolysin, Ply500. Bai et al. (2019) developed a liposome‐encapsulated endolysin, BSP16Lys, to target gram‐negative pathogens. This strategy demonstrated the enhanced bactericidal efficacy of liposomes containing BSP16Lys, leading to a >3‐log CFU/mL reduction in Salmonella cells at a 2.0 mM total lipid concentration. These results suggest that liposome encapsulation improves endolysin access to the OM of gram‐negative bacteria. However, further studies are needed to confirm whether liposomes truly penetrate the OM and precisely quantify the actual endolysin content within them.

In Gram‐negative bacteria, the OM covering the peptidoglycan impedes the effective action of exogenously applied endolysins, unlike in Gram‐positive bacteria, where the peptidoglycan layer is exposed and accessible to endolysins. To overcome this limitation, endolysin engineering involves the attachment of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) to enhance endolysin permeability through the OM. The term “Artilysin” was first established by Briers et al. (2014) to describe the fusion of an AMP with phage lysins that target Gram‐negative bacteria. AMPs typically contain positively charged amino acid residues and a significant proportion of hydrophobic residues, making them cationic or amphiphilic in nature (Huan et al., 2020; Papagianni, 2003). The amphipathicity of AMPs enables them to interact with the OM, leading to the disruption of the OM of the host cell and enhanced access of lysins to peptidoglycan. This proof‐of‐concept has been successfully applied to the development of lytic chimeras against Acinetobacter baumannii, Cronobacter sakazakii, E. coli, and V. parahaemolyticus (Islam et al., 2022; D. Kim, Kim, & Kim, 2024; H. Ning, Cong, et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2019). Another approach to enhance the synergistic antibacterial effect of phage proteins involves combining them with organic acids or essential oils (Oliveira et al., 2016; Sisson et al., 2024; Zermeno‐Cervantes et al., 2023). These compounds disrupt the integrity of the OM in host cells, thereby enhancing the accessibility of phage enzymes in a manner similar to that of AMPs.

3.4.3 Efforts to improve in vivo stability and reduce toxicity

The efficacy of phage‐derived proteins for in vivo applications depends on their biodistribution and residual time within the host system. For systemic infections, protein‐based therapeutics must ensure uniform circulation to effectively target and eradicate pathogens throughout the body. Simultaneously, they should exhibit a sufficiently long half‐life while avoiding clearance or neutralization by the host immune system. These strategies reduce the need for frequent administration and improve overall therapeutic efficiency. To achieve this goal, Resch et al. (2011b) engineered a dimerized anti‐pneumococcal endolysin to reduce renal clearance. They introduced a cysteine residue at the C‐terminus of Cpl‐1 to facilitate the formation of disulfide bonds between the two monomers, increasing the endolysin size beyond the glomerular excretion threshold of 60 kDa (Maack et al., 1979; Meibohm & Zhou, 2012). The resulting Cpl‐1 dimer demonstrated a significantly reduced plasma clearance rate (one‐tenth of that of the native form) and doubled the antibacterial efficacy against Streptococcus pneumoniae in vitro. Seijsing et al. (2018) expanded the endolysin structure by utilizing an albumin‐binding domain (ABD) to create an albumin‐endolysin complex. The ABD used in this study was derived from streptococcal protein G that is known for its role in immune evasion by binding to host plasma proteins and masking bacterial surfaces (Nilvebrant & Hober, 2013). Research has demonstrated that the staphylococcal endolysin LysK fused with ABD forms a stable complex with albumin. This complex exhibits enhanced biodistribution, reduced renal clearance, and improved bioavailability in a mouse model. Another approach to prolong the protein half‐life is PEGylation, a technique that attaches polyethylene glycol (PEG) to the protein of interest. Numerous studies have validated this strategy to enhance protein stability and reduce immunogenicity (Batra et al., 2012; Nie et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2004). However, attempts to apply PEGylation to anti‐pneumococcal endolysin were unsuccessful. Resch et al. (2011a) introduced amino acid substitutions into Cpl‐1 for cysteine‐mediated covalent bonding with PEG. Although these substitutions did not affect the enzymatic activity of the endolysin, the addition of PEG inhibited it. This finding suggests that PEGylation may not be a viable strategy for improving the in vivo half‐life of endolysins.

The metabolic mechanism of phage proteins is also a critical factor for evaluating their safety for in vivo administration. However, few studies have investigated how phage proteins are digested in the gastrointestinal system, the toxicity of phage enzyme‐derived metabolites, or their impact on the gut microbiota. Although some studies have examined the interactions of bacteriocins with animal systems or simulated gastrointestinal digestion conditions (Gough et al., 2017, 2018; Soltani, Hammami, et al., 2021; Soltani, Zirah, et al., 2021), more comprehensive studies are required to understand the influence of these exogenous proteins on physiological systems.

4 PHAGE‐DERIVED PROTEINS FOR DETECTION OF FOODBORNE PATHOGENIC BACTERIA

4.1 Introduction of phage‐derived binding proteins

Extensive studies are currently underway to incorporate various affinity reagents, such as Abs or aptamers, into in‐field rapid analytical interfaces, such as lateral‐flow assays (LFAs), for convenient bacterial detection. Although Abs are the most frequently used detection agents, in many cases, they present a significant bottleneck, known as the “reproducibility crisis” (Baker, 2015). This is attributed to issues such as nonspecific binding, which involves recognizing additional receptors beyond the original target molecule, and batch‐to‐batch variability in Abs. Furthermore, the susceptibility of Abs to changes in physicochemical conditions poses serious problems, leading to compromised efficacy or loss of function, particularly when operated under harsh conditions or during long‐term storage. To develop robust and cost‐effective probes, several diagnostic research groups have investigated the replacement of Abs with aptamers. Aptamers are single‐stranded nucleic acid molecules with a three‐dimensional structure capable of binding to specific targets with strong affinity. Although aptamers offer significant advantages as detection agents, such as convenience of production and high stability under various environmental conditions, their binding targets are limited to small molecules or toxins because of their simple nucleic acid structures (Kumar Kulabhusan et al., 2020). Only a limited number of aptamers have been used to detect bacteria with highly variable and complex surface structures (Teng et al., 2016). Therefore, the development of alternative affinity molecules for whole‐cell detection is crucial.

Since the advent of high‐throughput sequencing, marked by the introduction of the Roche/454 sequencer in 2005, exciting opportunities for analyzing the whole genome of bacteriophages have emerged. A plethora of phage RBPs, typically located at phage tails in the form of tail spikes or fibers, have been identified, and their binding properties have been extensively explored for applications in diagnostics and detection. In the initial step of tailed‐phage infection, RBPs, which are molecular apparatuses designed to selectively identify host cells, penetrate the cellular membrane and inject phage genetic material into the cytoplasm, are employed (Nobrega et al., 2018). The majority of tailed phages are categorized into three families based on their tail morphology. Podoviridae phages are characterized by a very short, non‐contractile tail, typically carrying six tail spikes. Phages belonging to the Myoviridae family, on the other hand, feature a long, contractile tail with a base plate, whereas Siphoviridae phages have a long, flexible, and non‐contractile tail (Vinga et al., 2012). Long tail fibers (LTF or tail fiber protein, TFP), attached to the phage base plate, consist of three twisted subchains, forming a homotrimeric, long filamentous structure (Klumpp et al., 2023). They bind to cell surface receptors, such as OM proteins, flagella, pili, teichoic acids, and LPS, but lack enzymatic activity (Figure 2a) (Nobrega et al., 2018; Walter et al., 2008). When the phage reversibly binds to its host using LTF, structural remodeling of the baseplate occurs, initiating the release of phage short‐tail fibers (STFs) that bind irreversibly to the host cell (Dunne et al., 2018). LTFs are complexes of several proteins with leg‐like shapes (Cerritelli et al., 1996). For instance, the Salmonella phage S16 LTF, which represents the structure of the majority of known T‐even phages, is composed of five distinct proteins, including an adhesin tip that serves the actual receptor‐binding function (Dunne et al., 2018).

(a) Long tail fiber proteins reversibly bind to outer membrane proteins or lipopolysaccharide (LPS), initiating the conformational rearrangement in the phage baseplate for irreversible attachment in the further absorption process. (b) Gram‐negative bacteria infecting phages utilize their tail spike proteins to identify and enzymatically hydrolyze the host receptor, facilitating their access to the receptor and the injection of phage DNA. (c) CBDs selectively bind to specific moieties within the bacterial cell wall, such as peptidoglycan or lipoteichoic acids, anchoring the entire endolysin to effectively degrade the murein layer and release their progeny virion particles. In contrast, TSPs play a crucial role in cell attachment and often recognize and hydrolyze polysaccharides in the bacterial cell envelope to facilitate DNA injection (Figure 2b). Once TSP reversibly binds to its bacterial receptor, normally LPS, it exhibits enzymatic activity (lyase, glycosidase, or esterase) toward the O‐antigen moiety within LPS, leading to irreversible binding of the phage to its target (Greenfield et al., 2020). Typically, TSP exhibits a homotrimeric structure, and this structure can be divided into three parts: a head binding domain at the N‐terminus, a parallel‐β‐helix domain containing right‐handed β‐helical turns in the middle, and a crucial trimerization part at the C‐terminus, which is also the determinant for the overall thermostability of TSP (Steinbacher et al., 1997; Vinga et al., 2012; Walter et al., 2008; Weigele et al., 2005). Although the structure of TSP remains largely conserved, variations exist in their receptor‐binding domains and host specificities (Nobrega et al., 2018).

As mentioned previously, phages produce endolysins to break down peptidoglycans, facilitating the release of virion particles. The CBDs of endolysins play a crucial role at this stage. They confer substrate specificity to the entire peptidoglycan hydrolase by recognizing and binding to the target moiety of the bacterial cell wall, such as peptidoglycan and wall teichoic acids (Figure 2c) (Eugster & Loessner, 2012; Heinemann & Sauer, 2011; Steen et al., 2003). This anchoring of the endolysin is not only essential for efficient targeted enzymatic action but also for protecting the surrounding microorganism flora from random, undesired lysis (Phothichaisri et al., 2022; Pohane et al., 2014; Schmelcher & Loessner, 2016). Taken together, these phage‐derived proteins with specific binding affinities have been used as novel detection agents for foodborne pathogenic bacteria in the food and agricultural industries. Here, we outline the applications of phage proteins (TFPs, TSPs, and CBDs) for the effective detection of foodborne pathogens (Table 3).

4.2 Applications of phage‐derived binding proteins for detection of foodborne pathogens

4.2.1 Phage‐derived binding proteins as capture agents

In the food industry, developing tools for rapid, sensitive, and precise confirmation of food contamination remains a primary focus for ensuring consumer safety and alleviating economic burdens. Because certain foodborne pathogens can induce severe symptoms even in small quantities, an enrichment step is necessary for sensitive detection of the target organism. The existing gold standard, a culture‐based enrichment assay, is time‐consuming and laborious. Hence, alternative approaches have been explored, focusing on the utilization of phage‐derived proteins with specific binding affinities as capture agents in various applications such as immunomagnetic separation (IMS), LFAs, and surface plasmon resonance (SPR). During the initial step of infection, phages explore their hosts by reversibly attaching to potential host targets, followed by irreversible binding through a lock‐and‐key mechanism using their tail proteins. Given the crucial role of phage adsorption in host recognition and identification, phage‐tail proteins naturally exhibit high specificity and sensitive binding to their target molecules. Similarly, the CBDs of phage endolysins participate in the selective lysis of host bacterial cells and possess the ability to distinguish target substrates in a highly distinct manner. Moreover, phage‐derived proteins can be easily produced using bacterial expression systems, thereby enhancing their applicability in various fields.

One innovative approach leverages phage‐derived host affinity molecules as capture agents on a magnetic separation platform. This method streamlines the enrichment of target pathogens in various samples, even large volumes, while ensuring reliable selectivity. Kretzer et al. (2007) conjugated CBDs from phages A118 and A500 (CBD118 and CBD500, respectively), each recognizing different LM serovars, to paramagnetic beads for the rapid concentration of LM cells across diverse serotypes. This CBD‐based immunomagnetic separation (CBD‐MS) method achieved an average detection level of 10 CFU/g in various food samples, including iceberg lettuce, smoked salmon, minced meat, turkey breast, and pasteurized milk, following a significantly reduced selective enrichment time of 6 h. In contrast, the traditional plating method, ISO 11290‐1, exhibited lower sensitivity for detecting LM in contaminated foods than CBD‐MS under identical conditions. This case exemplifies the successful application of CBDs in magnetic separation systems, providing a versatile and reliable tool for pathogen detection across various food matrices while reducing the time and complexity of the process. Similarly, Park et al. (2018) developed an ATP‐bioluminescence assay coupled with B. cereus‐specific LysPBC1_CBD‐based MS. They demonstrated highly selective detection of B. cereus, showing no cross‐reactivity with other tested pathogenic bacteria, including E. coli O157:H7, Salmonella enteritidis, and S. aureus, at a concentration of 105 CFU/mL. Furthermore, the detection limit of this method in blood samples was enhanced by 1 log CFU/mL by adding an enrichment step using CBD‐magnetic beads, and the overall luminescence was 1.44‐fold higher than that of Ab‐based IMS. This study demonstrated that the CBDs exhibited stronger binding affinities than did commercial Abs. Coupling CBD‐based MS with an ATP‐bioluminescence assay also enables the verification of viable pathogenic bacteria in samples, offering a more convenient alternative to traditional plating methods.

In addition to the CBDs, several studies have employed phage tail proteins as affinity molecules for IMS of gram‐negative pathogens. L. Wang, Lin et al. (2022) introduced a phage long tail fiber protein, LTF4‐a, from Salmonella phage STP4‐a for detecting Salmonella. They evaluated the capture efficiency of LTF4‐a‐conjugated magnetic nanoparticles (LTF4‐a), demonstrating recovery rates ranging from 97 ± 1% to 102 ± 3% for Salmonella cells at concentrations from 101 to 107 CFU/mL, with no observed cross‐reactivity with Enterobacteriaceae. Furthermore, they conducted quantitative PCR (qPCR) after LTF‐based IMS and demonstrated the capability of detecting as few as 7 CFU/mL of Salmonella cells in various food samples, including milk, romaine lettuce, and whole egg, within 3 h. Ding, Huang et al. (2023) and Denyes et al. (2017) developed an enzyme‐linked immunoassay coupled with a phage RBP‐incorporated IMS, which was designated as a magnetic microbead enzyme‐linked immunoassay (MELISA) and enzyme‐linked LTF assay (ELLTA), respectively. MELISA, based on the TSP RBP41 from Salmonella phage T102 as a capture molecule, showed the capability to separate and concentrate Salmonella cells in artificially contaminated food samples, including skim milk, lettuce, and chicken breast, with the limit of detection ranging from 1.0 ×10 to 1.25 ×105 CFU/mL within a total assay time of 1.5 h. Denyes et al. demonstrated that paramagnetic beads coated with recombinant gp37‐gp38 LTF complexes (LTF‐MBs) from Salmonella phage S16 could recover 10 CFU/25 g or mL of pre‐enriched food samples such as milk, chocolate milk, reconstituted infant formula, chicken, celery, and alfalfa sprouts. In addition, ELLTA using LTF‐MB showed a consistently high recovery rate of >90%, exclusively for the tested Salmonella strains, and was able to detect 102 CFU/mL of S. typhimurium cells within 2 h. These findings suggest the potential practical application of phage‐derived binding protein‐based IMS, with the advantages of high specificity and minimal cross‐reactivity. These studies confirmed the feasibility of using phage proteins in magnetic separation as reliable and effective capture probes for both Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative bacteria. Furthermore, they demonstrated the compatibility of these methods with detection techniques such as qPCR and ATP assays, highlighting the promising potential of phage proteins in pathogen detection and diagnostic applications.

As evidenced by IMS, phage RBPs or CBDs facilitate rapid and specific binding to targeted cells. Consequently, numerous efforts are currently underway to integrate these proteins into various biosensors for the selective capture of pathogens, such as LFA, SPR, and biolayer interferometry (BLI). Kong et al. (2017) devised a lateral‐flow detection platform employing the CBD from B. cereus phage B4 fused with a maltose‐binding protein as a capture probe (MBP‐CBD) and tagged with cysteine‐glutathione‐S‐transferase as a signal agent (GST‐CBD) in combination with gold nanoparticles. This platform exhibited a stronger detection signal when MBP‐CBD was used as a capture agent than when a commercial Ab was used, with a detection limit of 104 CFU/mL. Meanwhile, Pan et al. (2023) designed a lateral‐flow biosensor based on the tail fiber protein from LM‐specific phage A511. This assay showed no cross‐reactivity with E. coli O157:H7, Salmonella spp., and S. aureus cells, displaying high recovery rates for LM cells, ranging from 85.3% to 102.8%, in diverse food matrices, such as meat suspension, drinking water, and milk. These approaches highlight the simplicity and potential of these biosensors for in‐field verification of suspicious pathogens in food. A noteworthy example of the resilience of TSP to the inherent vulnerability of proteins under acidic conditions was demonstrated by Hyeon et al. (2021), who constructed an SPR detection platform using a TSP derived from Salmonella phage Det7. This platform detected 5 × 104 to 5 × 107 CFU/mL of S. typhimurium cells in buffer and 5 × 105 to 5 × 107 CFU/mL in 10% apple juice.

4.2.2 Phage‐derived binding proteins as detection probes

Generally, phage RBPs (monomeric) or CBDs are smaller than Abs. Therefore, it is easy to create recombinant proteins by fusing them with various functional tags, facilitating their application in detection fields. This approach involves the fusion of phage proteins with fluorescent or bioluminescent tags for spectroscopic identification of target pathogens. Cho et al. (2021) produced a recombinant CBD with an enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) tag for C. perfringens detection. This fusion protein (EGFP‐LysCPAS15_CBD1) was stable over a wide range of temperatures (−20 to 80°C) and pH values (2–12) and capable of binding to C. perfringens cells in milk samples within 5 min. Santos et al. (2020) developed a spectrofluorometric method using two distinct phage tail proteins for the multiplex detection of S. aureus and Enterococcus faecalis. They found that a GFP‐fused tail fiber protein from S. aureus phage vB_SauM_LM12 (GFP‐gp109) and an mCherry‐fused putative TSP from E. faecalis phage vB_EfaS_Max (mCherry‐gp18) could bind to S. aureus and E. faecalis cells, respectively, at a concentration of 108 CFU/mL. Besides, Farquharson et al. (2021) tested the adsorption capability of the T4 phage long‐tail fiber tip (gp37) against E. coli using a bioluminescence tag. In this study, T4 gp37 fusion with a bioluminescent tag (NLuc‐LTF) was used as a signaling agent, and vacuum filtration was applied to remove unbound Nluc‐LTF and concentrate the bacteria. This assay revealed that NLuc‐LTF successfully bound to 61 out of the 72 E. coli reference collections, producing highly sensitive luminescence signals.

Building on these examples, there are ongoing efforts to incorporate phage‐derived proteins as signaling markers on various platforms. Yu et al. (2016) designed an ELISA using CBD from S. aureus‐targeting endolysin PlyV12, which enabled the rapid and sensitive detection of S. aureus cells. They constructed a red fluorescent protein, tdTomato fusion CBD (T‐CBD), to enhance labeling efficiency with biotin. Biotinylated CBD was then combined with streptavidin‐labeled horseradish peroxidase (HRP) for the colorimetric detection of S. aureus. This approach required a total assay time of 1.5 h, with a detection limit of 4×103 CFU/mL in artificially contaminated milk. Additionally, Filik et al. (2022) developed an ELISA using the long tail fiber protein from phage ΦYeO3‐12 (TFP‐Gp17) as a reporter probe for the detection of pathogenic Yersinia enterocolitica O:3 (YeO:3). Yersinia cells were coated onto the surface of a microtiter plate and treated with TFP‐Gp17 containing MBP and a histidine tag (H/MTFP‐Gp17). The bound H/MTFP‐Gp17 was then detected using HRP‐conjugated anti‐His tag Abs, resulting in a detection limit of approximately 105 CFU/mL. Chen et al. (2023) performed indirect ELISA using three novel phage tail fiber proteins specific for STEC O91, O103, and O111. All three recombinant TFPs were constructed using a combination of a small ubiquitin‐like modifier (SUMO) tag and EGFP. By detecting the bound TFPs in STEC cells, this indirect ELISA was capable of detecting approximately 3.5 ×103 and 3.0–6.0 ×102 CFU/cm2 of STEC cells in lettuce and chicken breast models, respectively. Similarly, H. Yang, Xue et al. (2022) designed a sandwich fluorimetry method to detect MRSA and test its antibiotic susceptibility. Briefly, serially diluted MRSA cell aliquots were added to porcine IgG‐coated microplate wells, and a GFP‐fusion cell wall‐binding domain (GFP‐CWBD) from S. aureus phage P108 was applied after the unbound bacterial cells were removed. Finally, the fluorescence signal was analyzed after washing away the unbound GFP‐CWBD. To test the susceptibility of the MRSA strains, 105 CFU/mL MRSA aliquots were incubated with various concentrations of five different antibiotics prior to the addition of GFP‐CWBD. This fluorimetry method could detect MRSA cells in the range of 1.0×103 to 2.0×107 CFU/mL and showed no reactivity toward other foodborne pathogens tested, including LM, S. typhimurium, Shigella dysenteriae, Pseudomonas aureginosa, and Enterococcus faecium. The results of the fluorimetry‐based antibiotic susceptibility test correlated well with those of the conventional broth dilution method, indicating that the phage‐derived CBD‐based method offers reliable results. Meanwhile, Costa et al. (2020) employed GFP fusion CBD from S. aureus phage endolysin E‐LM12 (GFP‐AMI_SH3) as a recognition probe in flow cytometry for the rapid and sensitive detection of S. aureus cells in blood samples. The results demonstrated that this flow cytometry method could detect over 105 CFU/mL of S. aureus cells and was susceptible to other staphylococcal strains, but not to other non‐Staphylococcus strains. To confirm the detection feasibility, 1−5 CFU/mL S. aureus cells were inoculated into horse blood samples and enriched for 16 h. The inocula were then treated with water to deplete red blood cells, followed by centrifugation. Approximately 98% of S. aureus cells in the pretreated blood samples were successfully detected by flow cytometry using GFP‐AMI_SH3 as a labeling marker.

4.3 Limitations of phage‐derived binding proteins in food applications and approaches to overcome the challenges

Aside from the successful detection of target pathogens described in Section 4.2, the use of phage‐derived binding proteins has certain functional limitations. For example, the identification of CBD or RBPs within the vast array of phage genomic data is challenging and laborious. Fortunately, the growing volume of phage genome data has led to the development of genomic databases beyond the NCBI GenBank and EMBL‐EBI as well as novel prediction tools for identifying putative phage RBP sequences (Boeckaerts et al., 2022; Charoenkwan et al., 2020; Cook et al., 2021; Rolland et al., 2024; R. H. Wang, Yang, et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2023). Generally, phage RBPs comprise structural domains at the N‐terminus and functional domains at the C‐terminus, such as those involved in degradation, binding, and protein folding (Boeckaerts et al., 2022). Based on this information, they combined known phage RBP‐related hidden Markov models (HMMs) from the Pfam database with custom‐built HMMs to identify phage tail proteins. They also introduced a machine learning‐based classifier to accurately distinguish between RBPs and other phage proteins. Similarly, Zhou et al. (2023) developed a predictive algorithm for phage tail‐related proteins named “PhageTailFinder,” based on a two‐state HMM that considers the modularity of the given protein. Testing PhageTailFinder on 13 well‐studied phage genomes and 992 complete phage genomes from the NCBI database demonstrated a high true‐positive prediction rate of over 80% for more than half of the tested phages. Furthermore, when applied to the 189,680 viral genomes from human stool samples, PhageTailFinder maintained reliable accuracy and specificity in predicting phage tail proteins. Both methods complemented each other, and their best‐performing models outperformed currently available phage structural protein classification tools.

Another concern is that the CBDs of phage endolysins, the most extensively explored phage‐derived binding proteins, have only been used to detect Gram‐positive bacteria. As the peptidoglycans of Gram‐positive bacteria are exposed externally, CBD can identify and attach to its cognitive target when administered exogenously. In contrast, most phage endolysins targeting Gram‐negative hosts have a globular structure consisting of a single EAD, offering fewer opportunities for CBD acquisition (Love et al., 2020). Although certain endolysins originating from Gram‐negative host phages feature a CBD module, the OM remains a significant barrier, hindering the practical application of CBDs for Gram‐negative pathogen detection. In this case, TSPs or (long) TFPs could be attractive options because they typically target the outermost layer of bacterial cells (Ouyang et al., 2024). One possible problem is obtaining aqueously soluble phage tail proteins, because these proteins are usually large and form homotrimers, resulting in aggregation (Speed et al., 1997). To produce soluble fractions, several groups have truncated the amino‐terminal regions of the tail proteins responsible for anchoring them to the phage (head or tail) (Braun et al., 2020; Steinbacher et al., 1996; Walter et al., 2008). A more rational approach involves the fusion of a highly soluble protein with the target protein, thereby improving the solubility of the entire protein complex. Y. Zhang, Li et al. (2022) attempted to fuse the tail fiber protein from a Pseudomonas aeruginosa‐infecting phage with a SUMO tag to create a recombinant soluble tail fiber protein (STFP). STFP was evenly distributed onto nitrocellulose membrane paper and effectively worked as a capture agent, enabling the detection of P. aeruginosa cells in the range of 3.3 × 102 to 3.3 × 107 CFU/mL on the LFA platform.

During phage infection, TSPs occasionally exhibit enzymatic activity and degrade their receptors to facilitate host infection (Greenfield et al., 2019; Singh et al., 2010; Xu et al., 2016). Therefore, the enzymatic activity of TSP must be disabled to ensure that it can bind to the target receptor without degradation, enabling its application for detection purposes. This process involves identifying the active‐site residues within TSPs and experimentally determining the optimal mutations that eliminate enzymatic activity while retaining the binding capability. Some studies have shown that these active‐site mutants of TSPs can effectively detect target pathogens in a highly distinct manner. Kunstmann et al. (2018) introduced enzymatically inactivated TSP from phage Sf6 in an ELISA‐like tail spike absorption assay (ELITA) as a capture probe for the specific detection of Shigella flexneri. This ELITA method exclusively detected Shigella strains without cross‐reactivity with Salmonella and E. coli strains. Likewise, Lee et al. (2020) analyzed the binding feasibility of catalytically inactivated phiAB6TSP (I‐ΦAB6TSP) specific for A. baumannii. Fluorescence dye‐labeled I‐ΦAB6TSP was capable of binding to A. baumannii strain 54149 (Ab‐54149) with higher sensitivity than an Ab purified from anti‐Ab‐54149 EPS serum. Alternatively, another method leverages the selective lytic properties of phage proteins to detect foodborne pathogens. Vercauteren et al. (2023) devised an indirect detection technique for B. cereus using a porous silicon membrane‐based biosensor combined with a B. cereus‐specific phage endolysin. In the presence of target bacteria, endolysin selectively lyses them, allowing their lysates to penetrate the porous silicon membrane, which alters its optical characteristics. Conversely, non‐targeted bacteria accumulate on top of the sensor without affecting its optical properties. This indirect biosensor, which relies on phage bactericidal proteins, can detect up to 103 CFU/mL of B. cereus within 1 h 30 min.

The limited binding spectrum of phage RBPs and CBDs may impede their practical applications. This challenge can be overcome using protein engineering methods. Schmelcher et al. (2011) developed chimeric CBDs by linking different or identical CBDs, which resulted in a broadened binding range and increased binding affinity. Another approach for modifying the binding specificity of phage receptor‐binding modules within tail proteins is targeted or structure‐guided mutagenesis. Yehl et al. (2019) generated various synthetic “phagebodies” with diversified tail fiber proteins using E. coli phage T3 as a model system to create phages with altered host ranges. They confirmed that a mutation in the outward loops located at the C‐terminus of the T3 tail fiber protein (gp 17) resulted in a broadened host range. A more strategic approach is structure‐guided mutagenesis (Dunne et al., 2019). They created phage mutants with varied TSPs using listerial prophage PSA, which infects only a single serovar, SV 4b Listeria. By employing error‐prone PCR to generate random mutations in the PSA TSP (Gp15), the resulting sequences were assembled and transfected into Listeria L‐forms, producing six mutants with single point mutations that changed the host specificity from SV 4b to SV 4d. Next, they constructed a chimeric PSA harboring two different Gp15 proteins, native Gp15 and a mutant Gp15 with S334R substitution, expanding its host range to both SV 4b and 4d. Structural analysis of Gp15 has shown that mutations in the C‐terminal head domain alter host specificity. Aligning Gp15 with other prophage RBPs revealed conserved neck and stem sequences but high variability in the head domain. Using these data, they engineered phages with altered head domains to target different Listeria serovars, demonstrating that phage RBPs could be tailored to specific host‐binding properties through mutagenesis.

As phage‐derived binding proteins target surface ligands on pathogens, they cannot distinguish between live and dead cells, resulting in false‐positive results in thermally processed foods. This challenge can be addressed by integrating phage proteins into diverse platforms. For instance, combining CBD‐based IMS with a reporter phage enabled the detection of 102 CFU/mL of viable Listeria cells in pure culture within 6 h (Kretzer et al., 2018). For contaminated food samples, a pre‐enrichment step was included, resulting in highly sensitive detection (0.1–1.0 CFU/g of viable cells) within a total assay time of less than 22 h. Alternatively, Liu et al. (2022) explored the detection capabilities of a BLI method based on a mutant phage endolysin. They observed that the lytic activity of the S. aureus phage endolysin LysGH15 was compromised by the C54A mutation. They established a BLI method using the C54A mutant LysGH15 as the initial binding agent and native LysGH15 as the secondary detection probe. This method demonstrated a distinct pattern, wherein the peak initially increased in the presence of viable S. aureus cells in the sample, followed by a subsequent decrease in the second binding with native endolysin. In contrast, for dead cells, the peak continued to increase, allowing for differentiation between viable and dead cells. Moreover, this approach successfully detected S. aureus within 12 min, with recovery rates of 99.43% and 104.6% in light soy sauce and ice cube samples, respectively. These results indicate that phage proteins can be incorporated into diverse platforms for rapid and specific detection of viable bacterial cells.

Finally, the selection of an appropriate platform for the introduction of phage proteins is crucial for successful pathogen detection. As demonstrated in previous studies (Cho et al., 2021; Filik et al., 2022; Santos et al., 2020), fluorescence labeling often exhibits relatively low sensitivity for detecting target bacteria. This can be attributed to several factors, including high background noise that compromises detection accuracy, as well as the attenuation of fluorescence signals because of photobleaching, pH changes, or autofluorescence from bacteria (Bhowmik et al., 2024; Kwon et al., 2022; Le Guern et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021; Surre et al., 2018; Ticer et al., 2024). Furthermore, the abundant natural fluorescence within food matrices complicates detection as it may interfere with specific signals from labeled bacteria (Radotic et al., 2023; Souroullas et al., 2024). As high‐risk foodborne pathogens, such as Listeria spp., Salmonella spp., and STEC, can cause acute infections even in very small quantities (Hunt, 2010; Liustrovaite et al., 2023; Teunis, 2022), establishing rigorous minimum detection limits is crucial for expanding the applicability of these methods in the food industry. Ideally, the detection platforms should reliably identify these bacteria at concentrations of 100 CFU/mL or lower to ensure safety. To achieve this, selective enrichment or concentration of target organisms from large food samples is critical, an area where phage proteins can be effectively utilized for capture and separation prior to detection.

5 CONCLUDING REMARKS

Despite advances in modern technologies, foodborne pathogens persistently threaten food safety. The overuse and misuse of antibiotics have worsened this problem owing to the prevalence of multidrug‐resistant bacteria. Although rapid bacterial detection is essential for preventing outbreaks and mitigating the risk of product recall, time‐consuming culture‐based methods are still generally regarded as the “gold standard” for detecting bacteria in food. Therefore, the development of alternative antimicrobials and rapid diagnostic tools is crucial to ensure food safety. Bacteriophages are promising agents for biocontrol and detection, but they face challenges such as the emergence of phage‐resistant cells, narrow host ranges, and the potential transduction of harmful genes. In diagnostic applications, the limited binding spectrum, the large size of whole phage particles, and phage‐induced lysis may also present bottlenecks.

With recent advances in next‐generation sequencing technology, numerous bacteriophage proteins with biocontrol and detection capabilities have been identified. Among them, phage endolysins and tail proteins exhibit potent degradation activity and binding specificity, making them excellent candidates to replace conventional antibiotics and recognition elements (e.g., Abs). In addition, their proteinaceous nature and straightforward mechanisms alleviate concerns related to the regulatory complexities and phage‐induced transduction, positioning phage‐derived proteins as practical and promising alternatives to intact bacteriophages to address food safety challenges.

The studies presented herein introduce diverse applications of phage endolysins and tail proteins for food safety, highlighting their dual potential for both biocontrol and detection of nine major foodborne pathogens. Although numerous studies have confirmed the antimicrobial efficacies of phage proteins in food matrices (e.g., dairy, meat, fresh produce, and seafood), on contact surfaces (biofilms), and for treating infections in livestock, several issues should be carefully addressed before applying these proteins in the food industry. First, phage enzymes must endure high temperatures during food processing and harsh storage conditions. In addition to identifying novel, thermostable phage proteins, further protein engineering studies, such as truncation, domain swapping, or mutagenesis, are needed to enhance their stability and activity. Second, efficient expression and purification systems should be developed to reduce production costs and minimize potential in vivo toxicity. Given the previous GRAS certification of plant‐derived endolysins, studies focused on producing phage proteins using edible hosts (e.g., plant, probiotics, or yeast) are highly recommended. Third, for treating farm animals, in‐depth pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity studies are essential to address the instability and short half‐life of phage proteins.

Compared to sufficient reports evaluating the antimicrobial activity of bacteriophages and their lytic enzymes (especially endolysins) in foods, diagnostic studies using phage proteins are mostly at the proof‐of‐concept stage, detecting bacterial cells in simple buffer or distilled water. Considering that the complex matrices of food products may reduce the efficacy of phage proteins, further studies are required to validate the binding activity of phage proteins in various food samples. In addition to approaches focused on obtaining novel phage proteins with high affinity and binding specificity, the selective enrichment and concentration of target bacteria using phage proteins from large food samples are essential for improving detection limits and enabling practical applications in the food industry. With the plethora of phage genome sequencing and engineering techniques, phage proteins are expected to boost the development of new antimicrobial/diagnostic agents to prevent foodborne illnesses caused by pathogenic bacteria.
