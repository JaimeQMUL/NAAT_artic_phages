==============================
In this study, nanosponges (NSs) were synthesized using different molar ratios of β-cyclodextrin and diphenyl carbonate via the melting method. Gallic acid (GA) was encapsulated into the NSs at three different concentrations, and the optimal formulation showed high encapsulation efficiency, loading capacity, and enhanced solubility. The GA-loaded NSs were characterized using various physicochemical analyses. Cytotoxicity tests indicated that encapsulated GA exhibited lower toxicity than free GA in both normal and tumor cells, while showing approperiate antibacterial activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. The cumulative release of GA in simulated grape juice demonstrated a controlled release profile. Grape juice enriched with GA-loaded NSs maintained higher anthocyanin content, total phenolic compounds, and antioxidant capacity during 30 days of storage compared with samples containing free GA. Overall, this study highlights NSs-based encapsulation as a promising strategy to enhance the solubility, stability, and sustained release of poorly soluble bioactive compounds such as GA.

Highlights

• Gallic acid (GA) loaded NSs increased aqueous solubility up to 64-folds.

• GA loaded NSs showed selective cytotoxicity against MCF7 cells.

• Gram-positive bacteria were more sensitive to GA than Gram-negative strains.

• GA release from NSs depended on the simulated medium.

• GA loaded NSs improved grape juice stability during storage.

1 Introduction

Grape juice is a natural beverage rich in antioxidants, particularly flavonoids such as anthocyanins and resveratrol, which are associated with cardiovascular protection and anti-inflammatory effects. It is widely consumed for its refreshing taste and potential health benefits, including improved blood circulation and enhanced immune function. In addition, grape juice includes essential vitamins such as vitamin C, vitamin K, and B vitamins. In red and purple varieties, the presence of polyphenols contributes to protective effects against oxidative stress (Li & Padilla-Zakour, 2024). However, a major challenge in anthocyanin-based beverages industry during processing and storage is the color instability, which ultimately reduces product quality and marketability. Therefore, improving anthocyanins stability is of critical importance. One promising strategy involves the use of polyphenolic compounds such as gallic acid, ferulic acid, caffeic acid, and sinapic acid as co-pigments. These compounds interact with anthocyanins through co-pigmentation mechanisms, thereby enhancing their stability during processing and storage (Zhang et al., 2020).

Gallic acid (GA, 3,4,5-trihydroxybenzoic acid) is a phenolic acids recognized for its antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties (Tajfiroozeh et al., 2023). However, its low solubility results in limited bioavailability. To overcome this limitation, nanosponges (NSs) have been proposed as suitable carriers to improve the solubility of GA. In a related study, Zhang et al. (2020) used different molar ratios of GA and anthocyanin to enhance the color and stability of anthocyanins in blueberry juice.

NSs are solid colloidal structures that contain a three-dimensional (3D) network or scaffold with nanosized cavities. Various types of NSs include cyclodextrin-based (CD-NS), titanium-based, silicon-based, hyper-cross-linked polystyrene, and carbon-coated metallic NSs are already produced (Patel & Oswal, 2012). Among these, CD-NSs are particularly relevant in the food industry, serving as drug carriers, supports for enzyme immobilization, and for the removal of dyes or other contaminants from water and wastewater (Pant & Bhattacharya, 2024). CDs possess a truncated cone-like shape characterized by a hydrophobic inner cavity and a hydrophilic exterior surface, enabling the formation of inclusion complexes with a broad range of compounds through host-guest interactions (Sezen et al., 2025). CD-NSs are commonly synthesized by reacting CDs with cross-linking agents such as epichlorohydrin, carbonyl diimidazole, diphenyl carbonate, or pyromellitic anhydride. The most common CDs, referred to as α-cyclodextrin, β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) and γ-cyclodextrin, contain six, seven, and eight glucopyranose units, respectively (Pant & Bhattacharya, 2024). Among the natural CDs, β-CD is widely used for NSs preparation because of its high complex-forming potential, stability with cross-linking agents, and cost-effectiveness (Pawar et al., 2019). CD-based NSs offer several advantages: (i) stability over a wide pH range (1–11) and at temperatures up to 130 °C; (ii) reusability after treatments with environmentally friendly solvents; (iii) the ability to exist in crystalline or amorphous forms; and (iv) applicability in the encapsulation of bioactive compounds, as well as in converting liquids into solid powders (Patil et al., 2017; Tejashri et al., 2013). Many researchers have employed NSs as carriers to encapsulate and deliver biomolecules, thereby improving the stability and solubility of compounds. For example, Pant and Bhattacharya (2024) encapsulated trans-cinnamaldehyde in β-CD-NSs and proposed this system for active food packaging applications in the form of films, coatings, or sachets. Similarly, Salehi et al. (2021) employed β-CD-NSs as carriers to enhance the solubility of d-limonene. Despite previous studies on β-CD-NSs, their application for enriching fruit juices with phenolic compounds, particularly GA, has been scarcely explored. To address this gap, GA was encapsulated in a β-CD-NS system to enhance its solubility and stability, as well as to achieve a controlled release. The GA-loaded β-CD-NSs were then characterized, and finally incorporated into grape juice to evaluate its physicochemical properties during storage.

2 Materials and methods

2.1 Materials

β-CD, diphenyl carbonate (DPC) and 1,10-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH)) were obtained from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). GA (˃ 98% purity) was purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), while the Folin-Ciocalteau reagent was sourced from Solarbio (Beijin, China). Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB), Muller Hinton Agar (MHA), and Muller Hinton Broth (MHB) were obtained from Quelab (Montreal, Canada). The bacterial strains used in this study included Escherichia coli (ATCC:35150), Salmonella typhimurium (ATCC:14028), Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC:25923), and Bacillus cereus (ATCC:10987), which were supplied by the Pasteur Institute of Iran. All other chemicals and reagents employed in this research were of analytical-grade purity.

2.2 Synthesis of β-CD-NSs

The synthesis of β-CD-NS was performed according to Silva et al. (2019), using DPC as a cross-linking agent at various molar ratios of β-CD to DPC (1:2, 1:4, and 1:6). Initially, DPC was melted at 90 °C, after which CD was gradually added while stirring the mixture at the same temperature. The reaction was allowed to proceed overnight to ensure complete cross-linking. The resulting product was then thoroughly washed with distilled water and ethanol using a Buchner filtration setup to remove any unreacted β-CD and DPC. Further purification was carried out by Soxhlet extraction with ethanol for 24 h. Finally, the cross-linked NSs were air-dried and stored at 25 °C for subsequent applications.

2.3 Preparation of GA loaded NSs

GA loading into the NSs was carried out based on the method developed by Swaminathan et al. (2010) with some modifications. Briefly, 200 mg of NS was dispersed in 10 mL of distilled water, followed by the addition of GA at weight ratios (GA: NS) of 1:1, 2:1, and 3:1. The obtained mixture was continuously stirred for 24 h (200 rpm) at room temperature under dark conditions. Afterwards, the samples were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature to separate any unencapsulated GA. The resulting supernatant was collected and freeze-dried at −50 °C for 24 h.

2.4 Encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading capacity (LC)

The EE and LC for GA-loaded NSs were determined according to Kurd et al. (2019), with slight modifications. Briefly, 10 mg of GA-loaded NSs were dispersed in 10 m L of methanol and agitated for 5 min to remove any GA adsorbed on the surface. The suspension was subsequently filtered using a 0.45 μm syringe filter, and the absorbance of the filtrate was analyzed at 270 nm using a UV spectrophotometer (Hanon, China). EE and LC of samples were determined using Eqs. (1), (2).(1) EE=W1−W2W1×100

(2) LC=W1−W2W3×100

where W1 represents the total weight of GA, W2 denotes the weight of non-capsulated GA and W3 refers to the total weight of GA loaded NSs.

2.5 Measurement of solubility

To assess the solubility of GA, 25 mg of GA-loaded NSs and free GA (microencapsulated equivalent) were each dispersed in 10 mL of distilled water and stirred separately at room temperature for 24 h at 100 rpm. Following incubation, the suspensions were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 25 °C. The resulting supernatants were filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter, and their absorbance was recorded at 270 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Hanon, China) (Salehi et al., 2021).

2.6 Morphology, particle size, and zeta potential

The surface characteristics of the NSs were analyzed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM) (QUANTA FEG-450, FEI Company, USA) operated at an accelerating voltage of 15.0 kV. Before imaging, a thin layer of gold was applied to the samples under high-vacuum for 120 s to reduce charging effects during observation.

The particle size and zeta potential of the samples were determined using the dynamic light scattering (DLS) technique with a Zeta sizer Pro (Malvern Panalytical, UK). For the analysis, dry samples were dispersed in distilled water at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and sonicated for 5 min at 200 W. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

2.7 Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR was performed to evaluate the functional group and potential chemical interactions between the ingredients. The sample powders were combined with KBr and pressed into pellets under high pressure. FTIR spectra were recorded in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 using an FTIR spectrometer (MB100, Bomem Hartmann & Braun, Quebec, Canada) at a resolution of 4 cm−1.

2.8 X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD analysis of samples was conducted using an X-ray diffractometer (AW-XDM300, China). The XRD measurements were conducted within a 2θ range of 10° to 60° at a scanning rate of 2°/min. The relative crystallinity was determined by calculating the ratio of the crystalline peak area to the overall diffraction area using Origin software.

2.9 Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The thermal characteristics of NSs, GA, and GA-loaded NSs were evaluated using DSC (DSCI/500, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). The analysis was carried out at 25–600 °C and an empty aluminum pan was used as a reference standard to ensure accurate baseline correction. Approximately 5 mg of each sample was placed in a sealed aluminum pan, followed by analysis under a constant nitrogen purge at a flow rate of 20 mL/min with a heating rate of 10 °C/min.

2.10 Release and study of the GA release kinetic

The release pattern of GA-loaded NSs was evaluated under three conditions: simulated gastric (pH = 1.2), intestinal (pH = 6.8), and grape juice media (pH = 4). To this end, 10 mg of GA-loaded NSs were dispersed in 5 mL of distilled water and placed into a dialysis bag. The bag was initially submerged in 50 mL of simulated gastric buffer solution (HCl and NaCl) and shaken at 100 rpm for 2 h. It was then immediately transferred to 50 mL of simulated intestinal buffer (K₂HPO₄ and NaOH) and maintained for 6 h at 37 °C under shaking. GA release was further measured over 30 days into the simulated grape juice buffer (CH3COOH and CH3COONa). At each predetermined interval, 3 mL of the release solution was taken out and substituted with an equivalent volume of fresh buffer to ensure a constant overall volume. The cumulative percentage of GA released was calculated using Eq. (3):(3) Cumulative release%=CiVt+Vs∑2nCi−1Wt×100

where cᵢ is the GA concentration at time point i (1 to n), Vₜ is the total volume of the release medium, Vₛ is the volume of sampled medium (3 mL), and Wₜ is the total amount of GA in the NSs (Baniasadi et al., 2021). The obtained release results were modeled to four commonly used kinetic models: zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer–Peppas.

2.11 Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

The MIC of free GA and GA-loaded NSs against Escherichia coli, Salmonella typhimurium, Staphylococcus aureus, and Bacillus cereus was determined using the broth microdilution method in 96-well microtiter plates. GA and GA- loaded NSs were initially dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and deionized water, respectively. Then, 100 μL of each solution was poured to the first well of each row as the stock solution. Subsequently, 100 μL of MHB was introduced into all wells. Following this, 10 μL of bacterial suspension (106 CFU/mL) was introduced into each well.

After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the MIC value was determined as the minimum concentration of the tested sample that completely inhibited bacterial proliferation, confirmed by the absence of turbidity in the culture medium. To validate the results, the penultimate well in each row was used as the positive control (containing bacterial suspension and MHB), and the final well served as the negative control (containing MHB and the test compound).

After determination of MIC, 10 μL from the well without visible turbidity was transferred onto MHA and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The MBC was determined as the minimum concentration of the antimicrobial compound capable of reducing bacterial growth by 99.9% (Tian et al., 2022).

2.12 Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxic effects of free GA and GA-loaded NSs on tumor cell line (MCF7) and normal cell line (L929) were evaluated using the MTT colorimetric assay (Dhakar et al., 2019). Briefly, L929 and MCF7cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a seeding density of 1 × 104 cells per well and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂ for 24 h to facilitate cell adhesion. Afterward, 100 μL of various concentrations (16–1000 μg/mL) of free GA and GA-loaded NSs were added to the wells, followed by incubation for 24 h. Following treatment, the medium in each well was substituted with 100 μL of MTT solution (prepared at 0.5 mg/mL in serum-free medium), and the plates were then incubated for 4 h at 37 °C to enable the formation of purple formazan crystals by viable, metabolically active cells. Then, the MTT solution was aspirated, and 100 μL of DMSO was introduced into each well to dissolve the formazan crystals. The plates were gently shaken and incubated for an additional 15 min at room temperature. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 570 nm employing an ELISA reader, and cell viability (%) was calculated according to Eq. (4).(4) Cell viability%=ODof SampleODof Control×100

2.13 Grape juice preparation and treatments

Fresh grapes were obtained from a local market, thoroughly washed with tap water, and surface moisture was air-dried. The juice was extracted using an electric juicer. Different concentrations of free GA and GA-loaded NSs were added to the freshly prepared grape juice and mixed thoroughly at room temperature. The prepared samples were named as follows: free GA (GA1: 125 mg/L; GA2: 250 mg/L; GA3: 500 mg/L), and GA loaded NS (GA-NS1: 125 mg/L; GA-NS2: 250 mg/L; GA-NS3: 500 mg/L). The treated samples were then stored at 4 °C for subsequent evaluation of their physicochemical properties.

2.13.1 Measurement of total anthocyanin content (TAC)

The TAC of the samples was quantified using the pH differential method by Gamarra-Castillo et al. (2025). Briefly, the samples were diluted with two buffer solutions: potassium chloride buffer (pH 1.0) and sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.5). After a 15 min, the absorbance (A) of each solution was recorded at 500 nm and 700 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Hanon, China). The monomeric anthocyanin levels were calculated and presented as mg of cyanidin-3-glucoside (C3G) per 100 g of sample. The TAC was reported in mg/L as cyanidin-3-glucoside equivalents.(5) A=A500nm−A700nmpH1−A500nm−A700nmpH4.5

(6) TAC=A×MW×DF×103/ε×L

where MW refers to the molecular weight of C3G (449.2 gmol−1), DF denotes the dilution factor, ε indicates the molar extinction coefficient (26,900 Lmol−1 cm−1), and L corresponds to the cuvette path length (1 cm).

2.13.2 Total phenolic compounds (TPC)

The TPC of the samples was assessed according to the method of Li and Padilla-Zakour (2024) with slight modifications. To minimize interference from high sugar content, grape juice samples were diluted with distilled water to obtain various concentrations (0.02–0.10% v/v). Then, 100 μL of each diluted sample was mixed with 1 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (pre-diluted 10-fold) and 800 μL of sodium carbonate solution (7.5% w/v) to form a uniform solution. After incubation in the dark for 30 min, the absorbance was read at 765 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Hanon, China). A standard calibration curve was constructed using GA at known concentrations, and the TPC was expressed as milligrams of GA equivalents (mg GA/100 mL) of grape juice.

2.13.3 Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity of samples was assessed using the DPPH free radical scavenging assay (Chen et al., 2024). In this method, 10 mg of the sample was dissolved in 10 mL of 96% ethanol and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, 500 μL of the prepared solution was combined with 3 mL of 0.1 mM DPPH solution and allowed to react in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance of the mixtures was read at 516 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Hanon, China). The radical scavenging activity was quantified as the following equation:(7) DPPH Scavenging%=Acontrol−Asample/Acontrol×100

where A denotes the absorbance values of the sample and the DPPH control, respectively.

2.14 Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed statistically using SAS software, with results reported as mean ± standard deviation. Group differences were assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by the LSD test at a 95% confidence level (p < 0.05). All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

3.2 Morphological, particle size and zeta potential

The morphology of GA loading NSs was displayed by FESEM (Fig. 2a and b). The NSs exhibited a cubic and crystalline structure with relatively smooth and compact surfaces. This suggests that the NSs possess a dense polymeric network, likely resulting from the cross-linking reaction between β-CD and DPC. The compact surface morphology may contribute to the stability of the nanostructure and efficient encapsulation of GA.Fig. 2 (a and b) FESEM images of gallic acid (GA) loading nanosponges (NSs); and (c and d) particle size and zeta potential NS and GA-NSs.

Fig. 2

The particle size and zeta potential of the NSs and GA-loaded NSs are shown in Fig. 2c and d. The average hydrodynamic diameter ranged from approximately 67.90 ± 0.55 nm to 74.46 ± 1.11 nm. The hydrodynamic diameter increased with GA encapsulation. Sezen et al. (2025) confirmed that the incorporation of doxorubicin into NSs increased the hydrodynamic diameter of the CD-NSs.

This nanoscale size confirms the successful formation of well-dispersed and non-aggregated NSs in solution. The small particle size is advantageous for enhancing solubility, bioavailability, and potential cellular uptake. It also indicates that the cross-linking density was optimal to avoid excessive agglomeration or pore collapse, which can occur with overly rigid or high-density cross-linked structures (Sezen et al., 2025).

Zeta potential measurements revealed no significant difference between blank NSs and those loaded with GA, indicating that the incorporation of GA did not alter the surface charge of the particles. This suggests that GA is predominantly encapsulated within the internal cavities of the β-CD network, rather than adsorbed onto the outer surface, leaving the ionizable groups responsible for zeta potential largely unchanged. Stable zeta potential values in both formats further imply good colloidal stability critical for preventing aggregation and maintaining uniform dispersion in aqueous solutions.

3.3 FTIR

Fig. 3a shows the absorption bands of NSs, free GA and GA-loaded NSs. The characteristic band of NSs at 1650 cm−1 corresponds to the carbonate groups, validating the formation of carbonate bonds between two CD monomers (Salehi et al., 2021). GA exhibited its main characteristic bands at 3498 and 3282 cm−1 (hydroxyl stretching vibrations), which disappeared in the spectrum of GA-loaded NSs. This observation was also described by Karaman (2021) for GA-loaded yeast cells. Additionally, GA showed bands at 1700 cm−1 (CO stretching of carbonyl groups), 1617 cm−1 (aromatic ring stretching), and at 1383 cm−1 (–CH– bending of the benzene ring) (Salamat et al., 2024).Fig. 3 (a) FTIR spectra, (b) XRD diffraction patterns, and (c) DSC of nanosponges (NSs), gallic acid (GA), GA loaded NSs.

Fig. 3

In the spectrum of GA-loaded NSs, hydrogen bonding interactions between GA and NSs is observed (Leyva-Jiménez et al., 2024). However, the intensity of GA bands at 1700 and 1617 cm−1 decreased upon encapsulation, indicating the successful incorporation of GA into the NSs. Mashaqbeh et al. (2021) reported that the absence of curcumin characteristic bands in the NS complex can confirmed the successful formation of the host-guest inclusion adduct.

3.4 XRD

Fig. 3b illustrates the XRD patterns of NSs, free GA, and GA-loaded NSs. The NS XRD pattern displayed a crystalline structure, which seems be due to cross-linking β-CD with DPC. This cross-linking can induce ordered regions within the otherwise amorphous polymeric network, resulting in sharp or semi-sharp diffraction peaks (Swaminathan et al., 2010; Trotta et al., 2012). Free GA exhibited sharp and intense diffraction peaks at 2θ values of approximately 11.9°, 16.1°, and 20.6°, indicating its highly crystalline nature. However, upon incorporation of GA into the NSs, these characteristic peaks significantly decreased in intensity or completely disappeared in the XRD pattern of GA-loaded NSs. Instead, a broad hump or paracrystalline pattern was observed, suggesting a transition from a crystalline to an amorphous state (confirmed by solubility). Aytac et al. (2016) reported similar XRD results of GA microencapsulation.

This transformation is typically associated with the successful encapsulation of a crystalline drug or bioactive material into a polymeric carrier system, leading to amorphization of the guest molecule. The absence or reduction of GA's crystalline peaks suggests that GA molecules were molecularly dispersed within the NS matrix, likely through the formation of an inclusion complex or strong intermolecular interactions such as hydrogen bonding (Mihailiasa et al., 2016; Rao et al., 2018). Amorphization is a desirable outcome in many delivery systems, as it can enhance the solubility, dissolution rate, and bioavailability of poorly water-soluble compounds like GA. This confirms that the formulation has undergone amorphization, indicating the formation of an inclusion complex (Mihailiasa et al., 2016). Similar results were reported that microencapsulation of cinnamon essential oil in NSs led to peak broadening and the absence of additional peaks in the XRD pattern, indicating inclusion complex formation associated with amorphization (Pant & Bhattacharya, 2024).

3.5 DSC analysis

The DSC thermograms of NS, GA, and GA-NS are shown in Fig. 3c. None of the DSC thermograms showed solvent-related endothermic peaks, confirming that organic solvents had been completely removed. The DSC thermogram of β-CD-NS exhibited an endothermic peak at 280 °C, indicating the melting of anhydrous crystals and the β-CD polymer point, consistent with previous reports (Sangwan & Hooda, 2025). Pure GA exhibited a sharp endothermic peak at approximately 300 °C, which is characteristic of its melting point and thus confirms its crystalline nature (Neo et al., 2013). In contrast, the DSC thermogram of GA-NSs revealed a glass transition temperature (Tg) at 156 °C, followed by the onset of thermal degradation at around 200 °C. Importantly, the thermogram of GA-NSs did not display the melting peak associated with GA, suggesting that the encapsulated compound no longer retained its original crystalline form. This loss of crystallinity indicates that GA was molecularly dispersed or amorphized within the nanosystem during the formulation process, a transformation that can enhance its solubility and bioavailability. These observations are in consistent with findings previously described in the literature Mashaqbeh et al. (2021).

3.6 In vitro cytotoxicity

In this study, one normal cell line (L929) and one tumor cell line (MCF7) were selected to compare the cytotoxicity of GA and GA-loaded NS at concentrations ranging from 16 to 1000 μg/mL (Fig. 4a and b). According to the results, the cell viability of all samples decreased with increasing concentrations after 24 h. No significant difference in cell viability was observed for either L929 or MCF7cells treated with free GA or GA-loaded NS at concentrations up to 250 and 64 μg/mL (P > 0.05), respectively. At a concentration of 1000 μg/mL after 24 h, the lowest cell viability observed for free GA was 29.8% in L929 and 27.6% in MCF7 cells. In contrast, GA-loaded NS under the same conditions showed minimum cell viability of approximately 56% and 62.2% for L929 and MCF7 cells, respectively. Additionally, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for free GA was determined to be around 250 μg/mL for both cell lines. For GA-loaded NS, the IC50 values for both cell lines were greater than free GA that indicates its significantly reduced overall cytotoxicity. Moreover, the lower IC50 value for MCF7 cells compared to L929 cells suggests a slightly more potent effect on the cancer cell line. Previous studies have demonstrated that free phenolic compounds at high concentrations can induce oxidative damage and act as pro-oxidants (Maurya & Devasagayam, 2010). Comparable results were documented by Gholibegloo et al. (2019), who found that the cytotoxicity of free curcumin was higher than that of curcumin loaded NS. 3.7 Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

GA has been demonstrated to have a significant role in food preservation by inhibiting the growth of a wide range of bacterial species. Its antibacterial efficacy is mediated through several mechanisms, including the disruption of bacterial cell membrane integrity, interference with essential metabolic pathways, and inhibition of biofilm formation, all of which contribute to reduced bacterial viability and proliferation (Rahmawati et al., 2024). The MIC and MBC values of both free GA and GA-loaded NSs against Escherichia coli, Salmonella typhimurium (Gram-negative), Staphylococcus aureus, and Bacillus cereus (Gram-positive) are summarized in Table 2. Free GA exhibited a consistent MIC of 5 mg/mL and 1.80 for Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial, respectively. However, GA- loaded NSs significantly enhanced its antibacterial performance, as evidenced by a marked reduction in MIC values. Notably, Staphylococcus aureus demonstrated the highest sensitivity to GA-NS, with its MIC decreasing dramatically to 0.61 mg/mL nearly a three-fold improvement compared to the unencapsulated form. This improvement in antibacterial efficacy may be attributed to enhanced stability, increased solubility, and improved cellular uptake of GA when delivered via nanocarriers. These findings align with previous studies suggesting that nanoencapsulation enhances the bioactivity and targeted delivery of phenolic compounds by facilitating their interaction with bacterial membranes (Salehi et al., 2021). MBC data ranged from 1.25 to 10 mg/mL. Furthermore, a comparative analysis of MIC and MBC values revealed that Gram-positive bacteria were generally more susceptible to both free and encapsulated forms of GA than Gram-negative strains. For instance, Escherichia coli and Salmonella typhimurium, Gram-negative bacterium, exhibited higher MIC and MBC values compared to Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus cereus, both of which are Gram-positive. This difference in susceptibility is likely due to structural changes in the bacterial cell wall. Gram-negative bacteria, relative to Gram-positive bacteria, have an outer membrane rich in lipopolysaccharides, which acts as a permeability barrier and limits the penetration of antimicrobial agents. In addition, it has been reported that GA, in its non-ionized form, can diffuse across the bacterial cell membrane. Once inside the cytoplasm, it dissociates and releases protons, thereby lowering the intracellular pH. 3.8 In vitro GA loaded NSs release study

The in vitro cumulative release profiles of GA-loaded NSs were determined in simulated gastric, intestinal, and grape juice media (Fig. 4c and d). As shown, the cumulative release of GA in the simulated gastric medium was around 13% after 2 h. The initial rapid release could result from unencapsulated GA on the surface of the NSs (Fig. 4c). In one study, the delayed release of compounds in NSs was attributed to the structure of cyclodextrins (Swaminathan et al., 2010). The cumulative release of GA in the intestinal environment reached about 40% after 6 h (Fig. 4d). This may be explained by the high hydrated of the polymer matrix over time, which causes the network pores to open further and increases mass transfer or diffusion (Santamaría et al., 2025). In addition, the ionization of NS carbonate groups occurs at pH = 6.8, resulting in increased swelling due to coulombic repulsion between the chains (Groult et al., 2021). Moreover, the increased solubility of GA at neutral pH (water pH) demonstrated by NSs (2, 3) is consistent with these results. The cumulative release of GA in the grape juice simulated medium reached 86% after 30 days. This slow and controlled release can be suitable for improving the quality of grape juice during storage.

To characterize the release behavior of GA-loaded NSs, the mathematical models such as zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer–Peppas were employed (Table 3). The accuracy of these models was assessed using the correlation coefficient (R2), and the model exhibiting the highest R2 value was considered as the best fit. As shown in Table 3, the R2 value for the zero-order model in the gastric medium was 0.73, while in the intestinal and grape juice media it was 0.94 and 0.98, respectively. Since the zero-order model represents a constant rate of the bioactive compound over time, the relatively low coefficient of determination (R2 < 0.8) in the gastric medium may be attributed to the presence of uncoated GA on the surface. This likely caused an initial burst release followed by a slower, non-linear release profile that does not align with zero-order kinetics. For the first order model, the R2 values in simulated gastric, intestinal and grape juice fluids were, 0.92, 0.94, and 0.98, respectively. In the Ritger–Peppas model, the release exponent (n) depends on the geometry of the delivery system. Since the NSs were assumed to be spherical, the interpretation followed the criteria for spherical matrices. In simulated gastric fluid, the n value was 0.42, suggesting an anomalous (non-Fickian) transport mechanism, in which both diffusion and polymer relaxation contribute to the release. In the intestinal medium, the n value increased to 0.85, indicating that swelling-controlled release was dominant. In addition, ionization of the carbonate groups of the NSs at pH 6.8 resulted in increased swelling due to coulombic repulsion between the polymer chains. Meanwhile, in grape juice, the n value exceeded 1.0 (n = 1.05), which is characteristic of a super case II transport mechanism, implying that erosion of the carrier matrix was the primary mode of release.Table 3 Parameters of mathematical models of gallic acid release from nanosponges in gastric, intestine, and juice grape simulated media.

3.9 Effect of GA-loaded NSs on physical-chemical properties of the grape juice

3.9.1 Total anthocyanin content (TAC)

Table 4 illustrates the TAC levels of the samples during 30 days of storage. The anthocyanin content in all samples showed a significant decrease over time. The control sample exhibited the highest anthocyanin content in the on day 0, with a value of 260.90 mg/L. The reduction in anthocyanin compounds during storage is likely due to their degradation in the presence of oxygen, and condensation with protein and tannin compounds, which gradually leads to the darkening of the fruit color. The addition of GA and GA-NS had a positive effect on the preservation of anthocyanins in grape juice. Notably, the GA-NS containing samples exhibited greater anthocyanin stability compared to fresh grape juice and samples containing free GA. The highest TAC was observed in sample GA-NS2 (235.20 mg/L), while the lowest levels were found in fresh grape juice at 180.12 mg/L, and GA3 at 200.00 mg/L after 30 days of storage. Overall, at the end of the storage period, samples containing GA-loaded NSs showed a lower reduction in anthocyanin content compared to other samples, likely due to their higher solubility and improved stability in the NS formulation. 3.9.2 Total phenolic compounds (TPC) and antioxidant activity

Polyphenols are chemical compounds that play a key role in shaping the sensory properties such as color, bitterness, and astringency of grape-based products, and they are generally categorized into two main groups: flavonoids (anthocyanins) and non-flavonoids (phenolic acids) (Bendaali et al., 2024). The concentration of phenolic compounds was quantified and expressed as milligrams of GA equivalents per liter of grape juice (Table 4). The fresh grape juice sample exhibited a TPC of 1100.28 mg GAE/L, indicating a naturally high level of phenolic compounds inherent in grapes. During the storage, a general increase in TPC was observed across all treated samples compared to the control. Notably, the enhancement in phenolic content was more pronounced in samples treated with GA-loaded NSs, whereas samples containing free GA showed a relatively moderate increase. This suggests that the nanoencapsulation of GA may improve its stability and facilitate a more sustained release, leading to greater retention or accumulation of phenolic compounds over time (Amani et al., 2022). The highest TPC value was recorded in sample NS-GA2, reaching 1780.33 mg GAE/100 mL, which highlights the potential of NSs as an effective delivery system for phenolic enrichment in grape-based beverages.

The antioxidant activity of samples during 30 days of storage are shown in Table 4. The results demonstrated that all fortified samples exhibited significantly higher antioxidant activity compared to the control, confirming that the incorporation of GA enhanced the free radical scavenging capacity of grape juice. At the beginning of the storage period, the formulations containing GA1, GA2, and GA3 showed higher antioxidant activity than NS-GA1, NS-GA2, and NS-GA3. However, this activity declined more rapidly over time in the free GA formulations, whereas the encapsulated samples retained a higher proportion of their antioxidant activity throughout the storage period. The observed decrease may be attributed to the progressive degradation of GA in the free form, while encapsulation within the NSs matrix likely protected the compound against environmental degradation and promoted a more stable release profile. These findings are in line with previous studies suggesting that CD-based carriers can act as secondary antioxidants, not by direct radical scavenging, but by stabilizing primary antioxidant molecules such as polyphenols, thereby enhancing their functional performance (López-Nicolás et al., 2014).

The superior performance of NS-GA2 compared to NS-GA1 and NS-GA3 suggests the existence of an optimal concentration threshold. At lower levels (NS-GA1), the amount of released GA may not be sufficient to establish effective co-pigmentation and antioxidant synergism with native anthocyanins (Zhang et al., 2020). Conversely, at higher loading (NS-GA3), partial saturation of the NS matrix and possible self-association of GA molecules may reduce their effective interaction with anthocyanins. Excess phenolic concentration may also alter intermolecular equilibria, potentially limiting co-pigmentation efficiency or increasing susceptibility to oxidative reactions (Chitgar et al., 2018). Therefore, NS-GA2 appears to provide a balanced release profile and optimal phenolic ratio, maximizing anthocyanin stabilization and antioxidant performance.

4 Conclusions

This study demonstrates that β-CD-based NSs can function as an effective delivery platform for poorly soluble bioactive compounds such as GA, enabling their incorporation into anthocyanin-rich beverage systems. Rather than merely improving solubility, encapsulation within the NSs fundamentally altered the physical state and release behavior of GA, resulting in enhanced stability, controlled release, and reduced cytotoxicity compared to the free compound. From a broader perspective, these findings provide new insight into the role of CD-NSs as multifunctional carriers capable of simultaneously improving solubility, modulating biological activity, and protecting sensitive phytochemicals in complex food matrices. The observed improvement in anthocyanin retention, antioxidant capacity, and phenolic stability during storage highlights the potential of this system as a practical strategy for the development of functional beverages with extended shelf life and enhanced health-promoting properties. Importantly, this work expands the current literature by demonstrating the feasibility of using GA-loaded NSs not only as a solubility-enhancing system but also as a stabilizing agent in real beverage models. These results open new avenues for future studies focusing on in vivo performance, large-scale production, and the application of similar NSs-based systems to other bioactive compounds and food products.
