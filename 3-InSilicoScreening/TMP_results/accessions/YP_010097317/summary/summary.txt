Concise Summary:
The study investigated the role of a carbon starvation-inducible lipoprotein (Slp) in the initial adherence of a foodborne human pathogen to the bovine rectoanal junction (RAJ). Adherence assays were conducted using RAJ squamous epithelial (RSE) cells and RAJ-in vitro Organ Culture (IVOC) models. The key finding is that the lipoprotein facilitates differential adherence, specifically promoting attachment to columnar cells at the bovine RAJ, unlike squamous cells. This interaction involves the pathogen's Slp binding to the bovine polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR). The authors suggest this interaction may contribute to long-term colonization by interfering with mucosal immunity. Paper: PMC13188874.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:
- Incubation temperatures tested were 12°C and 32°C.
- For bacterial persistence, significant differences in recovery were observed between 12°C and 32°C after 8 months, with higher recovery at 12°C.
- For protozoan persistence (mRNA detection), significantly fewer oocysts were detected in samples incubated at 32°C compared to 12°C, with the greatest difference observed after 5 months.
- For protozoan persistence (mRNA detection), significantly greater numbers of oocysts were detected in 12°C samples compared to 32°C samples after 3 months and through the end of the experiment.
- For protozoan persistence (infectivity), significant differences in recovery were observed between 12°C and 32°C after the first several months, with higher recovery at 12°C.
- The study noted that the detection of mRNA was highly sensitive to temperature, showing significant differences between 12°C and 32°C, attributed to the inherent instability of mRNA molecules at elevated temperatures.

Environmental Context:
- The organisms were studied in soil inoculated with plant tissue, simulating a 'tilling under' process.
- The incubation temperatures (12°C and 32°C) were selected to represent expected low and high temperatures observed throughout a growing season in the Mid-Atlantic region of the United States.

Concise Summary:
Persistence studies were conducted for a bacterium and two protozoan parasites in soil inoculated with plant tissue, incubated at 12°C and 32°C for up to 12 months. The bacterium showed significantly higher recovery at 12°C compared to 32°C after 8 months. For the protozoa, mRNA detection showed a strong temperature dependence, with significantly higher levels detected at 12°C than at 32°C, suggesting mRNA instability at higher temperatures. Both infectivity and mRNA detection methods indicated that persistence was greater at 12°C than at 32°C. The study did not classify the organisms based on specific thermal adaptations (e.g., psychrophilic/thermophilic) but demonstrated differential survival rates across the tested temperature range, with lower temperatures correlating with higher measured persistence for all tested microbes.

Paper: PMC13181065.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:

Environmental Context:
- The organism was isolated from diverse sources including human, animal, environmental (water, soil), and food samples.

Concise Summary:
This systematic review and meta-analysis focused on the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance, and clinical outcomes of non-O157 Shiga Toxin–Producing *Escherichia coli* (non-O157 STEC) across Africa from a One Health perspective. The study analyzed data from 22 included studies, with the highest prevalence reported in Northern and Southern Africa. Key findings include a pooled prevalence of non-O157 STEC in African countries at 20.7% (95% CI: 11.1–30.2). Significant concerns were raised regarding antimicrobial resistance (AMR), with pooled resistance rates exceeding 10% to several commonly used antibiotics (e.g., tetracycline, ampicillin). Resistance genes detected include *blaCTX-M-1*, *blaTEM*, *blaNDM*, *blaOXA*, *sul1*, and *sul2*. The pathogen was found in human, animal, environmental, and food sources, with clinical manifestations primarily involving diarrhea, gastroenteritis, and UTI, most frequently in children < 5 years. The analysis emphasizes the zoonotic potential and the need for One Health surveillance, rapid diagnosis, and public education regarding antibiotic use to curb transmission and outbreaks.
