Background and Aims:

Inflammatory bowel disease is a chronic manifestation of dysregulated immune response to the gut microbiota in genetically pre-disposed hosts. Nearly half of patients with Crohn’s disease (CD) develop selective serum IgG response to flagellin proteins expressed by bacteria in the Lachnospiraceae family. This study aimed to identify the binding epitopes of these IgG antibodies and assess their relevance in CD and in homeostasis.

Methods:

Sera from an adult CD cohort, a treatment-naïve pediatric CD cohort, and three independent non-IBD infant cohorts were analyzed using novel techniques including a flagellin peptide microarray and a flagellin peptide cytometric bead array.

Results:

A dominant B cell peptide epitope in CD patients was identified, locating in the highly conserved “hinge region” between the D0 and D1 domains at the amino-terminus of Lachnospiraceae flagellins. Elevated serum IgG reactivity to the hinge peptide was strongly associated with incidence of CD and the development of disease complications in children with CD up to five years in advance. Notably, high levels of serum IgG to the hinge epitope were also found in most infants from 3 different geographic regions (Uganda, Sweden, and the USA) at one year of age, which decrements rapidly afterwards.

Conclusions:

These findings identified a distinct subset of CD patients, united by a shared reactivity to a dominant commensal bacterial flagellin epitope that may represent failure of a homeostatic response to the gut microbiota beginning in infancy.

Lay summary

Crohn’s patients and healthy infants share a dominant IgG antibody response to the “hinge region” of Lachnospiraceae flagellins, suggesting that failure of establishing homeostasis with the gut microbiota in infancy may contribute to the development of Crohn’s disease later in life.

Graphical Abstract:

INTRODUCTION

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is characterized as chronic inflammation in the gut mucosa, with Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) as the two main forms. It is generally accepted that aberrant immune responses to environmental factors, especially to the gut microbiota, play a critical role in the pathogenesis of IBD1–3. Although CD and UC share various genetic polymorphisms4, dominant antigens identified in CD are considerably different from that in UC5–7. Flagellins expressed by commensal Lachnospiraceae bacteria and CD4+ T cells specific to these flagellins are found in blood and intestinal lesions of Crohn’s patients8, 9, compatible with Lachnospiraceae flagellin being a keystone antigen driving gut inflammation in at least a subset of Crohn’s patients. Serum IgG reactivity to CBir1, a Lachnospiraceae flagellin isolated from mice, is elevated and associated with disease complications in patients with CD, but not in patients with UC or healthy controls (HC)7, 8. In addition, anti-flagellin IgG reactivity was detected years before the onset of CD10–13. However, although Lachnospiraceae flagellins have been identified as immunodominant antigens in CD at the protein level, the dominant B cell peptide epitopes and where they are located remain unclear.

The motility of various commensal and pathogenic bacteria, especially those that belong to the Proteobacteria and Firmicutes phyla (reclassified as Pseudomonadota and Bacillota, respectively), is driven by their expression of flagella, which can consist of as many as 30,000 flagellin monomers14. Flagellin proteins have a vast span of diversity among different organisms but share a characteristic molecular structure. This structure features highly conserved amino- (N-term) and carboxyl- (C-term) D0 and D1 domains, which are separated by hypervariable D2 and D3 domains15, 16. In addition to conferring motility, flagellin is also one of the most potent immune activators known in the microbiota17–21. Flagellin proteins expressed by intestinal pathogens such as Salmonella spp. and Escherichia coli (E. coli) mediate intestinal infection and inflammation22, 23. However, immune reactivity targeting flagellins expressed by commensal bacteria, especially those belong to the Lachnospiraceae family, is only elevated in patients with CD, but not in patients with UC or adult HC, despite the presence of abundant intestinal Lachnospiraceae bacteria in the latter groups24.

Previously, using a microbiota protein antigen microarray, we have shown that ~30% of CD patients in our regional IBD cohort recruited at the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) had elevated serum IgG reactivity to more than 10 different flagellins expressed by Lachnospiraceae bacteria (from both human and mouse origin), and reactivity to individual flagellins was highly correlated to each other24, suggesting that serum IgG response to Lachnospiraceae flagellins was driven by reactivity to shared epitopes, which remained unidentified. Consistently, these multi-flagellin reactive patients had significantly increased flagellin-specific effector/memory CD4+ T cells in the circulation24, which could travel back to the gut and drive colitis25–27 in these patients. In this study, we aim to identify immunodominant B cell peptide epitopes shared among Lachnospiraceae bacterial flagellins in patients with Crohn’s disease and to correlate this immune reactivity with the clinical disease course.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design.

The objective of this study was accomplished by performing a novel flagellin peptide array and flagellin peptide cytometric bead array with an adult discovery cohort recruited at UAB24 containing subjects with CD, UC, and HC, and a validation cohort of pediatric treatment-naïve Crohn’s patients and non-IBD controls collected at 28 sites in the USA and Canada (the RISK Stratification Study)28. Furthermore, a cohort of healthy and non-IBD control infants and their mothers from three distinct geographic locations (Uganda29, the USA29, and Sweden30) was utilized for the discovery of the homeostatic antibody response to the same dominant B cell epitope in Lachnospiraceae flagellins. The sample size for each cohort was fixed by the number of patients enrolled and by our exclusions. However, post hoc power analysis based on the fixed sample size and our previous data in the discovery cohort recruited at UAB revealed a power of 99% with the effect size of 0.8 and a type I error of 1.7% to account for multiple comparison for an increased hinge-specific serum IgG response to be detected in Crohn’s patients. Replicated samples were randomly omitted. Experiments of the validation cohort were conducted blinded until data analyses.

Patient sample collection.

Peripheral blood samples along with medical metadata of IBD patients and healthy volunteers in the UAB cohort were collected at the Kirklin Clinic of UAB Hospital and in research labs in Birmingham, Alabama upon informed consent. All steps followed UAB’s Institutional Review Board guidelines (IRB X300001155, X140515004, and F081003001) and the Declaration of Helsinki Principles. Serum samples of subjects in the RISK cohort were obtained from Dr. Subra Kugathasan at Emory University, USA. Serum samples of subjects in the Uganda and US cohort were obtained from Dr. Edward N. Janoff at the University of Colorado Denver, USA. Serum samples of healthy and allergic subjects in the Sweden cohort were obtained from Dr. Maria C. Jenmalm at Linköping University, Sweden. The Regional Ethics Committee for Human Research at Linköping University approved the study (Dnr 99323). Informed consent was obtained from both parents before inclusion. All serum samples of the RISK, Uganda, and US/Sweden cohorts were obtained through Material Transfer Agreement.

RESULTS

Lachnospiraceae flagellin hinge peptide contains key IgG binding epitopes in CD patients

Pearson correlation analysis of our previous microbiota protein microarray data showed that serum IgG response to most Lachnospiraceae flagellins strongly correlated with each other in CD patients24. This observation suggested that serum IgG reactivity in CD patients was targeting shared epitopes among different Lachnospiraceae flagellins. To test this hypothesis, we generated a novel flagellin peptide microarray consisting of 1512 peptides derived from 19 different Lachnospiraceae flagellins of both human and mouse origin. These were sequential 15mer peptides overlapping by 5 amino acids, which covered the entire sequences of 19 flagellin proteins. Sera from CD patients (n=135), HC (n=42), and UC patients (n=88) were probed against the array, and peptide-specific IgG or IgA reactivities were detected with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (Figure 1A). In the heatmap where serum IgG reactivity to peptides in the D0 and D1 domains of the N-term of Lachnospiraceae flagellins is shown (Figure 1B), those in CD patients clustered apart from those in UC patients and HC, whereas the response to Lachnospiraceae flagellin peptides in UC and HC subjects was not different. Notably, reactivities from CD sera fell in two major clusters, with increased IgG binding identified at p26–41 and p41–61 between the D0 and D1 domains in the amino chain of Lachnospiraceae flagellins (Figure 1B and 1C), which was not seen in UC or HC subjects. There was no significant difference seen in serum IgG reactivity among CD, UC, and HC subjects against other peptides included in the microarray (Supplementary Figure 1). Differences in the serum IgA response to Lachnospiraceae flagellin peptides among groups were not observed. Sequence analysis and motif discovery analysis revealed that, regardless of human or mouse origin, the majority of the 19 Lachnospiraceae flagellins included in the peptide microarray have significant amino acid homology in both the N-term and C-term of their D0 and D1 domains. Specifically, such homology peaks at the “hinge region” between the D0 and D1 domains at the N-term (Figure 1D and 1E), which is significantly different from flagellins expressed by Proteobacteria Salmonella dublin (S. dublin) and E. coli (Supplementary Table 1), although the latter two are homologous to each other. Multiple likely B cell epitopes were predicted in this hinge region when Bepipred Linear Epitope Prediction 2.0 was performed through Immune Epitope Database31 (Supplementary Table 2). The dominant B cell epitope in Lachnospiraceae flagellins identified in CD patients locates right at this “hinge region”. It includes sub-epitopes p25–44 in the D0 domain, and p41–59 in the D1 domain. Hereafter, we will refer to the dominant Lachnospiraceae flagellin B cell epitope in CD, D0–1N p25–59, as the “hinge peptide”.

Serum IgG multi-flagellin reactivity in CD patients is driven by reactivity to the dominant Lachnospiraceae flagellin peptide epitope

In order to confirm the findings of the flagellin peptide microarray, we developed a flagellin peptide cytometric bead array, where serum IgG, IgA, and IgM reactivity to different bead-bound peptides can be simultaneously detected in a multiplexed fashion (Figure 2A). Eight biotinylated conserved peptides (D0N p1–19, D0N p25–44, D1N p41–59, D0–1N p25–59, D1N p74–102, D1C p391–407, D0–1C p410–435, D0C p437–460) covering the N-term and C-term of D0 and D1 domains of Lachnospiraceae flagellins were synthesized and individually conjugated to 4-micron fluorescent beads (Figure 2B and 2C). Beads conjugated with different peptides can be differentiated by their fluorescent intensity in the APC/Cy7 channel with flow cytometry (Figure 2C). Serum samples from the UAB cohort were assayed and grouped based on the microbiota protein microarray data as CD patients with multi-flagellin reactivity (≥10 different flagellins, CDhigh, n=39), CD patients who reacted to less than 10 or no Lachnospiraceae flagellins (CDlow, n=126), UC patients (n=181) and HC (n=95), and IgG, IgA, and IgM reactivity to individual peptides were detected with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies. Standard curves using human IgA, IgM, and IgG capture beads and known concentrations of polyclonal human IgA, IgM, and IgG were obtained and applied to quantify peptide-specific antibodies, allowing cross-comparison of samples run at different times. Data from this cytometric bead array assay strongly validated the results we obtained from the flagellin peptide microarray (Figure 2D). IgG reactivity to the “hinge peptide” (D0–1N p25–59) is highly represented in CDhigh patients compared to CDlow, UC, and HC (Figure 2D–F). In addition, CDhigh patients have significantly elevated serum IgG specific to the sub-epitopes (D0N p25–44 and D1N p41–59) compared to CDlow patients, UC patients, and HC (Figure 2G and 2H), but not to other conserved peptides of the Lachnospiraceae flagellins including D0N p1–19, D1N p74–102, D1C p391–407, and D0–1C p410–435 (Figure 2I–K, and 2M). These data indicate that multi-flagellin IgG reactivity in Crohn’s patients is driven by reactivity to the hinge region of the N-terminus. Of note, CDhigh patients also have significantly elevated serum IgG reactivity to D0C p437–460 (Figure 2L), which is the terminal conserved region of Lachnospiraceae flagellin C-terminus, although the magnitude of reactivity is much lower than that to the N-term hinge peptide. Serum IgA and IgM responses to individual conserved peptides were also analyzed, but consistent with the microbiota protein microarray and flagellin peptide microarray data, no differential reactivity among groups was observed.

Lachnospiraceae hinge peptide is the dominant B cell epitope in pediatric CD patients and is associated with the development of disease complications

Although we were able to confidently identify the dominant B cell peptide epitope in CD patients using our IBD cohort recruited at UAB, there were several limitations involved. First, it is a regional adult cohort of IBD, which might not represent a broader IBD population. Also, the refractory nature of this cohort (rate of biologic therapy in CD was 78% and rate of surgery in CD was 66%)24 limited the power to analyze the association between the epitope-specific IgG sero-response and disease complications in CD. Therefore, we decided to validate this finding using a multi-site-recruited pediatric cohort consisting of patients who were newly diagnosed with CD as well as non-IBD controls (the RISK Stratification Study)28. CD patients in this cohort represented a variety of disease phenotypes regarding disease location, severity, and behavior, and they were recruited at diagnosis prior to any treatment (Supplementary Table 3). Furthermore, patients were followed up to 88 months after the initial sample collection, and their disease course was well-documented, thus the prospective nature of this cohort enabled us to test whether serum IgG to the dominant epitope in CD patients would be associated with the development of disease complications subsequently. In specific, we defined developing stricturing (B2) or penetrating (B3) disease behavior according to the Montreal classification or needing surgical treatment during follow-up as a complicated disease course. Patients who experienced complications at the time of diagnosis were excluded from the analysis.

To confirm that pediatric CD patients in the RISK cohort are reactive to Lachnospiraceae flagellins, we first performed the microbiota protein microarray as previously described24. Sera at recruitment of CD patients who remained inflammatory (B1 behavior, n=250) at 5-year follow-up, CD patients who developed strictures or penetrating disease (B2/3 behavior, n=142) at 5-year follow-up, and non-IBD controls (n=72) were probed with 19 Lachnospiraceae flagellins of human and mouse origin, in addition to S. dublin FliC and E. coli FliC. Previous study using this cohort has reported an augmented serum IgG specific to L. CBir1 Fla in 35% of CD patients who remained B1 at follow-up, and in 62% of CD patients who developed B2/3 at follow-up28. Consistent with this data, our results showed that CD patients that remained B1 exhibited a trend of increased, although not significant, serum IgG reactivity to Lachnospiraceae flagellins when compared to control subjects (Figure 3A). In contrast, CD patients that developed B2/3 at follow-up showed significantly elevated serum IgG reactivity to the majority of Lachnospiraceae flagellins compared to controls and to patients who remained B1 (Figure 3A and Supplementary Figure 2A). Consistent with what we observed in our adult CD cohort recruited at UAB, a subset of pediatric CD patients also exhibited serum IgG reactivity to the majority of Lachnospiraceae flagellins tested (Figure 3B), and the proportion of patients that were multi-flagellin reactive was significantly higher in those who developed B2/3 at follow-up (Supplementary Figure 2B–C).

Next, we tested whether the Lachnospiraceae flagellin hinge peptide also served as the dominant B cell epitope in CD patients in the RISK cohort by performing the flagellin peptide microarray with sera of the multi-flagellin reactive CD patients. Similar to what we observed in our adult IBD cohort recruited at UAB, serum IgG response in multi-flagellin reactive CD patients in the RISK cohort mainly targeted p26–41 and p41–61 between the D0 and D1 domains in the N-term of Lachnospiraceae flagellins (Supplementary Figure 3). Finally, patient and control sera from the whole cohort were assayed with the flagellin peptide cytometric bead array. Similar to adult CD patients, the dominant serum IgG binding sites in pediatric CD patients located at the conserved Lachnospiraceae flagellin hinge region between D0 and D1 domains in the N-term (D0–1N p25–59, D0N p25–44, and D1N p41–59, Figure 3C–E). IgG sero-reactivity to other peptides was either not significantly different among groups (D1N p74–102 and D1C p391–407, Supplementary Figure 4) or at a much lesser magnitude compared to the hinge peptide (D0–1C p410–435, D0C p437–460, and D0N p1–19, Supplementary Figure 4). Moreover, response to the N-term hinge peptide in patients who developed B2/3 at follow-up was significantly higher than that of patients who remained B1 at follow-up. Next, we stratified the CD patients by their surgical treatment at 5-year follow-up. Similar to patients who developed B2/3, patients that needed surgical treatment during follow-up also exhibited significantly higher serum IgG to the N-term hinge peptide at the time of diagnosis (Figure 3F). Survival curves showed that progression to disease complications was significantly faster in patients that were sero-positive to D0–1N p25–59 (Figure 3G). In conclusion, the dominant B cell peptide epitope in Lachnospiraceae flagellins in CD patients were validated with both the flagellin peptide microarray and flagellin peptide cytometric bead array, using the RISK cohort which represents a broad variety of disease phenotypes and behaviors. More importantly, results from the flagellin peptide cytometric bead array demonstrated that elevated serum IgG to Lachnospiraceae flagellin hinge peptide at diagnosis was positively associated with developing stricturing or penetrating disease, or needing surgical treatment at 5-year follow-up.

Serum IgG to Lachnospiraceae hinge peptide strongly correlates with multi-flagellin reactivity in 2 independent CD cohorts

Next, we sought to determine whether serum IgG reactivity to the conserved peptides included in the flagellin peptide cytometric bead array correlates with reactivities to Lachnospiraceae flagellins included in the microbiota protein microarray. First, we performed Pearson correlation analysis between serum IgG reactivity against individual flagellins and serum IgG reactivity specific to individual peptide epitopes for CD, HC, and UC subjects from the UAB cohort and CD patients from the RISK cohort (Figure 4A). The most significant correlations with serum reactivities specific to Lachnospiraceae flagellin proteins were observed in serum reactivities against D0N p25–44, D1N p41–59, and D0–1N p25–59, i.e. the hinge peptide, especially in CD patients from the UAB cohort and the RISK cohort. Sero-reactivities to the hinge peptide did not correlate with Proteobacteria flagellins E. coli FliC and S. dublin FliC. Moreover, sero-reactivity to the C-term conserved epitopes D0–1C p410–435 and D0C p437–460 showed significant correlation with Lachnospiraceae flagellin proteins in RISK cohort CD patients. To better view the correlation in each subject, we plotted individual sero-reactivity against the most significant peptide, D0–1N p25–59, and one of the least significant peptides, D1C p391–407, with that of R. intestinalis Fla1, and E. coli FliC, respectively (Figure 4B). Reactivity to D0–1N p25–59, especially in CD patients from both UAB and the RISK cohort correlated significantly with that of R. intestinalis Fla1, a Lachnospiraceae flagellin, but poorly with E. coli FliC. In contrast, reactivity to D1C p391–407 did not correlate with that of R. intestinalis Fla1 in any subject group, demonstrating that reactivity to the hinge peptide, but not to other conserved flagellin peptides, underlies sero-reactivity to Lachnospiraceae flagellins. Furthermore, analysis of the hinge specific IgG response and independently obtained clinical serological markers in a substantial proportion of the RISK cohort demonstrated a strong correlation between the hinge specific IgG and anti-CBir1 Fla response, but no correlation was found with other clinical serological markers, including anti-Saccharomyces cerevisiae (ASCA) IgA, ASCA IgG, anti-I2, anti-E.coli outer membrane porin C (OMPC), and anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) (Supplementary Figure 5), consistent with previous findings8. Lastly, there was a good concordance between sero-reactivity to the hinge peptide obtained from the flagellin peptide cytometric bead array and flagellin sero-positivity determined by the microbiota protein microarray in both the UAB cohort and the RISK cohort (Figure 4C and 4D). Collectively, these data demonstrated that multi-flagellin reactivity in CD patients is largely due to IgG binding to the dominant hinge peptide epitope.

Serum IgG reactivity to Lachnospiraceae flagellin hinge peptide is a homeostatic response in most infants

Lachnospiraceae is one of the most abundant mucus-associated bacterial families in both human and mouse intestines32, 33. The expansion of their gut colonization in humans coinsides with the introduction of solid food starting from 4–6 month of age34. It has been shown that healthy infants display elevated serum IgG reactivity to Firmicutes flagellins when a “homeostatic immunity” develops during the gut colonization of these bacteria30. We wondered whether flagellin reactivity existed in infants raised in distinct environments, and whether the Lachnospiraceae hinge peptide also serves as the dominant B cell epitope in this homeostatic response. To test this hypothesis, sera were obtained from human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-exposed but uninfected healthy infants from Uganda (n=92), healthy and allergic infants from Sweden (n=54), and healthy infants from the USA (n=16) at 6 and 12 months of age, as well as their cord blood representing antibody reactivity of the mothers. Data from the Swedish and the American infants were grouped together, representing reactivity from developed countries, whereas data from the Ugandan infants represented reactivity in developing countries.

Serum samples were probed with the flagellin peptide cytometric bead array and the level of IgG reactivity to the hinge peptide in each individual at different time points was compared to that of CD patients in both the UAB cohort and RISK cohort (Figure 5A). Hinge peptide specific IgG response in the cord blood of both Uganda and US/Sweden cohorts was low and comparable to that identified in adult HC. At 12 months of age, serum IgG response to the hinge peptide was significantly elevated in both cohorts, indicating that IgG sero-reactivity to Lachnospiraceae flagellins is a homeostatic response present in infants regardless of environmental exposures, and this heightened response is predominantly targeting the hinge epitope. Interestingly, increase in hinge-specific sero-reactivity already occurred in Ugandan infants at 6 months of age, but remained low in infants in the US/Sweden cohort; whereas reactivity was much greater in US/Sweden infants than Ugandan infants at 12 months of age. Of note, sero-reactivity to the hinge peptide was attributed to both sub-epitopes (D0N p25–44 and D1N p41–59) in the US/Sweden cohort (Figure 5B and 5C), but sero-reactivity to sub-epitope D0N p25–44 in Uganda infants at 12 months of age was low (Figure 5B). Serum IgG reactivity to the other conserved peptide epitopes in Ugandan infants also exhibited a significant increase at 6 months of age and started to decline at 12 months of age, which was not observed in the US/Sweden cohort (Supplementary Figure 6A–E).

Given the low level of sero-reactivity to Lachnospiraceae flagellins observed in healthy adults, we attempted to investigate the timeline of its decline from infancy using additional longitudinal serum samples at 24 and 84 months of age from the Sweden cohort. As depicted in Figure 5D, IgG reactivity to the hinge peptide in most individuals exhibited a significant surge at 12 months of age, followed by a rapid decline until reaching the adult level by 84 months of age. IgG reactivity to the other peptide epitopes tested in the cytometric bead array displayed a similar pattern (Supplementary Figure 6F), indicating that the overall decline of the homeostatic IgG response to Lachnospiraceae flagellins in the Sweden cohort is more gradual than that observed in the Ugandan cohort.

Collectively, these findings indicate the initiation of a robust homeostatic antibody response primarily directed at the Lachnospiraceae hinge peptide during early infancy, with a rapid decline observed after 12 months of age. Furthermore, environmental factors play a role in shaping the kinetics of this homeostatic IgG response to the gut microbiota.

DISCUSSION

The dynamic interactions of the host with its microbiota begins at birth and continues throughout the life of the host. The composition of human gut microbiota changes over a lifetime, but most critically early in life where the microbiota is unstable until 3 years of age33, 35, 36. A collection of genes is important in establishing and maintaining homeostasis with the microbiota, and when such genes are deleted from mice, colitis ensues37–39. A similar process occurs in humans resulting in IBD, which eventuates in immune reactivity to the microbiota. Indeed, serologic responses to gut microbial antigens, such as pANCA in UC5, 40, and ASCA antibodies in CD41, 42 have been identified. In addition, sero-response to flagellins, which is preferentially directed at flagellins of the Lachnospiraceae family of bacteria, was shown elevated specifically in patients with CD, but not in UC patients or HC7, 8, 24. Data in this paper provides further insight into the latter response, identifying for the first time a shared dominant B cell peptide epitope in CD patients. This peptide epitope is responsible for the elevated serum IgG response previously described in multi-flagellin reactive CD patients. Importantly, we show that sero-reactivity to this dominant epitope is present in pediatric CD patients at the time of diagnosis and is associated with the future development of disease complications during the next 5 years.

Lachnospiraceae bacteria are among the most abundant mucus-associated commensals in the gut32, 33. They are very diverse: analysis of the genome of 273 isolates from human donors found only 60% of genes shared among multiple species43. At homeostasis, they are beneficial commensals due to their ability to convert dietary fibers into short-chain fatty acids and thus promote the differentiation and maintenance of epithelial cells and regulatory CD4+ T cells (Treg) in the gut44, 45. Many, but not all, are flagellated, a property facilitating their colonization of the mucosa. Therefore, flagellin, the monomeric subunit of the flagellum, is one of the most expressed bacterial proteins in the intestinal lumen and the mucosal layer. A recent study by Bourgonje et al. profiled serum IgG response in IBD patients against 344,000 antigens including commensal and pathogenic microbial peptides, immune epitopes, and food allergens using a high-throughput phage-display immunoprecipitation sequencing technique46. Remarkably, conserved motifs (D0 and D1 domains) in both the N-terminus and C-terminus of Lachnospiraceae flagellins were identified as dominant antigens in CD patients from this extensive library, confirming our previous studies7, 24, 47. This finding demonstrated the highly selective nature of the antibody response in CD patients. In the current study, we further defined the dominant B cell peptide epitope (D0–1N p25–59) of Lachnospiraceae flagellins in CD patients for the first time. Importantly, the RISK cohort allowed us to demonstrate the association between sero-reactivity to this dominant epitope and the future development of disease complications. Development of stricturing or penetrating disease behavior or needing surgical treatment during follow-up were used as simple and measurable criteria for disease complications. It is worth noting that patients classified as B1 behavior can also exhibit a severe disease phenotype. This is evident as the moderate-to-severe disease activity, measured by the weighted Pediatric Crohn’s Disease Activity Index, and the rate of anti-TNFα treatment during follow-up are comparable between B1 and B2/3 categories (Supplementary Table 3). Due to data limitations, it remained unclear whether individuals in the B1 group who showed increased sero-reactivity to the hinge peptide had more severe disease compared to those who did not. Nonetheless, considering that an augmented flagellin-specific CD4+ T cell response accompanies multi-flagellin IgG reactivity, identification of CD patients with heightened hinge reactivity may benefit patient treatment by offering a novel flagellin peptide-guided immunotherapy, which ablates circulating flagellin-specific memory CD4+ T cells by concomitant cell activation and metabolism inhibition27.

Another important finding of this study was that the majority of human infants at one year of age have a robust IgG response to Lachnospiraceae flagellin, targeting the exact dominant peptide epitope identified in CD patients. Accompanied with the increased IgG reactivity to Lachnospiraceae flagellins, there was also an increase in sero-reactivity to other microbiota antigens at 12 months of age30. These elevated serum IgG responses coincide with the colonization and diversification of the gut microbiota and reflect an augmented homeostatic T and B cell response to the colonizing bacteria, in that this process does not involve intestinal inflammation. CD4+ T cell help is required for B cell responses to peptides, and T cells are restricted by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II molecules. The presence of the hinge peptide specific IgG in almost all infants despite human immune diversity indicates that the T cell response is not restricted to any MHC class II allele. Indeed, T cell epitopes that are promiscuous, i.e., can bind to multiple MHC class II alleles, are enriched in Lachnospiraceae flagellins. Interestingly, the magnitude of hinge-specific serum IgG is higher in Sweden/US infants than that of Ugandan infants. This might be due to how soon infants raised in different lifestyles are introduced to solid food, and how long breast feeding is continued. However, whether the generation of this adaptive IgG response is simply a result of antigen encounter, or the flagellin-specific antibodies are actively involved in Lachnospiraceae colonization needs further investigation. Importantly, this heightened sero-reactivity to Lachnospiraceae flagellins declines rapidly with age, potentially attributed to the concurrent rise in intestinal secretory IgA. This aligns with an active regulation of both B cell reactivity and the associated T cell reactivity. The robust IgG sero-reactivity to the Lachnospiraceae flagellin hinge peptide observed in CD patients and recently identified in a subset of patients with severe chronic fatigue syndrome48 indicates a conversion of the homeostatic immune response to flagellins during infancy into a pathologic response later in life, which is likely a consequence of a breakdown in immunological control. Hence, it is important for future studies to delve into the precise immune pathways responsible for the downregulation of these immune responses.

What you need to know

Background and context:

Nearly half of Crohn’s patients exhibit an elevated level of serum IgG response to flagellins expressed by the gut commensal Lachnospiraceae bacteria. Sero-reactivity to multiple flagellins correlates with increased frequencies of circulating flagellin-specific effector/memory CD4+ T cells.

New findings:

Dominant Lachnospiraceae flagellin B cell epitopes in Crohn’s patients and healthy infants are identified that account for multi-flagellin reactivity. Elevated sero-reactivity to these epitopes correlates with the development of Crohn’s complications up to 5 years in advance.

Limitations:

Further research is needed to understand the biological functions of IgG antibodies specific to the dominant Lachnospiraceae flagellin epitopes in Crohn’s disease and at homeostasis.

Clinical research relevance:

The flagellin cytometric bead array used in this study holds potential for a simplified yet robust diagnostic and prognostic assay for Crohn’s disease. Given that reactivity to the dominant flagellin epitope is strongly associated with the development of disease complications, this technique may also assist in identifying Crohn’s patients who would benefit from early therapy.

Basic research relevance:

The identification of the shared reactivity to the dominant flagellin B cell epitope in both Crohn’s patients and healthy infants underscores the need for future mechanistic investigations into the role of flagellin hinge peptide specific IgG antibodies at homeostasis and in the pathogenesis of Crohn’s disease. This study also offers a biological target for the development of antigen-directed immunotherapy of Crohn’s disease.
