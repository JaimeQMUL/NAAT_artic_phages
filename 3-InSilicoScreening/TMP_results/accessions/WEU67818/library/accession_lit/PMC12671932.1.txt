SUMMARY

Donor-specific antibody responses against human leukocyte antigen (HLA) proteins mismatched between transplant donors and recipients cause allograft loss, yet the structural HLA epitopes targeted by alloreactive B cells and antibody remain largely unresolved. We profiled the HLA-A*01:01-specific B cell response in the transplanted kidney and blood of a recipient undergoing antibody-mediated rejection and identified immunodominant B cell and antibody responses that emerged early in the alloimmune response and were focused on topographically exposed mismatched HLA residues located in the α-helices along the peptide-binding groove of HLA-A*01:01. We demonstrated that the anti-HLA-A*01:01 B cell alloresponse converged and was maintained on this same immunodominant HLA subregion, which comprises only 20% of the HLA molecule, in a diverse group of HLA-A*01:01-mismatched transplant recipients. Thus, the B cell and antibody alloresponses appear tightly focused on a topographically defined region on the HLA-A*01:01 crown that is conserved across individuals expressing distinct constellations of self-HLA-A.

INTRODUCTION

Antibodies (Abs) against mismatched human leukocyte antigens (HLA) cause graft loss in kidney transplantation.1–3 Up to 25% of kidney transplant recipients develop de novo donor-specific anti-HLA Abs (HLA-Abs) post-transplant,4 and these individuals suffer a 40% absolute decline in 10-year graft survival5 and significant increases in mortality.6 In addition to Ab-secreting cells (ASCs), which produce injurious donor-specific Abs (DSA), the presence of alloreactive donor-HLA-specific memory B cells (MBCs) also correlates with poor outcomes.7–9 Unfortunately, current therapies to treat Ab-mediated rejection (AMR) by depleting ASCs and MBCs are largely ineffective.10

In a typical response to foreign pathogens, the B cell response is broad and can target multiple distinct epitopes on the surface of a given pathogen-derived protein.11,12 However, it is appreciated that in some settings, B cell responses are focused on a limited number of immunogenic epitopes at the expense of other epitopic sites. This “immunodominant” focused B cell response is thought to be regulated by the frequency of epitope-specific B cells, prior antigen history, affinity, antigenic topography, antigenic polymorphisms, the duration of antigen delivery, and T cell help.13–16 Alloreactive B cell responses are distinct from pathogen-driven responses as the alloreactive response is directed against a non-self, allogeneic (allo)-HLA protein, which is “foreign” yet incredibly similar at a structural and molecular level to self-HLA. Although HLA amino acid (AA) residues that are mismatched between donor and recipient are necessary to generate alloimmunity17 and lead to the production of HLA-Abs, AMR, and graft loss,18 the relative immunogenicity of individual polymorphic residues and the rules governing B cell immune responses to allo-HLA antigens are not well defined.19–21 Indeed, to our knowledge, only a single HLA-Ab/HLA complex structure22 has been solved, and the HLA-Ab in this complex was isolated from a phage display library rather than from a patient responding to an alloantigen. Thus, it is not clear whether B cell alloresponses target epitopes across the entire HLA molecule or focus on specific immunodominant epitopes. This knowledge gap is important as the United States Kidney Allocation system currently prioritizes whole-antigen matches between donor and recipient.23 As AA mismatches between donor and self-HLA correspond with poor outcomes,18,24–26 there is considerable interest in matching at the residue level.19,27–30 However, this approach requires a comprehensive understanding of the rules governing allorecognition by B cells.

In this manuscript, we studied the human B cell alloresponse against one of the most common HLA-A alleles, HLA-A*01:01. We showed that the B cell A*01:01 response spanned the allograft and blood and was clonally selected and affinity matured. The alloresponse to A*01:01 was highly focused on a small subregion of the HLA molecule, its topographically exposed “crown.” This immunodominant B cell response was observed in multiple transplant recipients and remained stable over time. Thus, B cell alloimmune response to HLA-A*01:01 appeared to be conserved across individuals expressing diverse constellations of self-HLA-A proteins and was highly focused on a limited number of non-self “public” residues localized within a discrete topographical domain within the HLA Class I molecule.

RESULTS

Transplant recipient kidney and blood B lineage cells display features of antigen-driven selection.

Abs, directed against HLA mismatched between allograft donor and recipient, are important drivers of kidney allograft rejection,1–3 yet we know little about the alloreactive B lineage cells that give rise to these Abs.31 We therefore examined the kidney allograft and circulating donor-HLA-specific memory B cells (MBCs) and ASCs in a transplant recipient (N006) whose kidney allograft failed secondary to AMR and was surgically removed (Table S1). Consistent with N006’s clinical data (Table S1), circulating serum DSA directed against mismatched HLA Class I and Class II alleles were detected at the time of allograft removal (Table S1, Figure S1A). While we did not detect tertiary lymphoid-like structures32 in the kidney allograft, we did observe B and T cell clusters (Figure 1A), proliferating B cells (Figure 1B), and ASCs (Figure 1C). Using flow cytometry, we identified multiple B cell subsets (Figure 1D), including IgD–CD27+ MBCs, IgD–CD27– (double negative, DN) cells and CD27hiCD38hi ASCs, in the graft and found many intragraft MBCs expressing the activation marker CD71 (Figure 1E).

Next, we sort-purified bulk populations of ASCs, MBCs, and DN cell subsets from N006 blood and kidney samples (Figure S1B,C) and sequenced the B cell receptor (BCR) immunoglobulin heavy (IgH) chain variable domain (IgVH) of these cells.33 We observed extensive clonal expansion of B lineage cells within the allograft, with many connections between the ASC, MBC, and DN cell subsets (Figure 1F). In contrast to the low frequency of shared clonal lineages between blood and tissues isolated from a non-transplanted individual (Figure S1D), numerous lineages were shared between allograft and blood B lineage cells from recipient N006 (Figure 1G,H, Figure S1D). Most B cells in both sites were class-switched (Figure S1E), and the MBCs and ASCs from both sites had undergone extensive somatic hypermutation (SHM, Figure S1F). Thus, the B cells and ASCs in the rejected kidney and blood of recipient N006 were activated, isotype-switched, mutated, clonally expanded, and clonally related – all consistent with a local and systemic ongoing adaptive immune response.

Class-switched, mutated alloHLA-specific MBCs and ASCs accumulate in the allograft and blood.

Clonally expanded B cells in kidney allografts34 and intragraft B cell infiltrates35,36 correlate with poor transplant outcomes, however the specificities of these B cells are not well defined. Although N006 showed DSA responses to three mismatched donor alleles (A*01:01, A*32:01, and DRB4*01:03) at multiple timepoints post-transplant (Figure S1A), A*01:01-specific circulating polyclonal Abs were the most highly expressed as measured by EC50 values (Figure S2A,B). The circulating A*01:01-specific polyclonal Abs were largely of the IgG1 subclass (Figure S2C) and bound C1q at clinically positive levels at all examined timepoints post-transplant (Figure S2D).37 Since N006’s circulating anti-A*01:01 Ab response exhibited correlates of pathogenicity37–42, we used fluorochrome-labeled tetramers of recombinant HLA-A*01:01 to ask whether A*01:01-specific B cells were present in the allograft or blood at the time of graft removal. While B cells binding A*01:01 tetramers were rarely detected in the blood of non-sensitized, healthy control donors (Figure S2E,F), we did identify A*01:01-binding B cells in both allograft and blood of N006 (Figure 1J, Figure S2E–G). To validate that these B cells were specific for A*01:01, we used the A*01:01 tetramers to sort-purify single A*01:01-binding B cells from the allograft and the peripheral blood and then cloned, sequenced, and expressed the BCR IgH and Ig light (IgL) chain genes as recombinant human IgG1 monoclonal Abs (rmAbs). Using A*01:01-coated microbeads, we confirmed that 49 rmAbs derived from A*01:01-binding MBCs (42/49) and DN cells (7/49) (Figure S2H, Table S2) recognized A*01:01 (Figure 1K, Table S3). Next, we used the rmAb-derived IgVH sequences to evaluate whether these rmAbs, which were derived from kidney and blood A*01:01-specific B cells, were clonally related to any of the expanded lineages present in our large IgVH-Seq dataset from kidney and blood bulk MBCs, DN cells and ASCs (Table S2). We identified 13 distinct A*01:01-specific lineages that linked the 49 A*01:01-specific rmAbs to 4033 bulk IgVH sequences (Table S2). The cells in the 13 A*01:01-specific lineages were highly mutated (Figure 1L, Figure S2I,J) and almost exclusively expressed IgG (Figure 1M). Thus, the blood and kidney samples from N006 contained multiple lineages of A*01:01-specific IgG+ somatically-mutated B cells and ASCs.

Clonal lineages of allospecific affinity-matured B cells are shared across kidney and blood.

As expanded B cell lineages reflect the immunogenetic record of SHM and antigen selection,43,44 we used the SHM profile of the A*01:01-specific lineages to evaluate the molecular evolution of the alloresponse. We identified many A*01:01-specific lineages, which contained multiple types of kidney and blood B lineage cells (Figure 2A–C). Most of these lineages were large (Figure 2D) and encompassed at least two different types of B cells (Figure 2E). To assess the affinity of the BCRs expressed by the A*01:01-specific B cells, we used localized surface plasmon resonance (L-SPR, Figure 2F) to measure the binding affinities (KD) of the A*01:01-specific rmAbs that were cloned from the alloreactive B cells (Table S4). Almost all 49 rmAbs bound A*01:01 with high affinity, with a median KD of 2.1 nM (Figure 2G, Table S4). To confirm that this high-affinity binding was due to the accumulation of mutations within the BCR, we generated synthetic rmAbs that matched the sequence of the inferred unmutated common ancestors (UCAs) of 9 A*01:01-specific lineages and then assayed binding of the UCA rmAbs to A*01:01. The KDs of two somatically mutated (MUT) A*01:01-specific rmAbs (Figure 2H) were between 100- to 1000-fold lower than the KDs of the rmAbs encoding the inferred UCAs for the two MUT Abs. Consistent with this, all the rmAbs (n=36) derived from 9 different clonal lineages exhibited significantly higher affinity for A*01:01 relative to their respective UCAs (Figure 2I, Table S4) with 5/9 inferred UCAs showing no detectable binding to A*01:01 by L-SPR (Figure 2J, Table S4). The inferred UCA rmAbs did not appear to be polyreactive as no binding to off-target (non-HLA) antigens was observed for representative UCA and MUT rmAbs from 3 different lineages (Table S3). Thus, unmutated A*01:01-specific naïve B cells appear to give rise to high-affinity selected progeny B cells with fine specificity for donor A*01:01.

Allospecific tissue and circulating B cells are linked to the systemic allo-Ab response.

Circulating high-affinity DSA, which is produced by terminally differentiated ASCs, can also be derived from MBCs or DN cells that rapidly differentiate into ASCs upon stimulation. To determine whether the A*01:01-directed DSA made by N006 was clonally related to N006’s A*01:01 specific MBCs, we affinity-purified A*01:01-specific polyclonal Abs (appAbs) from N006 plasma and used liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry to compare the A*01:01-specific immunoglobulin proteome to the BCR repertoire of the kidney and blood derived A*01:01-specific B cells and ASCs. We identified overlapping A*01:01-specific appAb-derived peptides that matched the sequences of BCRs expressed by A*01:01-specific B cells and ASCs found in N006 kidney and blood (Table S5). For example, AA sequences of peptides recovered from the A*01:01-specific appAbs were identical to IgVH AA sequences from two different Lin4 rmAbs (D01 and G22) cloned from blood MBCs (Figure 3A–C). We identified many circulating appAb peptide sequences that mapped to IgVH sequences from 4 different A*01:01-specific lineages (Lin3, Lin4, Lin6, Lin14), which contained A*01:01-specific B cells and ASCs from kidney and blood (Figure 3D–F). Thus, the ongoing donor-specific blood and kidney B cell responses in recipient N006 are clonally related to the alloAb-producing ASCs that produce DSAs that are associated with AMR.1–3

Allospecific B cell lineages exhibit distinct HLA reactivity patterns.

Our identification of 13 different A*01:01-specific B cell lineages in N006 kidney and blood suggested that N006 made a diverse, polyclonal B cell response to allo-HLA. To assess whether this clonal diversity was indicative of a broad response to many epitopes across the HLA-A protein, we first examined the binding of the 49 A*01:01-specific rmAbs to 21 different HLA-A proteins to identify their individual HLA-A reactivity patterns (Figure 4A, Figure S3A, Table S3). Not surprisingly given our proteome data (Figure 3), comparison of the reactivity profile of the circulating A*01:01-specific appAbs with the aggregated reactivity profile of the 49 rmAbs (Figure 4B) revealed that the B cell-derived A*01:01-specific rmAbs, when considered as a collection, mirrored the overall reactivity pattern of the serologic, polyclonal anti-A*01:01 Ab response. However, despite the diverse HLA reactivity patterns displayed by the 49 A*01:01-specific rmAbs (Figure 4A, Figure S3B), some reactivity patterns were more highly represented. For example, 16 rmAbs from 7 distinct clonal lineages (Figure S3C) exhibited an A*01:01-monospecific reactivity pattern (Figure 4C, referred to as A1+). Similarly, 5 rmAbs from two different clonal lineages exhibited an A*01:01+A*29:02+A*36:01+A*80:01+ (A1+A29+A36+A80+) reactivity pattern (Figure 4C, Figure S3D). Another lineage (Lin1), which contained 10 rmAbs, exhibited consensus binding to 6 HLA-A alleles: A*01:01+A*03:01+A*11:01+A*24:02+A*36:01+A*80:01+ (Figure 4C, Figure S3E). Thus, 63% of the rmAbs could be slotted into 3 different reactivity patterns. Next, we investigated whether specific HLA reactivity patterns were associated with the largest lineages in the A*01:01-specific IgVH-seq database. We observed that the 5 A1+A29+A36+A80+ rmAbs, which were linked to two lineages (Lin4 and Lin12, Figure S3D), collectively constituted 40.0% (Figure 4D) of all A*01:01-specific IgVH sequences. Similarly, the seven lineages containing the 16 A1+-monospecific rmAbs (Figures S3C) represented 35.2% of the A*01:01-specific IgVH repertoire (Figure 4D). Therefore, two patterns of HLA reactivity, which together made up 75.2% of all A*01:01-specific IgVH sequences and included 913 lineages and a large fraction (43%) of the entire rmAb collection, dominated the alloresponse made by N006.

Immunodominant allospecific B cells recognize distinct HLA-A*01:01 epitopes.

Although mismatched residues between donor and recipient HLA are necessary to generate alloimmunity,17 the relative immunogenicity of individual mismatched residues is unclear.19–21 Our lineage and HLA reactivity data suggested that the B cell alloresponse might be focused on a small number of these mismatched residues. To test this idea, we used X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy (cryoEM) to determine the structures of A*01:01 complexed with: Lin2 rmAb E07 (Figure 4E), which exhibited the immunodominant A1+-monospecific reactivity profile (Figure S3C); Lin12 rmAb L02 (Figure 4F), which exhibited the immunodominant A1+A29+A36+A80+ reactivity profile (Figure S3D); and Lin1 rmAb M07 (Figure 4G), which exhibited the A1+A3+A11+A24+A36+A80+ reactivity profile (Figure S3E). As expected for typical protein epitopes,11,45 the A*01:01 epitopes recognized by these 3 rmAbs encompassed 582–912 Å2 of A*01:01 solvent-accessible surface area and contained 20–22 AA residues (Figure 4E–G, Table S6). In agreement with the different HLA reactivity patterns displayed by each rmAb, the rmAbs recognized three distinct epitopes on A*01:01, with little or no overlap in the binding footprints (Figure 4H–I). Thus, three A*01:01-specific rmAbs, which were derived from kidney MBCs and represented the three most dominant reactivity profiles, bound to distinct, largely non-overlapping epitopes on A*01:01.

Immune focusing of the alloreactive B cell response on the crown of HLA-A.

Since rmAbs E07, L02 and M07 exhibited different patterns of reactivity for third-party HLA (Figure 5A–C, Table S3) and recognized distinct epitopes on A*01:01 (Figure 4H–I), we predicted that the mismatched (MM) residues in each epitope must control binding of the rmAbs to A*01:01 and other third-party HLA-A proteins. We therefore aligned the AA sequences of the donor A*01:01 allele with the sequences of the self HLA-A alleles expressed by N006 (A*24:02 and A*30:01) to identify donor HLA residues that differed from both self-alleles (2MM residues) and residues that differed between donor HLA and one of the self-HLA alleles (1MM residues). We then asked whether any of these 2MM and 1MM residues could explain the third-party HLA-A reactivity profile of each rmAb. For example, the A1+-monospecific binding pattern of E07 could be potentially explained by three solvent-accessible epitopic residues (Figure 5D) – V158, R163 (both 2MM residues) and D166 (a 1MM residue) – that were buried by or formed H-bonds with E07 (Figure 5E) and were not present in any of the other tested third-party class I HLA-A alleles (Figure 5D, Table S3). Similarly, we identified candidate epitopic residues that could explain the A*01:01 specificity and HLA-A third-party reactivity profiles of L02 (Figure 5F–G) and M07 (Figure 5H–I).

We next asked whether common approaches used to predict HLA residue immunogenicity46–48 would identify 1MM and 2MM residues that were within the defined footprints of the rmAbs. However, no tool accurately discriminated (Figure S4A,B) between mismatched residues within the A*01:01 epitopes recognized by E07, L02, or M07 (epitopic) relative to the mismatched residues that were located outside of the structurally-defined epitopes (non-epitopic). We then asked whether A*01:01 epitopes recognized by the rmAbs might be focused on specific topographic regions of the alloantigen, as has been reported for other antigen/Ab complexes.49 We therefore assessed the position of the epitopes recognized by the three A*01:01-specific rmAbs within the quaternary structure of HLA-A*01:01 and examined the mismatched solvent-accessible residues that formed close interactions with the rmAbs. All three rmAbs made multiple contacts (Figure 5J–K) with the HLA-A α-helices (AH) that frame the peptide-binding groove (PBG) and form the membrane-distal “crown” of the HLA-A (AH-PBG region).50 We found that epitopic mismatched residues recognized by E07, L02 and M07 were significantly more likely to reside within the AH-PBG region of A*01:01 rather than other parts of the molecule (Figure 5K–M). Solvent-accessible 2MM and 1MM residues targeted by these rmAbs were also significantly closer to the center of the PBG (Figure 5N, Figure S4C,D), even when analyzed using more stringent conditions51 to define the solvent-accessible surface area of A*01:01 (Figure S4E–G). Not unexpectedly, given the binding of the rmAbs to the crown of A*01:01, two of the rmAbs (E07 and L02) interacted with the peptide in the PBG, however binding studies using A*01:01-expressing cells that present a variety of peptides (Figure S4H,I) and structural modeling analyses (see Methods) indicated that the presented peptide, at most, only modestly contributed to HLA binding activity of these rmAbs. Therefore, the A*01:01-specific B cell response made by N006 appears to preferentially target mismatched residues within the AH-PBG region.

AlloHLA-specific B cells target common immunodominant epitopes on A*01:01.

Since we had identified 19 additional rmAbs that were either from the same clonal lineages or exhibited the same HLA reactivity patterns as E07 and L02, we predicted that these Abs should also bind within the A*01:01 AH-PBG region. To address this, we first examined whether the murine anti-human HLA Ab W6/32,52 which recognizes a “pan” HLA epitope outside of the AH-PBG,53,54 blocked the binding of the 21 Abs to A*01:01. We preincubated A*01:01-coated microbeads with W6/32 and then incubated the beads with each of the 21 test rmAbs. We used fluorochrome-labeled secondary Abs specific for the constant region of human IgG, which selectively recognized the human A*01:01-specific test rmAbs. As expected, the pan-HLA W6/32 Ab effectively bound to HLA-A*01:01 (Figure S5A). However, W6/32 only minimally inhibited the binding of the A*01:01-specific rmAbs (Figure 6A). Thus, an Ab that binds outside of the AH-PBG region did not hinder the binding of the 21 A*01:01-specific rmAbs.

Next, we generated murine chimeric rmAb versions of E07 (cE07) and L02 (cL02) by replacing the human Fc domain of E07 and L02 with the mouse IgG Fc domain (see Methods) and evaluated whether cE07 or cL02 could block the binding of the 21 human A*01:01-specific test rmAbs. As expected, the cE07 rmAb completely inhibited the binding of the human non-chimeric Lin2 rmAb, E07 (Figure 6A). Likewise, cL02 rmAb blocked binding of the human non-chimeric Lin 12 human rmAb, L02 (Figure 6A). Consistent with our data showing that E07 and L02 recognize non-overlapping epitopes, cL02 only modestly inhibited binding of E07 to A*01:01 and cE07 did not inhibit binding of L02 to A*01:01 (Figure 6A). However, the Lin2-derived cE07 rmAb blocked the binding of the 6 other human Lin2 rmAbs (Figure 6A), indicating that all rmAbs in this clonal lineage recognized the same epitope. Moreover, cE07 rmAb strongly inhibited (≥88%) binding of nine additional human A1+-monospecific rmAbs (Figure 6A) derived from six other clonal lineages. However, cE07 only minimally affected binding of the four Lin4 A1+A29+A36+A80+ reactive human Abs (Figure 6B). Similarly, cL02 (a Lin12 A1+A29+A36+A80+-specific rmAb) blocked binding of the four A1+A29+A36+A80+-specific rmAbs from Lin4 (Figure 6A) but only modestly inhibited A*01:01 binding by any of the A1+-monospecific rmAbs (Figure 6C).

These data indicated that 21 human A*01:01-specific rmAbs cloned from B cells derived from 9 different genetic lineages appeared to recognize epitopes within the AH-PBG region of A*01:01. To assess whether this was true for another individual, we generated rmAbs from a second organ transplant recipient, subject U084. This individual received a mismatched A*02:01 kidney allograft (Table S1) and subsequently generated an A*02:01+ DSA response. Following similar methods, we generated A*02:01-reactive rmAbs and identified 3 rmAbs that bound A*02:01 and exhibited third-party reactivity for A*01:01. We observed that these 3 rmAbs could be markedly inhibited from binding A*01:01 by the chimeric cE07 and cL02 Abs but not by W6/32 rmAb (Figure 6D). Thus, multiple genetically unrelated clones of B cells derived from two distinct individuals recognize the same or overlapping immunodominant A*01:01 epitopes localized within the AH-PBG region.

Mismatched residues in the AH-PBG region are targeted by allo-HLA specific B cells.

To address whether mismatched residues within the AH-PBG region were important for immune allorecognition of A*01:01, we generated three recombinant single-residue mutants (SRMs) of A*01:01, focusing on the AH-PBG localized 1MM (D166) and 2MM (V158, R163) residues that were present in the E07 but not L02 epitope (Fig. 5D, 5F). These residues were reverted back to the “self” HLA-A residues expressed by N006 at these positions (V158A, R163T, D166E). Since structural modeling (Figure S5B) predicted these mutations would disrupt hydrophobic interactions (V158A, Figure S5C) and hydrogen bonds (R163T and D166E, Figure S5D,E) between E07 and A*01:01, we hypothesized that E07 would exhibit decreased binding to the A*01:01 SRMs. We therefore measured binding of E07 and L02 to the A*01:01 SRMs by calculating the EC50 value for each rmAb against WT (wild-type) A*01:01 and the V158A, R163T, and D166E A*01:01 SRMs (Figure 6E–H). As predicted, E07 showed significantly decreased binding to all three A*01:01 SRMs relative to the WT A*01:01 (Figure 6E,G), while L02 showed unaltered binding against the A*01:01 SRMs (Figure 6F,H). L-SPR analyses corroborated these results (Figure S5F,G). Thus, mismatched A*01:01 epitopic residues, which were bound by E07 and located in the AH-PBG region, were important for A*01:01 recognition by E07 but not L02.

Finally, we addressed whether the A*01:01 SRMs affected binding of other A1+-reactive and A1+A29+A36+A80+-reactive rmAbs. As expected, mutations in the E07 (A1+-reactive) epitope had minimal impact on the binding of the 4 additional rmAbs (Figure 6I,J) that were A1+A29+A36+A80+-reactive and recognized the same epitope as L02. By contrast, all (16/16) A1+-monospecific rmAbs showed significantly decreased binding to at least one of the SRMs (Figure 6I) with EC50 values up to 15-fold higher against the A*01:01 SRMs compared to WT A*01:01 (Figure 6J). In fact, rmAbs that were most inhibited by cE07 also exhibited higher EC50 values for binding to the A*01:01 SRMs that targeted the E07 epitope (Figure 6K–M). Thus, single mismatched residues localized in the A*01:01 AH-PBG region contribute to immune recognition by multiple distinct genetic lineages of alloreactive B cells.

The DSA response to A*01:01 is focused on the AH-PBG immunodominant epitopes.

Since the A*01:01-specific kidney and blood B cell response and the circulating A*01:01 DSA present in recipient N006 were clonally linked, we predicted that N006’s circulating DSA response should, like the B cell response, be focused on the A*01:01 AH-PBG region. To test this, we assessed whether chimeric cE07 and cL02 rmAbs inhibited binding of circulating A*01:01-specific appAbs (Figure 7A). Pre-incubation of A*01:01 beads with W6/32 Ab, which binds outside the AH-PBG region, had a minimal effect on the binding of the appAbs (Figure 7A,B). By contrast, preincubation of A*01:01 beads with either cE07 or cL02 partially inhibited A*01:01 binding by the appAbs (Figure 7A,B), while pre-incubation with both cE07 and cL02 completely inhibited (99%) A*01:01 binding by the appAbs (Figure 7B). Next, we measured A*01:01-specific appAb binding to the A*01:01 SRMs and observed 50–80% reduction in binding to the A*01:01 SRMs compared to WT A*01:01 (Figure 7C,D). Thus, both the cellular and serologic alloreactive responses made by N006 appear focused on a limited number of immunodominant A*01:01 epitopes containing solvent-accessible mismatched residues within the AH-PBG region.

Shared recognition of immunodominant HLA-A epitopes across transplant recipients.

Two rmAbs (E07 and L02) that bound to the A*01:01 AH-PBG region (Figure 7E) – a region comprised of 74 residues, or 27% of the entire 274 AA A*01:01 α-chain (Figure 7F) – were sufficient to completely inhibit N006’s humoral response to the A*01:01-mismatched allograft. Of the 41 epitopic residues recognized by E07 and L02, 73% were localized within the AH-PBG region (Figure 7F). Because this frequency was significantly greater than would be expected by chance, we predicted that the A*01:01 AH-PBG region might represent a “public” epitopic region that would be targeted not just by recipient N006 but also by other individuals receiving a mismatched A*01:01 allograft. We therefore performed the same cE07/cL02 blocking assays using serum samples collected from 11 solid organ transplant recipients (Cohort 1, Table S1) who received a mismatched A*01:01-typed organ and developed circulating polyclonal DSA responses to A*01:01 and third-party HLA-A molecules (Figure 7G). Blocking with the control W6/32 Ab had little effect and reduced serum Ab binding to A*01:01 by an average of 3.4% (Figure 7H,I). By contrast, the cE07 and cL02 rmAbs inhibited A*01:01 binding by circulating alloAbs in all 11 transplant recipients (Figure 7H,I), and the combination of cE07 and cL02 inhibited 90–100% (mean 97%) of A*01:01-binding by circulating serum alloAbs (Figure 7I). Similar results (Figure S6A–C) were observed with a second AMR-diagnosed cohort of 7 A*01:01 mismatched individuals with circulating DSA to A*01:01 ( Cohort 2, Table S1). Since samples from the two cohorts were collected at different timepoints post-transplant and the recipients expressed distinct combinations of self-HLA alleles and were treated with different immunosuppressive regimens (Table S1), we conclude that the A*01:01-specific B cell response of a diverse group of transplant recipients is focused on immunodominant epitopes within A*01:01 AH-PBG region.

Shared recognition of mismatched residues in the AH-PBG region of HLA-A*01:01 across transplant recipients.

Although Cohort 1 recipients expressed distinct self-HLA alleles (Figure 7G), their allo-A*01:01-specific serologic Ab responses uniformly converged on A*01:01 epitopes that were localized within the AH-PBG region and were “covered” by the E07 and L02 rmAbs. We determined that 62% (61/98) of the solvent-accessible 2MM residues identified in Cohort 1 individuals were located within the A*01:01 AH-PBG region (Figure S6D). Moreover, the epitopes recognized by E07 and L02 encompassed every 2MM residue within the AH-PBG region for 63% (7/11) of the subjects in Cohort 1 with most recipients double-mismatched at the E07 epitopic residues R163 (9/11), D166 (6/11), and V158 (11/11) (Figure S6E–G). To address whether binding of the A*01:01-specific polyclonal Abs present in Cohort 1 serum samples was influenced by mismatched residues localized within the AH-PBG region, we compared binding of the subjects’ serum to WT A*01:01 and the three A*01:01 SRMs (V158A, R163T and D166E) that targeted the E07 epitope. We observed significant reductions in the binding of serum Abs from these recipients to the three A*01:01 SRMs relative to WT A*01:01 (Figure 7J) with an average reduction in binding of 57–61% for R163T and D166E (Figure 7K). We next compared the binding of the serum samples to the A*01:01 SRMs (Figure 7K) with the results of the chimeric cE07 and cL02 binding assays (Figure 7I) and, as expected, found no correlation between inhibition by cL02 and sensitivity to the A*01:01 SRMs targeting the E07 epitope (Figure 7L). By contrast, serum samples that were highly inhibited by cE07 bound significantly less well to the R163T and D166E A*01:01 mutants (Figure 7L). Therefore, all 11 individuals who received a mismatched A*01:01 allograft made a B cell response that targeted residues localized in the A*01:01 AH-PBG region.

The HLA-A*01:01 AH-PBG region is targeted by DSA at early and late timepoints during transplant rejection.

To address whether the A*01:01 humoral immune response of subject N006 was stably focused on AH-PBG-localized epitopes, we analyzed serum samples collected at 5 timepoints post-transplant. We observed that circulating A*01:01-specific Abs from all timepoints were inhibited by cE07 and cL02 (Figure 7M) and demonstrated a similar degree of sensitivity to the A*01:01 SRMs (Figure S6H,I). To see whether the DSA response of other A*01:01-mismatched patients was also stably focused on the AH-PBG region of HLA-A*01:01, we obtained additional longitudinal serum samples from four Cohort 1 individuals starting with serum collected at the first detection of anti-A*01:01 Abs. Again, we found that serum polyclonal Ab binding to A*01:01 was significantly inhibited by the combination of cE07 and cL02 Abs over time (Figure 7N, Figure S6J). Thus, the immunodominant humoral immune response directed against the AH-PBG region of A*01:01 is detected early and remains stable over time in multiple A*01:01 mismatched transplant recipients. Taken together, these data show that rmAbs E07 and L02, which were cloned from kidney-infiltrating B cells in transplant recipient N006, blocked the serum A*01:01 DSA response of N006 and 18 other A*01:01 mismatched transplant recipients. Based on these results, we propose that the topographically defined AH-PBG region of A*01:01 may represent a site for immunodominant “public” epitopes that are consistently targeted by B cells and Ab from transplant recipients mismatched at the A*01:01 locus. These findings have potential relevance to to HLA matching strategies and immunotherapies directed against humoral alloresponses.

DISCUSSION

Although anti-HLA DSA2,4 and HLA-specific B cells7–9 are clearly associated with organ transplant rejection,9 the rules governing B cell recognition of HLA alloantigens are unresolved. This is because past analyses have almost exclusively focused on the HLA alloreactivity patterns of polyclonal DSA – patterns that we showed do not easily reveal the specific A*01:01 epitopes recognized by individual B cells or Abs.55 Based upon our single-cell, structural, and molecular studies, our data support a model in which A*01:01-directed alloresponses made by a diverse group of transplant recipients converge upon immunodominant “public” B cell allo-epitopes that contain mismatched residues and are localized within the topographically exposed crown of the HLA molecule.

While prior studies report expanded clonal B cell populations in transplant patients,31,56–58 the data showing that these populations specifically target HLA31 is limited. In our analysis of the alloreactive B cells isolated from transplant recipient N006, we observed what appeared to be a typical “high quality” non-polyreactive, clonally-selected and affinity-matured B cell response. This finding was consistent with other chronic diseases with continuous antigen accessibility including persistent infections,59 autoimmunity,33,60 and cancer.61 Similar to prior studies reporting tissue-infiltrating B cells specific for non-self or altered-self antigens in transplantation,56 cancer,61,62 and infection,63 we identified multiple abundant alloreactive clones containing B cells and ASCs that were present in the allograft. Our data does not resolve whether these allo-specific B cells were generated in situ, migrated to the tissue following affinity maturation in secondary lymphoid tissues, or appeared only after removal of immunosuppression. However, the data showed that diverse clones of alloreactive B cells and ASCs, many of which were clonally linked to the clinically relevant serum DSA response, may reside in the allograft.

The recognition of A*01:01 by the alloreactive B cells was not solely driven by the physicochemical properties46–48,64,65 of the mismatched AA residues in alloHLA A*01:01. Instead, the topography of the HLA antigen itself and the accessibility of mismatched residues within a discrete region of the quaternary structure of A*01:01 dictated immunogenicity. This conclusion is consistent with predictions made by newer tools66,67 that incorporate the quaternary structure of proteins to predict B cell epitopes. However, this AH-PBG region-centric model of B cell allorecognition does not fit well with one of the leading theories in the field, referred to as eplet recognition.55,68–71 In the eplet model of allorecognition, mismatched residues between donor and recipient HLA molecules are grouped into small, 3.5 Å-radius patches, called eplets, that are recognized by the heavy chain CDR3 domain of alloreactive Abs. The eplet model did not fit with the HLA-A reactivity data for the 49 A*01:01-specific rmAbs studied here nor did the eplet model explain the structural data for A*01:01 complexed with alloreactive rmAbs E07, L02 and M07. Given that the A*01:01 AH-PBG region was extensively targeted by the polyclonal DSA from all transplant recipients studied here, we think that B cell allorecognition is not focused on small patches of mismatched residues across the molecule but rather converges on common residue mismatches located within a discrete subregion of the HLA molecule.

Our data argue that the polyclonal allo-HLA-A*01:01 Ab response made by 19 transplant recipients was directed against shared immunodominant epitopes in A*01:01. Similar convergent epitope targeting has been observed in infection.72–75 While multiple factors contribute to B cell immunodominance,13 previous studies of antiviral B cell responses emphasize the importance of antigen orientation to B cell fine specificity.49,76–78 Given that the topographically exposed crown of the HLA Class I serves as the binding site for other immune receptors,79,80 we suggest that this same region likely also supports recognition and binding by BCRs targeting the solvent-accessible mismatched residues. The convergence of alloHLA-B cell responses across a diverse group of recipients at multiple timepoint post-transplant suggested that immune focusing on the AH-PBG region may be a general and stable feature of A*01:01 reactivity. This is consistent with studies of COVID-19 infection and vaccination that showed intra-antigenic B cell immunodominance hierarchies (i.e., the distribution of B cells targeting certain subdomains of the target protein) remained stable over time.81,82 This stable immunodominant response can be contrasted with the phenomenon of B cell epitope intramolecular spreading,83 and suggests that the topographic features that contribute to immunogenicity during the initial alloresponse remain critical to B cell allorecognition over time. While we do not know whether differences in immunosuppression or immunogenetics can modulate this epitopic focusing, the individuals studied here expressed a diverse set of self-HLA alleles and were treated with a variety of immunosuppressive regimens. Yet all made a similarly dominant Ab response to the A*01:01 AH-PBG region. This fits well with a study that examined immune responses to SARS-CoV-2 vaccination and found no differences in intramolecular epitopic focusing between healthy controls and immunosuppressed kidney transplant recipients.82

In conclusion, this work provides a framework for understanding the structural and molecular basis of B cell epitope targeting in organ transplant rejection. It highlights the anatomy of the B cell alloresponse as it spans tissue and blood and matures into high-affinity donor HLA-specific responses that can be detected as circulating DSA. Our data indicate that the quaternary structure of HLA-A*01:01 enforces immune focusing and immunodominant B cell responses in transplant rejection. Although our study was limited to relatively few individuals, our observation that topographic “public” features of A*01:01 can drive immunodominant B cell and Ab responses opens a range of possibilities for future basic and translational studies that may provide a framework for prioritizing HLA Class I mismatches or designing immunotherapeutics that block the highly immunogenic crown of HLA.

Limitations of the study

In this study, the molecular and structural experiments were conducted with samples collected from one individual (N006); however, the core findings were corroborated across 19 other individuals and were not affected by the timing of sample collection, immunosuppression, infection, immunogenetics, or other clinical factors. Despite the structural and topographic similarities between different class I and class II proteins,84 we do not know whether the AH-PBG region of other HLA molecules will be selectively targeted by alloAbs. Therefore, analyses of additional locus- and allele-specific responses will be critical, particularly given the strong association of class I and class II DSA with kidney allograft failure.85 Additional longitudinal analyses of B cell alloresponses are needed to understand the structural basis of epitopic focusing during clonal selection and affinity maturation. Finally, we need to understand how T cell help supports these immunodominant B cell responses86 and how linked recognition of epitopes operates in alloimmunity.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Subjects.

A kidney transplant recipient (subject N006) with a history of allograft rejection and graft loss who was undergoing transplant nephrectomy was enrolled in this study at the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) (Table S1 for clinical history). Additional biobanked serum samples from subject N006 and from n=18 solid organ transplant recipients (Table S1) who received an HLA A*01:01-mismatched solid organ transplant and subsequently developed anti-A*01:10 DSA were analyzed. Four samples from healthy control doors were used for serum and PBMC analysis. This study was approved by the UAB Institutional Review Board (IRB, protocols # IRB-300004168, # IRB-300007045, and # IRB-150612005), and informed consent was provided by the study subjects. Biologic samples from two deceased organ donors (including subject U084, Table S1 for clinical history), deemed non-human subjects research by the UAB IRB, were used. Table S1 provides demographic information (age, sex, race/ethnicity), if available, for all individuals providing biosamples for this project. The numbers of individuals included in each analysis can be found in the figure legends. The data reported here were not powered to analyze potential differences related to sex-, race/ethnicity or age. This is a limitation of the study and larger studies will be needed to address potential differences related to the demographics of the participants.

METHOD DETAILS

Clinical anti-HLA Ab testing.

Clinical anti-HLA Ab testing for subject N006, subject U084, the cohort of n=18 solid organ transplant recipients, and the n=4 healthy control donors (HCDs) was performed by the UAB Histocompatibility Laboratory using its standard clinical assays. For the clinical MFI data (Table S1 and Figures 1I, 7G, S1A, and S6A), IgG Abs from patient sera or plasma were collected using a Melon IgG spin column and then analyzed neat using the OneLambda HLA arrays, according to manufacturer instructions (OneLambda, ThermoFisher).87 Proprietary software was used for the MFI calculation with a MFI ≥1500 used as the clinical threshold for a positive reading. HLA-specific Ab data was obtained from the patients’ electronic medical record. All research HLA-Ab cytometric bead array data were generated in the research lab using methods described below.

Clinical C1q binding assay.

Assessment of donor-specific complement fixation (OneLambda, # PEC1Q) was performed by the UAB Histocompatibility Laboratory using standard clinical assays, per manufacturer protocols. Serum (neat), plasma (neat), or recombinant monoclonal Abs (10 μg/mL) were analyzed. Samples containing polyclonal anti-A*01:01 Abs were incubated with C1q and HLA-coated microbeads. To detect bound C1q a fluorochrome-labeled anti-C1q Ab was used. Samples were analyzed according to manufacturer instructions. Proprietary software was used for the MFI calculation. Data reported as positive or negative relative to threshold set per manufacturer protocol and per published literature at MFI≥500.37

Clinical autoAb testing.

Assessments of autoantigen binding (OneLambda, # LSAUT1–3) were performed by the UAB Histocompatibility Laboratory using its standard clinical assays, per manufacturer protocols. For autoantigen binding, rmAbs (2 μg/mL) were incubated with autoantigen-coated microbeads. Bound Ab was quantitated using fluorochrome-labeled anti-human IgG. Samples were analyzed according to manufacturer instructions. Proprietary software was used for the MFI calculation. Data reported as positive or negative relative to threshold set per manufacturer protocol and per negative control threshold verified with anti-influenza rmAbs.88

Clinical HLA typing by next-generation sequencing or by imputation.

HLA typing of donor (donor to recipient N006) and recipient (subject N006) was performed by the UAB Histocompatibility Laboratory. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using the Qiagen EZ1 DNA blood kit via the Buffy coat protocol. Library construction and enrichment were performed per manufacturer’s protocols for the CareDx AlloSeq Tx 17 assay and libraries were run on the Illumina MiSeq. Propietary CareDx software, AlloSeq Assign, v1.0.3 (CareDX, Stockholm, Sweden) utilized references from the Immuno Polymorphism Database-ImMunoGeneTics project/HLA Database (IPD-IMGT/HLA database) v3.47.0.0 to make allele assignments for all HLA Class I and Class II loci. For subject U084 and any donors or recipients in Cohorts 1 and 2 (n=18) that were not HLA typed by next-generation sequencing, SSO typing was performed, 4 digit HLA-A alleles for recipient and/or donor were imputed (Table S1) based upon ethnicity and population frequencies.89

Exposure times (in milliseconds) for each channel (DAPI, FITC, PE, APC): Figure 1A (500, 3000, 6000, 10000), Figure 1B (2000, 3000, 8000, 10000), Figure 1C left (60, 70, 1000, 600), Figure 1C right (250, 70, 1400, 800). The tonal range of RGB was reduced in Adobe Photoshop (Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA) to increase brightness and contrast, with settings uniformly applied across all images utilizing the same staining panel.

Next generation sequencing of the IgVH repertoire (IgVH-Seq).

IgVH sequencing was performed as previously described.88 After sorting B cells subsets directly into lysis buffer (Norgen Single Cell RNA Purification Kit) with 1% mercaptoethanol, lysates were snap frozen on dry ice and stored at −80°C. Total cellular RNA was extracted from bulk sorted B cell and ASC subsets according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was prepared (BioRad iScript) per manufacturer’s instructions, and DNA amplicon products were generated using Ig-specific variable and constant gene primers (Table S2) and Platinum PCR Supermix (Invitrogen).88 Samples were indexed (Nextera Index Kit, Illumina), purified, quantitated (KAPA Library Quantification Kit, Roche) and pooled into libraries. Unique molecular identifiers were not used. Following generation, libraries were denatured per manufacturer instructions (Illumina) and loaded onto a 600-cycle V3 MiSEQ cartridge (Illumina) for sequencing33 in the UAB Heflin Sequencing Core.

Phylogenetic analyses of IgVH-Seq data.

IgVH sequencing data were processed and analyzed as described previously.33 Joined paired-end reads were assembled and quality filtered using FastQC scores. Using IMGT/HighV-QUEST,90 sequences were annotated, and productive sequences were utilized for downstream analyses. Sequences were clustered into lineages based upon IGHV and IGHJ gene identity, identical HCDR3 length, and HCDR3 nucleotide identity >85%. Downstream analyses were performed in Matlab (R2020a, The Mathworks Inc.) or in R (R core team). Phylogenetic trees were constructed using Phylip’s DNA parsimony (dnapars) tool (v3.695; adjusting settings 1, 4, 5, 6 and O setting the germline sequence as the outgroup).91 Phylogenetic trees were visualized using Cytoscape v3.8.2.92

Circos and alluvial visualization of IgVH-Seq data.

The outer numbering of Circos plots shows sequence counts for a given tissue-phenotype cell subset. The outer ring colored (non-gray) for lineages contained in the top 20% of size-ranked lineages within a given tissue-phenotype cell subset. Links connect lineages shared between tissue-phenotype subsets; links are colored (non-gray) if the lineage is contained in the top 20% of size-ranked lineages in at least one tissue-phenotype cell subset. Alluvial ribbons depict connectivity of shared lineages, with the individual lineages ranked by size in each subset along the y-axis. Shared lineages are represented as ribbons with individual lineages ranked by size in each subset. In three-subset alluvial plots, lineages shared between all 3 subsets are colored orange and lineages shared between any two subsets are colored green. The numbers of sorted cells for IgVH sequencing, numbers of unique non-singleton lineages and %D50 are indicated. %D50=(#lineagesintop50%ofsubsetA/#oflineagesinthetop50%ofsubsetB)×100.

Single cell rmAb cloning, sequencing, expression and purification.

rmAbs were generated using previously described methods.88 cDNA was prepared from mRNA isolated from single non-naïve dual HLA-A*01:01 tetramer-binding B cells (subject N006) or A*02:01 tetramer-binding B cells (subject U084) that were index-sorted into hypotonic lysis buffer in 384-well PCR plates which were stored at −80°C until used. RT-PCR was performed to amplify IgVH and IgVK/L transcripts (Table S2). Amplicons were cloned into human IgG1, IgK, or IgL expression vectors, as previously described.88 The resulting plasmids underwent Sanger sequencing at the Genetic Resources Core Facility (RRID SCR_018669, Johns Hopkins University Department of Genetic Medicine, Baltimore, MD). IgH and IgL plasmids for each Ab were co-transfected into 293FreeStyle cells (Invitrogen) using polyethylenimine (Polysciences). Conditioned supernatants were screened for anti-HLA reactivity using HLA cytometric bead arrays as described below. HLA-A*01:01-reactive (subject N006) or HLA-A*02:01-reactive (subject U084) rmAbs were purified from conditioned media using Sepharose G Fast Flow (Cytiva) and the resulting rmAb concentrations were determined by UV spectrophotometry (Nanodrop, Thermo Fisher). For x-ray crystallography and cryoEM studies, the heavy chain expression vector was modified using site-directed mutagenesis to remove the IgCH gene segment of each rmAb (E07, L02, and M07). The resulting constructs were transfected into 293Freestyle cells as described above to produce antigen-binding fragments (Fabs). Fabs were purified using CaptureSelect IgG-CH1 Affinity Matrix (Thermo Scientific).

Generation of inferred UCA rmAbs.

Inferred UCAs for each rmAb were generated by reverting all nucleotides encoding variable gene segments (VH, DH, JH, VK/L, JK/L) to the IMGT reference sequence of the gene segment alleles. Nontemplated nucleotides (N-additions) were not modified. Recombinant human IgG1 mAb constructs were synthesized (Sino Biological) using the inferred UCA nucleotide sequences.

Immunogenetic analysis of rmAbs.

IgVH and IgVK/L sequences from the cloned and verified A*01:01-specific rmAbs were submitted to IMGT/HighV-Quest for annotation. rmAbs were grouped into clusters based on identical VH, DH, JH, VK/L, JK/L gene assignments and identical HCDR3 and LCDR3 lengths. All 49 rmAb IgVH sequences were then directly compared to the sequences in the bulk-sorted kidney and blood B cell/ASC IgVH-Seq database. Any A*01:01-specific rmAb meeting these lineage criteria (same VH, DH, JH gene assignments, identical HCDR3 length and >85% NT identity across the HCDR3) was assigned as a member of the bulk B/ASC clonal lineage, and these lineages were then defined as A*01:01-specific. This approach resulted in the identification of 13 unique A*01:01-specific lineages, which each contained one or more A*01:01-specific rmAbs (see Table S2).

High-throughput localized surface plasmon resonance (L-SPR).

L-SPR data were collected using a 1:1 referencing system on the Nicoya Alto HT-SPR instrument according to manufacturer’s recommendations and as described.93 Biotinylated recombinant WT A*01:01 (500 μg/mL, NIH Tetramer core or produced in-house) or single-residue mutants of A*01:01 (500 μg/mL, produced in-house) was resuspended in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 and immobilized on an EDC/NHS-activated carboxyl sensor (Nicoya) for 20 min. The rmAbs (analytes) were first diluted to 1.3 μM in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20. Single-cycle kinetic analyses were then performed at 25°C with five threefold analyte dilutions, with maximum concentrations of 433 nM. Kinetic fitting was performed using a 1:1 Langmuir model within the Nicoya user portal. For datasets that poorly fit with a 1:1 Langmuir model, an affinity fit model was used (TraceDrawer, Ridgeview Instruments). The limit of detection for the equilibrium constant (KD) was estimated to be 4.3 μM (10 times the highest concentration of analyte tested). Recombinant mAbs with responses <50 plasmon resonance response units or without monotonic increases across increasing concentrations were judged to not have measurable binding by L-SPR. For visualization and for calculations of fold-change in affinity, if a rmAb had no detectable binding by L-SPR (KD >4.3 μM), a KD of 5 μM was used.

Plasma immunoglobulin proteomics.

Recombinant A*01:01 was conjugated to CNBr-activated Sepharose (Cytiva) following manufacturer’s protocol. Subject N006 plasma was diluted 1/10 in PBS, filtered using a 0.22μm syringe filter (Sigma-Aldrich) and passed over the A*01:01 column. Bound protein was eluted with 0.1M glycine pH 2.5, buffer-exchanged into PBS, concentrated to 1 mg/mL, and stored at 4°C. The resulting affinity-purified A*01:01-binding polyclonal plasma Abs (appAbs) were reduced with DTT, denatured at 70°C for 10 minutes (min) and separated on a 10% Bis-Tris Protein gel. IgH and IgL bands were visualized with colloidal Coomassie, excised from the gel, equilibrated in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate (AmBc), and digested overnight with Trypsin Gold, Mass Spectrometry Grade (Promega) or endoproteinase AspN (New England BioLabs) following manufacturer’s instructions. Peptide digests were reconstituted in 0.1% Formic Acid/ddH2O at 0.1 μg/μL, injected (8 μL each) onto a 1260 Infinity nHPLC stack (Agilent Technologies), and separated using a 75 μm I.D. x 15 cm pulled tip C-18 column (Jupiter C-18 300 Å, 5 μm, Phenomenex). This system runs in-line with a Thermo Q Exactive HFx mass spectrometer, equipped with a Nanospray FlexTM ion source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Data were collected by the UAB Mass Spectrometry / Proteomics Core in CID mode. The nHPLC was configured with binary mobile phases that includes solvent A (0.1% FA in ddH2O), and solvent B (0.1% FA in 15% ddH2O / 85% ACN), programmed as follows: 10 min at 5% solvent B (2 μL/min, load), 90 min at 5%−40% solvent B (linear: 0.5 nL/min, analyze), 5 min at 70% solvent B (2 μL/min, wash), 10 min at 0% solvent B (2 μL/ min, equilibrate). Following each parent ion scan (300–1200m/z at 60k resolution), fragmentation data (MS2) was collected on the top-most intense 10 ions at 7.5k resolution. For data dependent scans, charge state screening and dynamic exclusion was enabled with a repeat count of 2, repeat duration of 30 seconds (s), and exclusion duration of 90 s. Following LC-MS/MS, the data were processed, searched, filtered, grouped, and quantified, as previously reported.94 LC-MS/MS-derived peptide AA sequences were used to probe an AA sequence database that included all IgVH sequences derived from the kidney, blood and rmAb IgVH-seq libraries. Exact peptide matches to IgVH sequences were determined and members of the lineages identified were ranked by % of total AA matched. For visualization, peptide sequences were plotted by position within the IgVH sequence. The mass spectrometry proteomics data were deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE95 partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD043144 and 10.6019/PXD043144.

HLA expression plasmids.

Expression plasmids for A*01:01 α-chain (pTCF5, Plasmid #180448) and β2m (pTCF158, Plasmid #180457) were purchased from Addgene. Single-residue mutants (V158A, R163T, or D166E) were generated using site-directed PCR mutagenesis using complementary primers (Table S2, Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc.) and Q5 High-Fidelity PCR Kit (New England Biolabs). Plasmid DNA was sequence-verified for each HLA α-chain mutant.

Recombinant HLA production.

Wildtype (WT) A*01:01 or single-residue mutants (SRMs) of A*01:01 (V158A, R163T, D166E) were produced in-house as described.96–98 BL21 (DE3) E. coli chemically competent cells were transfected with expression plasmids for human β2m, WT A*01:01 α-chain, or A*01:01 SRMs. Expressed recombinant proteins were extracted from inclusion bodies and refolded by dilution of the α-chain and β2m into refolding buffer containing excess peptide (VTEHDTLLY, Biosynth Gardner, MA).99 The A*01:01 WT and A*01:01 SRM complexes were purified by ion exchange chromatography (DEAE-C) and size-exclusion chromatography, buffer exchanged into PBS, concentrated, and stored at 4°C. For experiments requiring biotinylated HLA, the proteins were enzymatically biotinylated with BirA enzyme (Avidity), then purified by ion-exchange and size-exclusion chromatography.

Generation of HLA-coated microbeads.

Custom HLA cytometric bead arrays were generated as previously described88 using 4 μm and 5 μm carboxy functionalized array kits (Spherotech, PAK-4067–8K and PAK-5067–10K), conjugated with standard polymeric Streptavidin (SA, Southern Biotech), or monomeric streptavidin (monoSA, Sigma). SA or monoSA was buffer-exchanged using PD-10 columns (Cytiva) into PBS and diluted to 2 mg/mL. To conjugate SA or monoSA to unlabeled 4 and 5 μm Spherotech beads, the beads (1×108) were isolated by centrifugation (3000g, 5min) and resuspended in 0.5 mL of 2 mg/mL of SA or mono SA. 0.5 mL of 6 mM 1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) in 0.05 M MES buffer pH 5.0 (Pierce) was added and the reaction mixture was rotated at RT overnight. Following incubation, the reaction was quenched with 0.1 mL of 1 M tris pH 8.0. The beads were washed twice in 1 mL PBS and resuspended at 1×108/mL in PBS with 1% BSA and 0.005% NaN3 and stored at 4°C. Recombinant, biotinylated HLA (see above) was incubated with microbeads conjugated with either SA or monoSA (1×108 microbeads/mL) at 1 mg/mL overnight at 4°C. The HLA-loaded beads were washed twice in 1 mL PBS and resuspended at 1×108/mL in PBS with 1% BSA and 0.005% NaN3 and stored at 4°C until used. Standard SA-HLA coated beads were used in all assays except Ab inhibition studies (see below), which used monoSA-HLA coated beads to minimize non-specific inhibition due to HLA packing density.

HLA cytometric bead arrays.

Commercial (OneLambda, ThermoFisher) or custom (see above) HLA-coated microbeads were incubated for 15 min with analytes (single IgG1 rmAbs, plasma/serum, or appAbs) diluted in PBS or PBS with 1% BSA. Following incubation and washing, the beads were incubated with anti-human IgG::PE or anti-IgG::AF488 (2.5 μg/mL, Southern Biotech) in PBS with 1% BSA for 15 min. Alternatively, samples were incubated with human IgG subclass-specific secondary Abs at 1.25 μg/mL in PBS with 1% BSA for 15 min: anti-IgM::AF488 (Southern Biotech, #2020–30), anti-IgA::PE (Southern Biotech, #2050–09), anti-IgG1::PE (Southern Biotech, #9054–09), anti-IgG2::PE (Southern Biotech, #9070–09), anti-IgG3::FITC (Southern Biotech, #9210–02), anti-IgG4::PE (Southern Biotech, #9200–09). Beads were washed, resuspended in PBS and analyzed on either a CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences) or LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed in FlowJo v10.8.1 (BD). The gMFI of Ab bound to the different HLA allele-specific microbeads were calculated. Anti-influenza human IgG1 rmAbs88 were used as negative controls. The mean gMFI of the negative controls (see Figure S3A and Table S3) was subtracted from each allele-specific gMFI to obtain gMFI minus background (net gMFI). To select the minimum threshold for specific binding to HLA beads, the net gMFI of rmAb binding to three representative HLA alleles (A*01:01, A*02:01, and A*24:02) was compared to the L-SPR-calculated KD of binding by the rmAb to each HLA allele. As 100% of tested rmAbs with an allele-specific net gMFI ≥40,000 at 10 μg/mL had measurable allele-specific binding (KD < 4.3 μM) by L-SPR, specific binding to the HLA beads (for clustering analyses) was defined as a net gMFI ≥40,000.

Binding of Abs to HLA-A*01:01 mutants.

MonoSA-conjugated microbeads loaded with WT A*01:01 or A*01:01 SRMs (see above) were stained with analytes (rmAbs, appAbs, or sera/plasma) at multiple concentrations, and the gMFI of analyte binding to the beads displaying A*01:01 WT or A*01:01 SRMs was determined. The half maximal effective concentration (EC50) of rmAb binding to each A*01:01 SRM was calculated using the sigmoidal, 4PL, x=concentration model in GraphPad Prism, with constraints (bottom = 0, top = 100). The gMFI of rmAb binding to A*01:01 SRM-coated beads was normalized to the maximum gMFI according to the following formula: %maximumgMFI=(gMFIofsample/highestgMFIforgivenprotein)×100%. To ensure accurate curve fitting, prozone points were omitted from the analysis. EC50 values were calculated from at least two technical replicates for each experiment. Serum anti-HLA Ab activity was interpolated from a standard curve using the pan-HLA Class I-reactive Ab W6/32 (Biolegend, 15.6 ng/mL to 2000 ng/mL) binding to WT A*01:01 and mutant HLA A*01:01 beads and corrected for dilution factor. The resulting standard curves were each fitted to a sigmoidal, 4PL, x=concentration curve. Each serum sample was initially assayed at a 1:500 dilution. In cases where the interpolated value for a given sample was outside of the vertical range of the standard curve, the serum sample was retested at a higher (1:4000, 1:8000, or 1:12000) dilution.

Chimeric rmAb inhibition assays.

Mouse IgG1 Fc chimeric (c) Abs (cE07 and cL02) were generated by fusing the human IgVH domain of E07 or L02 to the mouse IgG1 constant region. The human IgVL domain of E07 or L02 was then fused to either the mouse constant region kappa (for L02) or lambda (for E07) (Sino Biological) and the chimeric IgH and IgL proteins were co-expressed in 293 Freestyle cells. For Ab competitive inhibition analyses, monoSA-conjugated beads loaded with A*01:01 were produced (see above) then diluted in PBS alone (non-blocked beads) or diluted in PBS containing 5 μg/mL recombinant mouse chimeric cE07 or cL02 Abs (blocked beads) and then incubated for 10 min at RT. Analytes, including test human rmAbs (from subjects N006 and U084), polyclonal appAbs, or polyclonal serum/plasma (from subject N006 and transplant recipients in Cohort 1 and 2), were added at varying concentrations to the blocked and unblocked beads and bead suspensions were incubated for an additional 10 min. The beads were washed in PBS, stained for 10 min with anti-human IgG Fc:PE (multi-species absorbed, Southern Biotech) diluted in PBS with 1% BSA, then washed and analyzed as described above. Percent inhibition of binding to the beads was calculated as follows: (unblockedgMFI–blockedgMFI)/(unblockedgMFI–backgroundgMFI)×100. All negative percent (%) values in analyses were set to 0. Percent inhibition values were calculated from at least two technical replicates for all experiments.

Selection of U084 rmAbs for chimeric inhibition assay.

The A*02:01-specific rmAbs (n=44), which were cloned from B cells isolated from the spleen of subject U084, were screened against a panel of HLA-A alleles (OneLambda, ThermoFisher) to determine the reactivity of these Abs against third-party HLA-A alleles. Three of these rmAbs, which bound with high MFIs to A*01:01, were selected for chimeric inhibition experiments.

X-ray crystallography and CryoEM sample preparation.

HLA/Fab complexes were generated by mixing recombinant, non-biotinylated HLA-A*01:01 with either one Fab (1:1 molar ratio for E07/A*01:01, on ice) or with two Fabs (1:1:1 for L02/M07/A*01:01). Complexes were purified into PBS using size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex S200 column. Complexes were concentrated and prepared for subsequent structural studies.

X-ray crystallization, data collection, structure determination, and refinement.

Purified E07/A*01:01 complexes were concentrated to 9.8 mg/mL and screened for crystallization conditions against commercially available kits using a Mosquito liquid handling robot (SPT Labtech). Crystallization conditions were optimized, and diffraction quality crystals were grown in 1.015 M sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate, 0.1 M sodium cacodylate pH 6.5 at a 1:1 ratio of protein to precipitating agent. Crystals were cryo-protected in crystallization solution supplemented with 20% (v/v) ethylene glycol and frozen. Diffraction data was collected at SER-CAT beamline 22-ID and processed with HKL2000.100 Data analysis was performed at UAB. Crystals exhibited a pseudo-cubic symmetry but had true C2 symmetry. Initial phases were solved by molecular replacement with PHASER101 using prior models of HLA (PDB ID: 6AT9),102 Ab domains: VH (PDB ID: 6PZH),103 VL (PDB ID: 4HK0),104 CH1 (PDB ID: 6AZZ).105 After identifying a trimeric assembly of HLA-Fab complexes, the assembly was used to find three additional copies of the assembly, yielding an asymmetric unit that contained 12 copies of HLA and 12 copies of the Fab. Models were iteratively rebuilt with COOT106 and refined with phenix.refine.107 Data collection and refinement statistics are provided (see Table S6). Though 12 copies of each protein could be identified in the asymmetric unit, domain α3 for some HLA and β2m had degenerate density following refinement due to high B-factors, likely a result of crystal packing, and were thus removed from the final model. After removal, electron density from Fo-Fc difference maps showed density for the additional domains, confirming their presence in the lattice. The final model contains six copies of the complete HLA-Fab complex, six additional copies of the Fab, three copies of the HLA domain with α3 removed, and two copies of β2m. Structure factors and final refined coordinates were deposited in the PDB (8T7R).

CryoEM grid preparation and data collection.

The L02/M07/A*01:01 complex was concentrated to 3.04 mg/mL in PBS. Grids for cryoEM were prepared by placing 3mL of solution on a 2/1–3T C-flat grid (R2/1 thick carbon, 300 mesh, Protochips), which was plunged into liquid ethane with a Mark IV Vitrobot. Grids were clipped and transferred to a G3 FEI-Krios electron microscope (La Jolla Institute for Immunology) equipped with a Gatan Bio-quantum energy filter with a 20eV slit. Images were detected on a K3 camera in counting mode (not super-resolution). The electron beam intensity was ~15e−/pixel*s and 50 fractions were collected for every image, giving the specimen 1e/A*A*frame. The beam size was 900nm, allowing for collection of 6 images per hole in the sample.

CryoEM data processing, model building and refinement.

CryoEM data processing was performed by the UAB CryoEM Facility. The images were pre-processed with Warp108 to correct for movement, CTF parameters were estimated, and an initial set of particles was picked. CryoSPARC v4 was used for data processing.109 Pre-processing was repeated using patch motion correction and CTF estimation. From 14,572 exposures, blob picking and 2D classification yielded 93,263 preliminary particles for Topaz training (Figure S7A).110 Topaz autopicked 804,662 particles, which were initially downsampled to 2.64Å/pix and cleaned via 2D classification. The remaining particles were classified into four classes using ab-initio 3D reconstruction. This was done in quadruplicate for three iterations, keeping any particles that contributed to a good class. 252,746 particles from 3D classes corresponding to the L02/M07/A*01:01 complex were kept. After reverting to the original 0.66 Å/pix particles, these were refined using non-uniform refinement to a resolution of 3.14Å (Figure S7B–C).111 To initiate atomic model building, AA sequences were input to ColabFold via ChimeraX to generate AlphaFold predicted models.112–114 M07 and L02 were differentiated based on AAs with bulky side chains present in either, but not both, which could be clearly identified in the reconstruction. AlphaFold initial models were rigid-body fitted to the reconstruction using ChimeraX and refined in real space using Coot (Figure S7D).106 Phenix was used for global real-space refinement and model validation.115

Structural analyses.

Properties of Fab/HLA binding interfaces were analyzed using UCSF ChimeraX.113 Interacting residues at the Fab:HLA interface were identified using a heavy-atom (non-hydrogen atom) distance cutoff of ≤5 Å.45 Epitopic residues were defined as those with an interatomic distance between a heavy atom in the Fab and a heavy atom in the HLA complex ≤5 Å or any residues in the HLA complex with solvent-accessible surface area that was buried in the interface between the Fab and HLA-A*01:01. Hydrogen bonds were identified using UCSF ChimeraX hbond command with default criteria.113 In silico methods were used to observe interactions of Fab E07 with HLA complexes harboring mutations V158A, R163T, or D166E. Individual mutants were modeled with Coot. Complexes were subjected to the molecular dynamics energy minimization routine in YASARA.116

Modeling of rmAbs with alternate HLA-peptide complexes.

The RCSB PDB was searched for models corresponding to allele HLA-A*01:01 α-chain. Coordinates were downloaded for PDBIDs: 1W72,117 3BO8,118 4NQV,119 4NQX,119 5BRZ,120 5BS0,120 6AT9,102 8XKE,121 8YIV,122 8YJ2.122 The ten structures harbor nine different peptides ranging in length from nine to twelve amino acids in length. HLA α-chain residues 1–181 and the associated peptide were extracted from each PDB. HLA α-chain residues were aligned with the aid of COOT106 to α-chain residues in the HLA-E07 and HLA-L02-M07 structures individually. Coordinates of E07, or L02 and M07 from the aligned structures were combined with each corresponding HLA α-chain and peptide. Hybrid structures were energy minimized with YASARA 116 and analyzed for proximity of residues in the Ab footprints. PDB files of simulated Ab-binding to HLA-A*01:01 with variant loaded peptides is deposited on Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.15699403).

Structural and alignment-based visualization of epitopic residue properties.

Data is visualized by demonstrating A*01:01 α-chain AA residues (top row, residues numbered 1–274) in each structurally defined HLA-A*01:01 epitope bound by each rmAb. The epitopic HLA-A*01:01 residues are aligned with other HLA-A alleles, including self-alleles A*24:02 and A*30:01 and third-party HLA alleles. In alignment panels, AA are colored based on chemical properties (yellow=aromatic, red=acidic, blue=basic, aliphatic=orange, polar=green). Identical residues between the A*01:01 donor allele and both self-alleles (0 mismatches or 0MM are shown as dots (“.”)). Residues mismatched between self, donor or third-party alleles are indicated with the one letter AA code. Residues double-mismatched against both self-alleles (2MM, red) or single-mismatched (1MM) against one self-allele (A*24:02, orange or A*30:01, yellow) are indicated. HLA alleles bound by the rmAb (gMFI ≥40,000 at 10 μg/mL) are bolded. Heatmap panels demonstrate structural features of epitopic residues: those involved in hydrogen bonds with the rmAbs, the 0MM, 1MM or 2MM mismatch characterization of A*01:01 epitopic residues, and the amount of surface area of each epitopic residue that is buried by rmAb binding.

Analysis of HLA residue properties.

For consistency across structural models, a previously reported structure of HLA-A*01:01 (PDB: 3BO8)118 was used for calculations of HLA-A*01:01 residue solvent accessibility in the unbound state by PISA123 and Ellipro scores for residue protrusion.124 Physicochemical mismatches were compared as previously described125 for hydrophilicity and electrostatic mismatch, and AA substitution similarity scores were used for dissimilarity calculation.126 The AH-PBG region was defined as the HLA-A*01:01 α-chain α1 domain residues 50–85 and α2 domain residues 138–175, which frame the peptide-binding groove (PBG).50 To calculate residue distances from the center of the PBG, the previously reported HLA-A*01:01 (PDB: 3BO8)118 structure was used and the center of the PBG was defined as the α-carbon of the 5th residue (T5) in the peptide nonamer (EADPTGHSY) presented within the PBG. Distances were calculated between the peptide T5 residue α-carbon and the α-carbon of each AA in A*01:01 using UCSF ChimeraX.113

A*01:01-typed T cell binding assays.

Purified, cryopreserved human CD3+ T cells that were HLA-typed as A*01:01 / A*01:01 homozygous were obtained from StemCell (Kent, WA). T cells were thawed and aliquoted to 5,000 cells/well and incubated for 15 min with the single IgG1 A*01:01 rmAbs (E07, L02, M07), W6/32 (anti-HLA positive control, ThermoFisher) or purified human polyclonal Abs (SouthernBiotech) diluted in PBS. Following incubation and washing, cells were incubated with anti-IgG::AF488 in PBS with 1% BSA for 15 min. Cells were washed, resuspended in PBS and analyzed on a Cytoflex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences). Data were analyzed in FlowJo v10.8.1 (BD) and the gMFI of binding by the anti-HLA Abs to the T cells was calculated.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSES

Statistical analyses.

All statistical comparisons were performed in Prism (GraphPad Software). Comparisons of an observed distribution with the expected distribution were performed using the binomial test and the confidence interval of the proportion was calculated using the hybrid Wilson/Brown method. Comparisons of proportions were made using Fisher’s exact test. Following confirmation of distribution normality by the Shapiro-Wilk test, comparisons of group means was made using an unpaired t-test. If distributions were assessed to be non-normal, group ranks were compared via the Mann-Whitney test. Paired analyses were performed using the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test. Comparisons of multiple groups were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test (nonparametric with no matching), the Friedman test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons (nonparametric with matching), or the Brown-Forsythe and Welch ANOVA tests with Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons test (parametric with no matching). Spearman correlations were used to assess association between two nonparametric variables. Nonlinear regression with a sigmoidal four-parameter logistic curve used to fit binding curves, and EC50 values across conditions were compared using the extra sum-of-squares F test. Hierarchical clustering, heatmap plots, and other visualizations were created using R v4.2.0 (R Core Team 2022),127 Matlab (R2020a, The Mathworks Inc., Natick, MA), UCSF ChimeraX v1.5,113 Cytoscape v3.8.2,92 FlowJo v10.8.1 (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ), Prism v10.2.1 (GraphPad Software, LLC., Boston, MA), or Biorender.com (Toronto, Canada).128 All statistical tests performed in this study are indicated in the figure legend.
