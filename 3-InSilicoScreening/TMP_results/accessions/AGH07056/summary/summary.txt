Paper: PMC13159264.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:
- **Temperature Gradient:** The study analyzed microbial communities across a natural thermal gradient ranging from $50^	ext{o}	ext{C}$ to $93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$.
- **Community Shifts:** Distinct community shifts were observed correlating with temperature: moderate-temperature springs ($50–70^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) were dominated by certain phyla; high-temperature springs ($70–90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) enriched specific taxa; and extreme-temperature springs ($	ext{≥}90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) were characterized by distinct, specialized lineages.
- **Taxa Specificity:** Specific phyla showed temperature preferences: e.g., one phylum dominated at $85^	ext{o}	ext{C}$ but was scarce at $93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$; another phylum was found to dominate exclusively at $93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$.
- **Adaptation Trends:** Thermophilic lineages in extreme-temperature environments exhibited genomic streamlining, specialized metabolic functions, and narrower ecological niches, consistent with adaptation to persistent thermal stress.
- **Metabolic Pathways:** Carbon fixation pathways shifted with temperature, moving from partial Calvin-Benson-Bassham (CBB) and 3-Hydroxypropionate (3H) cycles at moderate temperatures to reductive tricarboxylic acid (rTCA) cycles, dicarboxylate-hydroxybutyrate (DH) cycle, and hydroxypropionate-hydroxybutyrate (HH) cycle at extreme temperatures.
- **Nitrogen Metabolism:** Nitrogen metabolism showed progressive simplification with rising temperatures, shifting from diverse processes (like dissimilatory nitrate reduction) at moderate/high temperatures to efficient, nitrogen-retaining pathways (like assimilatory nitrate reduction, ANR) in extreme environments.
- **Genome Adaptation:** Hyperthermophilic lineages showed significantly smaller genome sizes compared to mesophilic phyla, accompanied by a loss of regulatory genes and reduced functional complexity, suggesting energy conservation and enhanced thermal stability.
- **Molecular Markers:** Increased GC content in tRNA and rRNA, particularly in tRNA, was noted in hyperthermophilic phyla, suggesting genomic adaptations for thermotolerance.

Environmental Context:
- **Habitat/Source:** Western Sichuan Plateau hot springs, representing a natural thermal gradient.
- **Isolation Context:** Organisms were isolated from geothermal hot springs spanning a wide thermal range ($50–93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$). The study explicitly analyzed the adaptation of thermophilic and hyperthermophilic microorganisms.

Concise Summary:
Temperature is identified as the primary driver shaping microbial community structure, genome evolution, and metabolic specialization in hot spring environments. The study analyzed communities across a $50–93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$ gradient, revealing clear shifts in dominant phyla and metabolic strategies. Extreme thermophiles ($	ext{≥}90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) are characterized by genomic streamlining, reduced regulatory complexity, and specialized metabolic pathways (e.g., rTCA cycle, ANR), indicative of adaptation to persistent thermal stress and resource limitation. Conversely, moderate-temperature communities exhibit greater metabolic flexibility (e.g., CBB cycle, diverse nitrogen reduction). Genomic adaptations include increased GC content in nucleic acids and reduced genome size in hyperthermophiles, suggesting evolutionary optimization for high-temperature survival. The findings establish a strong link between increasing temperature, increasing thermal specialization, and decreasing niche breadth among the recovered lineages.

Paper: PMC13001362.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:
- Temperature emerged as the strongest driver structuring viral communities, outpacing the effects of salinity, depth, dissolved oxygen, chlorophyll-a, and geography.
- Viral diversity was consistently higher in the northern warmer waters (7.5–10.4 °C), while southern colder sites (4.0–7.4 °C) harbored less diverse and compositionally distinct communities.
- Giant viruses shift from Pandoravirales and AG_04 (Algavirales) at lower temperatures to increasing dominance of IM_01 (Imitervirales) and Prasinoviridae (AG_01) in warmer habitats.
- Prokaryotic virus communities also displayed strong temperature structuring, with additional influence from salinity.
- For giant viruses, 65.1% of total gvOTUs were detected exclusively in samples at higher temperatures, with the majority assigned to the Prasinoviridae (AG_01). In contrast, only 14.3% of gvOTUs were exclusive to colder temperatures, most belonging to the IM_01 family.
- Temperature shifts resulted in distinct community transitions: AG_04 family and Pandoravirales dominated below 6.1 °C, IM_01 family became prominent between 6.4 °C and 7.5 °C, and AG_01 family dominated above 8.9 °C.
- For prokaryotic viruses, community composition differed markedly between colder and warmer samples, and PERMANOVA confirmed that temperature had a statistically significant effect on beta diversity (R² = 0.21, p < 0.05).

Environmental Context:
- Subantarctic Chilean Patagonia fjords, spanning a latitudinal gradient with temperatures ranging from 4 to 10 °C.
