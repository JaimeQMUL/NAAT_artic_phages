Paper: PMC13037465.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:
- No specific optimal or growth temperature ranges were provided for the viral taxa or their hosts.
- The study analyzed samples spanning the late Pleistocene and Holocene, which were characterized by pronounced temperature variations, including periods of extremely cold climate (Last Glacial Maximum) and subsequent warming (Holocene).
- The analysis noted that the abundance of certain viral taxa (e.g., Casjensviridae) was higher during cold glacial periods compared to warm interglacial periods, suggesting a potential association with environmental stress or cold conditions.
- The abundance of certain viral taxa (e.g., Mimiviridae) showed patterns that contrasted with expected temperature gradients, with one genus showing an increase in abundance after a period of warming.
- The study discussed that lower temperatures or reduced UV exposure (e.g., during frozen lake surfaces) can stabilize virions and prolong their persistence in the environment.
- The abundance of certain host lineages (e.g., SAR11 clade members) was associated with cold-adapted strains found in Arctic and Antarctic regions.

Environmental Context:
- Sedimentary ancient DNA (sedaDNA) from five Siberian lakes and three Subarctic/Antarctic marine cores, spanning the late Pleistocene and Holocene.

Concise Summary:
The study utilized sedaDNA from glacial-interglacial transition periods in both lake and marine environments to reconstruct ancient viral communities. While no specific optimal growth temperatures were detailed, the temporal analysis revealed that viral community composition tracked major climatic shifts. Evidence suggests that certain viral taxa exhibit differential abundance correlated with temperature changes, such as the increased presence of specific phage families during colder glacial periods. Furthermore, the physical stability of viral particles was noted to be enhanced under conditions of low temperature or reduced UV exposure, suggesting a mechanism for long-term preservation in cold environments. The analysis of host lineages also highlighted associations with cold-adapted strains from polar regions, indicating that the ancient viromes reflect responses to significant, long-term environmental temperature fluctuations.
