Klebsiella pneumoniae is an opportunistic pathogen associated with bovine mastitis and dairy contamination, threatening animal health and food safety. This study addresses iuc3-positive, multidrug-resistant, hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (MDR-hvKP) from dairy sources. Two virulent phages, vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2, were isolated from dairy farm wastewater and characterized as members of distinct genera. They mediate specific lysis by targeting different surface receptors: vB-KP80 recognizes capsular polysaccharide encoded by genes (wzc/wcaJ), whereas vB-KPT2 utilizes lipopolysaccharide and outer membrane proteins encoded and regulated by genes (rfaH/fhuA). The phage cocktail combining both showed superior bactericidal activity over individual phages in pasteurized milk and a murine mastitis model, significantly reducing bacterial loads and alleviating tissue inflammation. Critically, the cocktail exerted receptor-directed evolutionary pressure, promoting resistant mutants with capsule deficiencies from mutations at multiple loci. These mutants displayed marked virulence attenuation, including reduced biofilm formation, impaired host cell adhesion, lost serum resistance, and increased phagocytosis susceptibility. Thus, the cocktail provides dual benefits: direct killing of MDR-hvKP and indirect protection by evolving less virulent resistant populations. This eco-friendly, antibacterial–antivirulence strategy shows promise for controlling bovine mastitis and enhancing dairy product safety.

1 Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae is an opportunistic pathogen that causes severe human infections and poses growing challenges to animal health and food safety (Hartantyo et al., 2020). In dairy production, it is a leading cause of clinical bovine mastitis, resulting in substantial economic losses, reduced milk yield, and compromised product quality (Cheng et al., 2020). Of particular concern is the emergence of multidrug-resistant hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (MDR-hvKP), designated a “priority pathogen” by the World Health Organization (Zheng et al., 2025). Our previous studies demonstrated that MDR-hvKP strains, especially those carrying the iuc3 operon, are increasingly prevalent in large-scale dairy farms. These strains exhibit high resistance to β-lactams, aminoglycosides, and tetracyclines, limiting the effectiveness of conventional antibiotic therapy (Wang et al., 2025). Importantly, the iuc3 operon enhances iron acquisition capacity and contributes to hypervirulence, promoting persistence within the mammary gland and increasing the likelihood of transmission along the farm-to-food chain (Hetta et al., 2025; Amiri et al., 2025). The presence of these strains in raw milk, dairy products, and farm environments therefore represents a dual threat—combining antimicrobial resistance with enhanced virulence—posing significant risks to both food safety and public health (Yang et al., 2025).

Phages, as natural bacterial predators, represent a promising approach for biocontrol and food safety. They specifically recognize bacterial surface receptors, lyse pathogens without disturbing commensal microbiota, and can be applied across the food chain, from livestock treatment to post-harvest decontamination (Garvey, 2022). Compared with antibiotics, phages offer several key advantages, including high specificity and self-amplification at infection sites, and they are generally considered suitable for food applications under regulatory review (Ghosh et al., 2025). Previous studies have demonstrated the strong efficacy of phage therapy against multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae infections in humans, suggesting their potential role in improving dairy cattle health and enhancing food chain safety (Zhang et al., 2025). Furthermore, the use of phage cocktails broadens host range and reduces the likelihood of resistance, thereby maximizing both therapeutic effectiveness and food safety benefits (Chen et al., 2024).

A major hurdle in phage-based control of bacteria is the evolving phage-host dynamics, with pathogens deploying defenses like adsorption blockade, DNA injection inhibition, and phage genome degradation (Costa et al., 2024). In K. pneumoniae, adsorption blockade often arises from altered surface receptors, particularly the capsular polysaccharide (CPS)—a vital phage receptor and virulence factor that shields against immune assaults, including antimicrobial peptides, complement killing, and phagocytosis (Zierke et al., 2025). CPS synthesis relies on the cps gene cluster, featuring key genes such as wzc (tyrosine kinase) and wcaJ (glycosyltransferase) (Xu et al., 2024); mutations here curtail capsule production and virulence. In addition to CPS, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and outer membrane proteins (OMPs) also function as important phage receptors while contributing to membrane integrity, immune evasion, and nutrient uptake (Haudiquet et al., 2024). Alterations in these surface structures may confer phage resistance but often impose fitness or virulence costs, highlighting an evolutionary trade-off in which structures essential for pathogenicity simultaneously render the bacterium susceptible to phage infection.

This study tested the hypothesis that a precisely formulated phage cocktail could not only eliminate susceptible K. pneumoniae but also direct the evolution of resistance toward a less virulent phenotype. Two lytic phages, vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2, were isolated using hypervirulent, iuc3-positive K. pneumoniae from dairy cows as hosts. After characterizing their biological and genomic profiles, we formulated a phage cocktail and evaluated its efficacy against multidrug-resistant, iuc3-positive K. pneumoniae in a murine mastitis model and in fresh milk. The results showed that the cocktail rapidly reduced bacterial loads and triggered a “receptor-directed evolutionary trapping” mechanism. This process compelled escape mutants to undergo genetic alterations in CPS, LPS, and outer membrane protein loci, reshaping the bacterial surface receptor landscape at the cost of fitness and virulence. Such receptor-mediated evolutionary trade-offs underpin the dual “bactericidal–virulence attenuation” effect of phage cocktail therapy, reinforcing its promise for enhancing animal health and dairy product safety.

2 Materials and methods

2.1 Phage isolation and purification

Wastewater samples were collected from a dairy farm in Jiangsu Province, China, where cows were suffering from mastitis. Samples were diluted in Saline Magnesium (SM) buffer, centrifuged at 10,000× g for 10 min, and filtered through 0.22 μm membranes. Filtrates were mixed with logarithmic-phase host iuc3-positive K. pneumoniae (KP80 and KPT2, 1:5, v/v) and incubated at 37 °C, 150 rpm for 16–24 h. Enrichment was repeated 3–4 times. Phages were detected using spot assays and double-layer agar plating. Individual plaques were picked and purified through at least three rounds of plaque isolation to obtain homogenous phage stocks, designated vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2, and stored in SM buffer at −20 °C. Phage titers were determined via the double-layer agar method.

2.2 Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Purified phage suspensions (≥109 PFU/mL) were adsorbed onto carbon-coated copper grids (300 mesh) for 5 min, negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid (pH 7.2) for 60 s, and air-dried. Phage morphology was examined using a H-7650 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan).

2.3 Host range determination

The spot assay was used to evaluate the lytic spectrum of phages against 8 iuc3-positive strains and 31 other KP strains from dairy cows. The strains included KP80 (ST215-KL16), KP23 (ST776-KL3), KPT2, KPTP12, KPT4, KPTP15 (ST636-KL126), KPTP1 (ST35-KL22), and KPTP11 (ST35-KL108) (Wang et al., 2025). Briefly, 10 μL of phage suspension (108 PFU/mL) was spotted onto the center of double-layer agar plates containing the bacterial lawns. After air-drying, the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h, and the lytic activity was then examined.

2.4 Optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI)

Host bacteria were co-cultured with phages at varying MOIs (0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, and 100) for 5 h in an incubator shaker set at 37 °C and 180 rpm. Phage titers were determined using the double-layer agar method to identify the optimal MOI.

2.5 One-step growth curve

Bacterial culture in the log phase (5 × 108 CFU/mL) was infected with phages at the optimal MOI in 10 mL fresh LB broth and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C to maximize phage adsorption. The mixture was centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 4 °C, 10 min) to remove non-adsorbed phages through the supernatant. The pellet was washed twice with pre-warmed LB broth and resuspended in 10 mL fresh LB medium. The cultures were subsequently incubated in an incubator shaker set at 37 °C and 180 rpm. Culture samples were collected every 10 min for 120 min to determine phage titers. The burst size was calculated using the following formulae: Burst size = Initial bacterial concentration (PFU/cell)/Final phage titer (PFU/mL). The experiment was performed in triplicate.

2.6 Temperature and pH stability of phages

Phages vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2 (1 × 109 PFU/mL) were incubated in SM buffer at 4 °C, 25 °C, 37 °C, 50 °C, 60 °C, 70 °C, and 80 °C for 30 min and 60 min to determine their temperature stability. The titers of the surviving phages were measured using the double-layer agar method (in triplicate). Phages vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2 were also exposed to PBS buffer at pH 2∼12 (37 °C, 60 min), followed by titer determination to assess their pH stability.

2.7 Phage adsorption assay

The adsorption kinetics of phages vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2 were determined using the wild-type K. pneumoniae strain KP80 and its spontaneously derived resistant mutants (Δwzc/wcaJ, ΔrfaH, and ΔfhuA). Phages (1 × 107 PFU/mL) were mixed with mid-logarithmic phase bacterial cultures (1 × 108 CFU/mL) at a MOI of 0.1 in LB broth and incubated at 37 °C. Aliquots were withdrawn at 0, 5, 10, 20, and 30 min post-infection, immediately diluted in SM buffer, and centrifuged to remove bacterial cells along with adsorbed phages. The titers of unabsorbed free phages in the supernatants were quantified using the double-layer agar. The adsorption rate was calculated as follows: Adsorption percentage = [1 - (PFU at time t/PFU at time 0)] × 100%.

2.8 In vitro bactericidal assay in milk

Pasteurized milk inoculated with logarithmic-phase K. pneumoniae KP80 (1 × 108 CFU/mL) was treated with individual phages (vB-KP80 or vB-KPT2) or a 1:1 (PFU:PFU) phage cocktail (each at 108 PFU/mL). The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C, and bacterial counts were determined by serial dilution on LB agar at 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 h post-inoculation.

2.9 In vivo mammary infection model

24 pregnant ICR mice (gestation day 21) were randomly assigned to four groups: Control (PBS), single phage (vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2) and phage cocktail. At 4–6 days postpartum, pups were temporarily separated from their dams. Dams were anesthetized (Zoletil 50, 25 mg/kg) and disinfected with 75% ethanol. Under a stereomicroscope, K. pneumoniae (50 μL/nipple, 108 CFU/mL) was administered via the mammary duct using a microinjection syringe. In the phage treatment groups, mice were infected similarly and subsequently treated with either a phage cocktail (titer ratio 1:1, 50 μL/nipple, 108 PFU/mL) or single phages (50 μL/nipple, 108 PFU/mL). After 24 h, mammary tissues and blood samples were aseptically collected. Tissues were homogenized in PBS, serially diluted, and plated on LB agar for bacterial enumeration (Zhao et al., 2021).

2.10 Histopathological and inflammatory cytokine analysis of mammary tissue

Mammary tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24–48 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4–5 μm) were cut, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) following standard procedures. Stained sections were examined under a light microscope to assess inflammatory cell infiltration, alveolar structure, and tissue damage. For cytokine analysis, total RNA was extracted from frozen mammary tissues using an RNA extraction kit (TIANGEN DP430), and cDNA was synthesized for RT-qPCR analysis of TNF-α and IL-6. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with β-actin as the reference gene (Kouadjo et al., 2007).

2.11 Mutations and receptor analysis of phage-resistant strains in vivo

In the mouse mammary infection model, the development of phage resistance was monitored at days 1, 2, and 4 post-treatment. Mice (n = 3 per group) were euthanized, and their mammary tissues were processed (homogenization, serial dilution, plating on LB agar) for bacterial quantification. The total K. pneumoniae count for each group was derived from the sum of colonies from all three mice. Subsequently, random colonies from this total bacterial population were screened for phage resistance via a spot assay against vB-KP80, vB-KPT2, or the cocktail, allowing for the determination of resistance phenotypes and the calculation of the resistant mutant isolation rate. Whole-genome sequencing was performed on both the wild-type KP80 and its phage-resistant mutant. Paired-end libraries with an insert size of 300 bp were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X-Ten platform (Illumina, USA), achieving a minimum coverage of 250 × per strain. Raw sequencing data were quality-assessed using FastQC (v0.11.9), followed by adapter trimming and quality filtering using Trimmomatic (v0.39). Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) between the resistant mutant and the wild-type strain were identified and annotated for synonymous and non-synonymous mutations using Snippy (v3.0) with default parameters, to determine potential phage receptor targets.

2.12 Biofilm formation assay

Biofilm formation was assessed in 96-well plates. Briefly, 180 μL of TSB supplemented with 1% glucose was inoculated with 20 μL of exponential-phase bacterial suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 24, 48, or 72 h. Wells were washed with PBS, fixed with 95% ethanol (20 min), and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (15 min). After washing and air-drying, bound dye was solubilized with 33% acetic acid (200 μL, 10 min), and absorbance was measured at 570 nm.

2.13 Virulence assessment in a murine model

To assess virulence, ICR mice (20-30 g) were divided into five groups (n = 6 per group). We inoculated mice via intraperitoneal injection with 300 μL of a bacterial suspension (1 × 107 CFU/mL in PBS) containing either the KP80 wild-type strain or phage-resistant mutants (vB-KP80R, vB-KPT2R, vB-KP80T2R), while the control group received an equal volume of sterile PBS. Mouse mortality was monitored over a 36-h period post-infection.

2.14 Phagocytosis assay

To quantitatively assess phagocytosis, mouse RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 106 cells per well. The cells were infected with either the KP80 wild-type strain or its phage-resistant mutants (vB-KP80R, vB-KPT2R, vB-KP80T2R) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100:1. After incubating at 37 °C for 20 min, remove extracellular bacteria by washing the cells three times with DMEM. The macrophages were then lysed with PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100. The lysates were serially diluted, plated on culture agar, and incubated for colony-forming unit (CFU) enumeration to determine the number of internalized bacteria.

2.15 Cell adhesion assay

The cell adhesion assay was performed using bovine mammary epithelial cell line (MAC-T). Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 106 cells per well. The cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then inoculated with the KP80 strain or phage-resistant mutants (vB-KP80R, vB-KPT2R, vB-KP80T2R) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100. After incubation at 37 °C for 1.5 h, the cells were washed with DMEM to remove non-adherent bacteria and lysed with 200 μL of 0.5% Triton X-100 per well. The lysates were serially diluted, plated on culture agar, and incubated for colony-forming unit (CFU) enumeration.

2.16 Serum killing assay

The serum resistance of K. pneumoniae strains was evaluated using fresh bovine serum. Log-phase bacteria were adjusted to ∼106 CFU/mL in PBS, and 100 μL of suspension was mixed with 900 μL of fresh bovine serum (active or heat-inactivated as control). Mixtures were incubated at 37 °C, and aliquots were taken at 0, 1, 2, and 3 h, serially diluted, and plated on LB agar for CFU enumeration.

2.17 Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Significant differences between groups were determined using the two-tailed nonparametric Student's t-test. The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

3 Results

3.1 Isolation and morphological characteristics of bacteriophages vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2

Phages vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2 were isolated from dairy wastewater using iuc3-positive K. pneumoniae strains KP80 and KPT2 isolated from dairy cows as host bacteria. Phage vB-KP80 formed large, clear plaques surrounded by translucent halos, suggesting that it encodes a depolymerase (Fig. 1A). In contrast, phage vB-KPT2 produced smaller, clear plaques without visible halos (Fig. 1B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that vB-KP80 possesses a polyhedral head with a diameter of 55 ± 5 nm and a short, non-contractile tail, morphologically resembling members of the former family Podoviridae (now integrated within the class Caudoviricetes) (Fig. 1C). 3.3 Genomic features and phylogenetic classification of phages vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2

Genome sequencing revealed that vB-KP80 possesses a linear double-stranded DNA genome of 41,700 bp with a GC content of 52.5%, encoding 53 predicted open reading frames (ORFs). Among these, 42 ORFs could be assigned to functional modules related to structural proteins, DNA replication and transcription, DNA packaging, phage metabolism, and lysis, whereas the remaining 11 ORFs included 7 hypothetical phage proteins and 4 putative proteins of unknown function (GenBank: PX404940). In contrast, the genome of vB-KPT2 is substantially larger, consisting of 144,096 bp with a GC content of 44.5% and encoding 249 ORFs. Of these, 70 ORFs were annotated with known functions, while 179 encoded 95 phage-related proteins and 84 hypothetical proteins (GenBank: PX413393). Importantly, neither vB-KP80 nor vB-KPT2 harbored genes associated with antibiotic resistance, virulence, or lysogeny, indicating that both represent strictly lytic phages (Fig. 2A). Comparative genomic analysis highlighted distinct organizational features between the two phages. The genome of vB-KP80 is relatively compact, encoding head and tail proteins highly similar to those of phage KN4-1 but lacking genes for an RNA polymerase and nucleotide metabolism, suggesting a dependency on the host for transcription and replication. Moreover, the depolymerase gene of vB-KP80 shared only 43% nucleotide similarity with the corresponding gene in phage KN4-1, implying that the enzyme may target a different capsular polysaccharide type. In contrast, vB-KPT2 represents a large, complex phage genome, encoding a complete set of RNA and DNA replication and nucleotide metabolism genes, supporting greater genomic autonomy. Notably, its tail fiber protein genes differ substantially from those of its closest relative KpS8, suggesting that vB-KPT2 may exhibit a distinct host range and lytic spectrum compared to KPS8 (Fig. 2C).

The efficacy of the phage cocktail was further validated in a murine mastitis model. Mice were infected with K. pneumoniae KP80 to establish mastitis and then treated with the phage cocktail for 48 h. 3.5 Phage cocktail pressure induces receptor-specific resistance mutations

On days 1, 2, and 4 post phage therapy, K. pneumonias trains were isolated from mammary gland tissues of mice (n = 3 per group) and assessed for phage resistance. The results indicated that the isolation rate of strains resistant to a single phage exhibited an initial increase followed by a decline. On day 1, the isolation rates of strains resistant to phages vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2 were 2% (1/50) and 4% (2/50), respectively. On day 2, these rates increased to 14% (7/50) and 18% (9/50), respectively. However, on day 4, the rates decreased to 3% (1/30) and 7% (2/30), respectively. Notably, the cocktail treatment group showed a lower propensity to induce resistant strains. No resistant strains were isolated on day 1 (0/50) or day 4 (0/30), and only one resistant strains (2%, 1/50) were isolated on day 2 (Fig. 4A). We observed that the resistant isolates exhibited significantly altered phenotypes, including reduced colony size and decreased viscosity. Whole-genome sequencing identified distinct genetic features associated with resistance. In resistant isolates treated with phage vB-KP80, mutations were primarily located in the wcaJ and wzc genes, which are essential for capsular polysaccharide biosynthesis. In contrast, resistant isolates treated with vB-KPT2 carried mutations in fhuA and rfaH, encoding an outer membrane receptor protein and a lipopolysaccharide expression regulator, respectively. Notably, the phage-resistant isolates obtained from the cocktail treatment group carried mutations in all four loci (wcaJ, wzc, fhuA, and rfaH), suggesting that phage cocktail therapy exerts stronger evolutionary pressure, forcing bacteria to accumulate mutations at multiple gene loci to evade phage recognition (Fig. 4C). Specifically, vB-KP80 phage-resistant strain vB-KP80R (80R), resistance was conferred by a G→A substitution at nucleotide position 1223 in wzc, resulting in a missense mutation (arginine to lysine at amino acid position 408), and a G→T substitution at position 1291 in wcaJ, leading to a premature stop codon (glutamate to stop codon at position 431). In the vB-KPT2 phage-resistant strain vB-KPT2R (T2R), a G→A mutation at nucleotide position 11 in rfaH caused gene truncation (tryptophan to stop codon at position 4), while an A→T transversion at position 601 in fhuA introduced a premature termination codon (lysine to stop codon at position 201). Notably, the strain exhibiting resistance to the phage cocktail (vB-KP80T2R) concurrently harbored both sets of the aforementioned mutations (Fig. 4D).

We further confirmed this phenomenon through phage adsorption assays. As shown in Fig. 4E, phage vB-KP80 efficiently adsorbed to wild-type K. pneumoniae, with over 95% adsorption within 10 min. However, its adsorption capacity was significantly inhibited in the capsule-deficient strain 80R (<20% adsorption within 30 min), while the LPS and outer membrane protein-deficient strain T2R remained unaffected. In contrast, the adsorption kinetics of vB-KPT2 to the wild-type strain were relatively slow, reaching a plateau of approximately 80% after 10 min. Its adsorption capacity was significantly reduced in the LPS and outer membrane protein-deficient strain T2R, decreasing to 40% after 30 min, but remained normal in the capsule-deficient strain 80R. These results indicate that wzc/wcaJ serve as receptor targets for vB-KP80, while rfaH/fhuA are the receptor targets for vB-KPT2. Mutations at these loci lead to altered expression of CPS, LPS, and outer membrane proteins, thereby impairing phage adsorption.

3.6 Phage resistance confers a virulence fitness cost, attenuating pathogenicity

To evaluate whether phage resistance affects the biological fitness of K. pneumoniae, we compared the phenotypes of resistant strains with those of the wild-type strain. Growth curve analysis revealed that the growth rates of the different phage-resistant strains were comparable to that of the wild-type strain, indicating that the acquisition of resistance did not significantly influence the proliferative capacity of the strains (Fig. 5A). However, mutations in CPS- and LPS-related genes suppressed biofilm formation, a phenomenon that was particularly pronounced in the multi-phage resistant strain (vB-KP80T2R) (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5B). Correspondingly, in the mouse sepsis model, mice inoculated with the phage-resistant strains exhibited significantly higher survival. With the exception of one mouse that succumbed at 24 h and another at 36 h in their respective groups, all other mice in these groups survived for over 48 h. Furthermore, we found that the multi-phage resistant mutant exhibited a more significant reduction in adhesion to MAC-T epithelial cells compared to the wild-type and single-phage tolerant strains, by approximately 2 log10 CFU/mL (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5D). It was also more susceptible to phagocytosis by RAW264.7 macrophages, with a higher number of intracellular bacteria observed (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5E). Additionally, a bovine serum resistance assay demonstrated that while the wild-type strain KP80 maintained a stable bacterial count within 3 h, the serum resistance of all resistant strains was significantly impaired, with a more pronounced decrease observed in the multi-phage resistant strain (Fig. 5F). These results indicate that phage resistance is associated with a significant reduction in pathogenicity, manifested as diminished biofilm formation, attenuated adhesion to host cells, increased susceptibility to macrophage phagocytosis, and loss of serum resistance.

4 Discussion

In this study, two novel lytic phages, vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2, were isolated from dairy farm wastewater and exhibited good lytic activity against iuc3-positive, multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae. Both phages, classified under the class Caudoviricetes, demonstrated high stability under various pH and temperature conditions. Genomic analysis confirmed the absence of lysogeny, antibiotic resistance, and virulence genes, underscoring their safety for potential application in the dairy industry (Fernández et al., 2017). These properties align with previous reports indicating that strictly lytic phages are preferable for therapeutic use due to a lower risk of horizontal gene transfer and lysogenic conversion (Strathdee et al., 2023). Given these features, vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2 represent promising candidates for controlling foodborne MDR-hvKP in milk and dairy products, thereby mitigating risks to both animal and public health.

The phage cocktail of vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2 exhibited a broader host range and could lyse 87.5% (7/8) of the high virulence iuc3 positive tested strains. This suggests that the cocktail therapy could be more effective than single-phage treatments in combating infections caused by diverse bacterial strains (Mengshi et al., 2024). In pasteurized milk, the cocktail achieved significant bacterial reduction within 24 h and sustained suppression for up to 36 h, indicating its suitability for direct application in milk and dairy processing settings. Furthermore, the translucent halos observed around vB-KP80 plaques suggest depolymerase activity, which may aid in degrading bacterial capsules or biofilms-offering additional potential for improving pathogen control in milk storage and processing environments (Islam et al., 2024).

In a murine mastitis model, the phage cocktail not only significantly reduced bacterial loads but also alleviated tissue damage, confirming its in vivo therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, phage treatment favorably modulated host immune responses without inducing adverse effects (Liu et al., 2021). These results underscore the dual potential of this phage therapy-targeting hvKP infections and modulating inflammatory responses to control mammary infections, thereby minimizing the risk of contaminated milk entering the food supply and improving milk quality (Ranveer et al., 2024).

Further mechanistic analyses demonstrated that vB-KP80 targets capsular polysaccharide (wzc/wcaJ), while vB-KPT2 binds to receptors related to lipopolysaccharide (rfaH) and outer membrane proteins (fhuA) (Nang et al., 2024; Qiu et al., 2024). This complementary receptor usage underlies the cocktail's enhanced efficacy and underscores the importance of incorporating receptor diversity in designing phage-based strategies against dairy pathogens (Mutalik and Arkin, 2022). Notably, phage cocktail-induced resistance mutations were not limited to CPS biosynthesis genes but also involved genes related to LPS modulation and outer membrane integrity. These genetic alterations collectively account for the observed virulence attenuation: mutations in wzc and wcaJ directly impair CPS synthesis and surface display, a key determinant of immune evasion, serum resistance, and hypervirulence in K. pneumoniae. Simultaneously, mutations in rfaH, a global transcriptional regulator required for full LPS expression, disrupt LPS integrity and downstream virulence gene expression, sensitizing bacteria to host defenses(Klein and Raina, 2019). Furthermore, truncation of fhuA compromises iron uptake and alters outer membrane architecture, negatively impacting bacterial growth under iron-restricted conditions and reducing adhesion or invasion efficiency(Mikael et al., 2003). In summary, while CPS loss remains a primary factor in virulence attenuation, defects in LPS regulation and membrane protein function additively contribute to broader and more severe fitness costs.

Beyond its therapeutic potential, this study offers novel insights into the co-evolutionary dynamics between phages and bacteria. Notably, compared to single-phage treatment, the phage cocktail not only improved bacterial clearance but also elevated the genetic barrier to resistance-requiring simultaneous mutations in multiple, functionally distinct receptor genes for bacterial survival, thereby substantially impeding the emergence of multi-phage-resistant strains (Marchi et al., 2025). This finding is consistent with previous reports that phage cocktails can delay resistance evolution (Costa et al., 2024). Although the development of long-term resistance may be unavoidable, our results further indicate that such resistance incurs substantial fitness costs, likely disadvantaging resistant bacteria in natural environments (Tang et al., 2023). Specifically, multidrug-resistant mutants selected by the cocktail showed marked defects in key virulence traits-including biofilm formation, host cell adhesion, serum resistance, and phagocytosis evasion, collectively leading to severe attenuation of pathogenicity. These observations align with existing studies demonstrating that phage-driven selection can compromise bacterial fitness and virulence both in vitro and in vivo, suggesting a viable strategy for long-term infection control (R.M. M. & A.D. B., 2020). Importantly, these fitness trade-offs are expected to reduce the competitiveness of resistant mutants in real-world settings, including dairy farms and milk processing chains. Increased sensitivity to disinfectants, pasteurization, and host immune defenses likely facilitates their clearance and limits environmental persistence, thereby lowering the risk of bulk tank milk contamination and enhancing dairy safety through an evolutionarily mediated trade-off(García-Anaya et al., 2020). Taken together, these findings suggest that phage therapy should be viewed not merely as a bactericidal intervention but as an “evolutionary steering” strategy capable of redirecting pathogen evolution toward reduced virulence and heightened susceptibility to host immunity, offering a more sustainable framework for infection control.

It should be acknowledged that the present study primarily captures short-term evolutionary outcomes under phage-mediated selection. The stability of capsule-deficient or low-virulence phenotypes in the absence of phage pressure remains to be verified. Future work should systematically evaluate the stability and reversibility of these attenuated phenotypes through long-term passage without selection, serial cultivation in milk or mastitis models, and competitive fitness assays. Additionally, the ecological persistence and transmission risks of resistant mutants should be assessed in vivo. Moreover, the phage cocktail constructed in this study exhibited high efficacy specifically against iuc3-positive multidrug-resistant hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (MDR-hvKP), whereas it showed no significant lytic activity against other KL-type strains lacking the iuc3 gene cluster. Although the current formulation demonstrates high specificity and safety, its applicability may be limited. Therefore, expanding the phage resource library or developing broader-spectrum multi-component formulations is warranted to enhance the overall control efficacy and practical feasibility in complex farm environments.

5 Conclusion

This study shows that the phage cocktail (vB-KP80 and vB-KPT2) exerts a dual-mechanism action against iuc3-positive MDR-hvKP strains: direct bacteriolysis and indirect virulence attenuation. These findings validate its therapeutic potential for controlling infections on dairy farms and highlight the “evolution-steering” strategy of using receptor-diverse phages. This approach not only eliminates bacteria but also drives pathogen evolution toward reduced virulence, offering a sustainable pathway to combat antibiotic resistance and improve food safety. Future work should focus on the long-term stability of attenuated phenotypes and expanding the phage repository. Practical on-farm formulations, such as teat-dip solutions and bulk-tank milk treatments, can be developed for integrated application in mastitis control and milk decontamination.
