Paper: PMC13159264.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:
- **Temperature Gradient:** The study analyzed microbial communities across a natural thermal gradient ranging from $50^	ext{o}	ext{C}$ to $93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$.
- **Community Shifts:** Distinct community shifts were observed correlating with temperature: moderate-temperature springs ($50–70^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) were dominated by certain phyla; high-temperature springs ($70–90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) enriched specific taxa; and extreme-temperature springs ($	ext{≥}90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) were characterized by distinct, specialized lineages.
- **Thermophilic Adaptation:** Lineages found in extreme-temperature environments exhibited clear signs of thermophilic adaptation, including genome streamlining, reduced regulatory complexity, and specialized metabolic strategies.
- **Thermal Specialization:** Deeply branching phyla were predominantly enriched in high- and extreme-temperatures, while shallower-branching groups favored moderate temperatures.
- **Metabolic Adaptation:** Carbon fixation pathways shifted with temperature: moderate temperatures showed utilization of partial Calvin-Benson-Bassham (CBB) and 3-Hydroxypropionate ($3	ext{H}$) cycles; high temperatures favored the Wood–Ljungdahl (WL) pathway; and extreme temperatures utilized specialized autotrophic pathways like reductive tricarboxylic acid (rTCA), dicarboxylate-hydroxybutyrate (DH), and hydroxypropionate-hydroxybutyrate (HH) cycles.
- **Nitrogen Metabolism:** Nitrogen metabolism showed progressive simplification with rising temperatures, shifting from diverse, loss-prone processes (like dissimilatory nitrate reduction, DNR) in moderate/high temperatures to efficient, nitrogen-retaining pathways (like assimilatory nitrate reduction, ANR) in extreme temperatures.
- **Genome Stability:** Genomic adaptations for thermotolerance included increased GC content in tRNA and rRNA, and a shift in amino acid composition towards stability-enhancing residues.
- **Enzymes/Processes:** The study noted the enrichment of specific enzymes and pathways (e.g., sulfur oxidation pathways, rTCA cycle) in hyperthermophilic lineages.

Environmental Context:
- **Habitat/Source:** Western Sichuan Plateau hot springs, representing a natural thermal gradient.
- **Isolation Context:** Organisms were isolated from geothermal hot springs spanning a wide thermal range ($50^	ext{o}	ext{C}$ to $93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$). The study explicitly analyzed the adaptation to persistent thermal stress in these extreme environments.

Paper: PMC13001362.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:
- Temperature emerged as the strongest driver structuring viral communities, outpacing the effects of salinity, depth, dissolved oxygen, chlorophyll-a, and geography.
- Viral diversity was consistently higher in the northern warmer waters (7.5–10.4 °C), while southern colder sites (4.0–7.4 °C) harbored less diverse and compositionally distinct communities.
- Giant viruses shift from Pandoravirales and AG_04 (Algavirales) at lower temperatures to increasing dominance of IM_01 (Imitervirales) and Prasinoviridae (AG_01) in warmer habitats.
- Prokaryotic virus communities also displayed strong temperature structuring, with additional influence from salinity.
- For giant viruses, 65.1% of total gvOTUs were detected exclusively in samples at higher temperatures, with the majority assigned to the Prasinoviridae (AG_01). In contrast, only 14.3% of gvOTUs were exclusive to colder temperatures, most of which belonged to the IM_01 family.
- Temperature shifts resulted in distinct community transitions: AG_04 family and Pandoravirales dominated below 6.1 °C, IM_01 family became prominent between 6.4 °C and 7.5 °C, and AG_01 family dominated above 8.9 °C.
- For prokaryotic viruses, community composition differed markedly between colder and warmer samples.
- PERMANOVA confirmed that temperature had a statistically significant effect on beta diversity in both viral communities (Giant viruses: R² = 0.30, p < 0.05; Prokaryotic viruses: R² = 0.21, p < 0.05).

Environmental Context:
- Subantarctic Chilean Patagonia fjords, spanning a latitudinal gradient with temperatures ranging from 4 to 10 °C.

Concise Summary:
Temperature is identified as the primary ecological driver structuring both giant virus and prokaryotic virus communities in the subantarctic Chilean Patagonia fjords. Higher temperatures (7.5–10.4 °C) correlate with significantly higher viral diversity compared to colder southern sites (4.0–7.4 °C). Community composition shifts are temperature-dependent: giant viruses transition from Pandoravirales/AG_04 at lower temperatures to increased dominance of IM_01 and Prasinoviridae (AG_01) in warmer waters. This temperature dependency is confirmed by statistical analyses (PERMANOVA, R² > 0.20 for both viral groups). The study details distinct community transitions across the temperature gradient, suggesting that viral assemblages are highly sensitive to thermal regimes, which has implications for biogeochemical cycling in this cold marine ecosystem.

Concise Summary:
The organism is a polyextremophilic cyanobacterium, originally isolated from thermal springs, exhibiting thermophilic characteristics. It can grow optimally at 25 °C but demonstrates robust growth and adaptation up to 40 °C, with a reported survival capability up to 50 °C. Under high temperature stress (40 °C), the organism undergoes significant membrane lipid remodeling, characterized by a shift in acyl unsaturation from monounsaturated to saturated fatty acids (e.g., 16:1 $\rightarrow$ 16:0). This adaptation is consistent with a general thermophilic response observed in related cyanobacteria, aiming to counteract increased membrane fluidity. Furthermore, the accumulation of triacylglycerol (TAG) also shifts towards more saturated molecular species at elevated temperatures. These lipidomic changes in both membrane and nonpolar lipids are crucial components of its adaptive strategy to extreme thermal conditions.
