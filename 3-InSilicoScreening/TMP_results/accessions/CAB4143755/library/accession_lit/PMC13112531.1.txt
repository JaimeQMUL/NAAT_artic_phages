The gut virome is a complex ecosystem characterized by the interplay of diverse viral entities, predominantly bacteriophages and eukaryotic viruses. The gut virome has a critical role in human health by shaping microbial community profiles, modulating host immunity and influencing metabolic processes. Different viral metagenomics approaches have revealed the remarkable diversity of the gut virome, showing individual-specific patterns that evolve over time and adapt dynamically to environmental factors. Perturbations in this community are increasingly associated with chronic immune and inflammatory conditions, metabolic disorders and neurological conditions, highlighting its potential as a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target. The early-life gut virome is particularly influential in establishing lifelong health trajectories through its interactions with diet, immune pathways and others, thereby contributing to inflammatory and metabolic regulation. This Review synthesizes current knowledge of gut virome composition, dynamics and functional relevance, critically evaluating evidence distinguishing causal from correlative roles in disease pathogenesis. The interactions of the virome with other microbiome components and host immunity are examined, and emerging translational applications, including phage therapy and biomarker development, are discussed. Integrating these insights while acknowledging methodological challenges provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the complex roles of the gut virome in health and disease.

Introduction

The gut virome is a critical, but under-studied, component of the human microbiome, comprising a diverse community of entities, predominantly bacteriophages, eukaryotic viruses and viroids (small infectious RNAs). This complex viral ecosystem is crucial in shaping the microbial community within the gut, influencing microbial balance, modulating immune responses and contributing to key metabolic functions that influence host health. Advances in metagenomic sequencing and specialized virome profiling techniques have begun to unravel the remarkable diversity of the gut virome, revealing distinct patterns that vary between individuals, change over time and respond dynamically to environmental factors.

Emerging evidence links alterations in the gut virome to autoimmune and inflammatory conditions such as inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), metabolic disorders including obesity and diabetes, and several neurological conditions. These associations highlight the potential of the virome as a diagnostic biomarker and a therapeutic target. Early-life gut virome alterations can influence future health outcomes, and interactions between the gut virome and diet and virome-driven modulation of the resistome and inflammatory pathways have been revealed as important potential lifelong modulators of host health.

Despite growing recognition of the importance of the gut virome, fundamental questions remain unanswered limit our ability to harness viral communities for therapeutic benefit. First, when do virome alterations cause disease versus simply reflect bacterial dysbiosis? Most studies document correlative associations, yet distinguishing causative relationships is essential for developing targeted interventions. Second, why do different diseases show opposing patterns of viral diversity? Some conditions exhibit viral diversity loss whereas others show specific viral expansion, suggesting distinct underlying mechanisms that remain poorly understood. Third, how do methodological choices shape our biological understanding? Different experimental approaches capture fundamentally different aspects of viral activity, potentially creating contradictory narratives about the same biological processes. These gaps are addressed by focusing on three key analytical perspectives: examining causative versus correlative evidence across disease states, identifying shared mechanisms that explain opposing patterns of viral dysbiosis, and evaluating how methodological biases influence biological interpretation. Rather than cataloguing viral diversity, we focus on understanding how and when virome perturbations drive pathological outcomes.

This Review synthesizes current knowledge on the human gut virome and related experimental animal studies where relevant, including composition, dynamics and functional relevance, examining its adaptive relationship with the host microbiota and immune system. We critically evaluate evidence for virome involvement in disease pathogenesis, distinguishing between correlative associations and demonstrated causal mechanisms. We also address the emerging applications of the virome, including phage therapy and biomarker development. By integrating these findings, this Review provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the gut virome and identifying methodological challenges for investigating its complex roles in health and disease.

Composition of the human gut virome

The human gut virome is one of the most diverse and complex viral communities on earth. This viral community includes free bacteriophages (phages), prophages integrated into bacterial genomes, eukaryotic viruses, archaeal viruses and newly discovered virus-like entities such as obelisks1. These diverse viral communities collectively influence microbial population dynamics, metabolic functions, immune responses and potentially host health outcomes.

The gut harbours an exceptionally high concentration of viral particles, estimated at up to 109 to 1010 virus-like particles (VLPs) per gram2,3. Although free phages are often estimated to outnumber bacterial cells by approximately 10:1 (ref. 4), some studies have suggested that the ratio of free phages to bacteria in human stools might be closer to 1:1 or even lower5. This revised understanding recognizes factors such as virion adsorption to bacterial debris, resistant bacteria, or mucus.

Major bacteriophage groups in the gut

Bacteriophages constitute the majority of the gut virome, with several key groups now recognized for their abundance and ecological importance. The gut viral community is primarily composed of DNA bacteriophages, particularly double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) phages of the Caudovirales order (now abolished by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses and reclassified into multiple families based on genomic relationships6), and single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) phages from the Microviridae family and Inoviridae family, the latter constituting a group of filamentous phages that contribute substantially to overall virome composition despite being under-studied. Caudovirales, in particular, are characterized by their tailed structures, which house highly genetically plastic receptor-binding proteins (RBPs) essential for the attachment to bacterial host and host range determination7.

Despite numerous advances in viral metagenomics, most phage sequences recovered from the gut virome remain unclassified, representing what researchers term ‘viral dark matter’8,9. This classification challenge stems from the absence of universal marker genes for viruses10 and the immense, largely uncharacterized diversity of viral genomes11. Metagenomic analyses continue to uncover novel phage families5 beyond the limited community of readily culturable viruses. Large-scale metagenomic mining efforts have expanded known phage diversity, identifying novel phage genomes12 and thousands of putative new phage genera13.

CrAss-like phages

Among tailed phages, the crAss-like phages, classified within the Crassvirales order, have emerged as particularly important, constituting up to 90% of the total viral DNA load in some individuals14–18. First identified through cross-assembly (crAss) of metagenomic data17,18, these phages exhibit several distinctive features. They predominantly infect bacteria of the phylum Bacteroidota (formerly Bacteroidetes), particularly the genus Bacteroides, but potential bacterial hosts can include other bacterial phyla17,19. Their genomes are large (97–192kb), with circular or terminally redundant linear structures, and exhibit high genomic plasticity that enables rapid adaptation through recombination and acquisition of transcription-related genes20. This genetic versatility contributes substantially to the ecological success and widespread distribution of crAss-like phages within the human gut21. The strong association of crAss-like phages with human faecal contamination makes them valuable indicators for microbial source tracking in environmental monitoring22.

Emerging bacteriophage groups

Metagenomic advances have revealed several additional phage groups associated with the human gut, each with unique genomic features and potential health implications. Lak phages represent a clade of megaphages with exceptionally large dsDNA genomes (>540kb) that probably infect bacteria of the genus Prevotella23,24. Their association with Prevotella, a bacterial genus linked to plant-rich diets and improved health outcomes in children25, suggests potential relevance in diet–microbiome interactions. Gubaphage, Flandersviridae, Quimbyviridae, and Gratiaviridae have also been characterized from human faecal samples and are associated with the Bacteroidales order. These clades exhibit specialized genomic features including diversity-generating retroelements targeting defence genes in Quimbyviridae, and host cell wall-modifying enzymes such as HipA-family kinases in Gratiaviridae13,23. Furthermore, the Varidnaviridae order, a group of tailless phages composed mainly of prophages infecting Alphaproteobacteria and Verrucomicrobia, has now been recognized as a minor yet important constituent of the gut virome26.

Beyond bacteriophages: other viral components

Eukaryotic viruses.

Although less abundant than bacteriophages, in the gut, eukaryotic viruses that infect mammalian hosts or other eukaryotes such as fungi or plants can represent both pathogenic and commensal entities whose presence and influence vary according to host genetics and environmental factors, including diet, physical activity and smoking27. Metagenomic analyses show that eukaryotic viruses in the gut include ssDNA Circoviridae, dsDNA Herpesviridae, and RNA viruses from families such as Picobirnaviridae, Alphaflexiviridae, Bromoviridae, Luteoviridae and Virgaviridae28. The gut eukaryotic virome displays extensive interindividual variability driven by host-intrinsic factors. For example, infants exhibit transient enteroviruses and viruses from the family Picornaviridae, whereas adults harbour persistent anelloviruses and adenoviruses27,29. Metagenomic studies of diarrhoeal samples reveal novel eukaryotic viruses, such as divergent astroviruses and noroviruses, which could contribute to up to 40% of paediatric diarrhoeal cases with unknown aetiology30. Disease states can further reshape the eukaryotic virome composition; for example, immunodeficient hosts experience virome expansion dominated by adenoviruses, which can exacerbate mucosal damage and systemic inflammation31, whereas systemic viral reactivation (for example, herpesviruses and polyomaviruses) is observed in malignancies, altering tissue-specific virome profiles32. Beyond viruses that infect human cells, plant-derived viruses, such as the pepper mild mottle virus, are detected in up to 66% of healthy individuals, probably originating from dietary intake33.

Archaeal viruses.

Archaeal viruses, particularly those infecting methanogenic archaea, are an under-studied but essential component of the gut virome and many other ecosystems. These viruses exhibit high genetic diversity with unique adaptation to gut environments. A distinguishing feature is their possession of genes that encode lysin proteins specific for archaeal pseudomurein degradation, crucial for archaeal cell wall interactions and viral release34.

The complex composition of the gut virome suggests multifaceted roles in human health and disease. The high degree of individuality in virome composition suggests that personalized approaches might be necessary when considering therapeutic interventions targeting the virome. As metagenomic technologies advance and reference databases expand, our understanding of this viral ecosystem continues to deepen. The continued exploration of the gut virome promises to reveal new insights into microbial ecology and to potentially transform our approaches to monitoring and modulating human health.

Methods for viral analysis in the gut

The exploration and characterization of the gut virome have substantially advanced with developments in viral detection, including metagenomics, viral enrichment techniques, single-virus genomics (SVG), viral tagging and proximity ligation approaches. Metagenomics enables culture-free analysis of viral genetic material extracted directly from environmental samples using two main strategies: bulk metagenomics and VLP metagenomics35,36. Bulk metagenomics involves sequencing total DNA from gut samples without prior viral enrichment, capturing a broad portion of microbial diversity, including bacteria, fungi and their associated viruses. This approach allows the identification of prophage genomes integrated into bacterial host genomes, facilitating the discernment of phage–host associations37. The increasing application of long-read sequencing technologies has improved the assembly and classification of bacterial metagenomic-assembled genomes38, consequently enhancing prophage identification rates. However, bulk metagenomics often overwhelmingly sequences non-viral nucleic acids, obscuring low-abundance viral sequences39 and limiting its ability to resolve rare viral taxa or specific viral types such as ssDNA40.

Viral enrichment methods, such as VLP extraction, enhance the sensitivity of viral detection by concentrating viruses from samples before sequencing. This method markedly improves the recovery of low-abundance viruses, but generally excludes viral genomes integrated into bacterial genomes41. The under-representation of eukaryotic viruses in the human gut underscores the utility of these technologies, which can be coupled with targeted sequence capture to improve the detection of eukaryote-infecting viruses42. VLP-based sequencing traditionally relies on short-read technologies due to the limited nucleic acid yield after VLP preparation. However, strategies involving the pooling of multiple DNA extractions from the same sample have been applied to enable long-read sequencing of VLP-derived samples, which allows the implementation of hybrid approaches that yield a better representation of viral diversity43. Although all metagenomic strategies can provide broad exploration, results can be influenced by methodological factors such as amplification steps, sequencing technology and bioinformatics pipelines, which can introduce biases into the viral community profile35,44.

SVG extends traditional genomic methodologies by isolating and sequencing viral genomes directly from environmental samples45,46. In SVG workflows, viral particles are first labelled with fluorescent dyes and individually isolated from environmental samples using techniques such as fluorescence-activated sorting or microfluidic systems. The capsid of the sorted virions is disrupted through pH adjustment combined with thermal treatment. The genetic material is then amplified using whole-genome amplification methods45,47. SVG is particularly powerful at revealing the hidden diversity and abundance of viruses that evade detection by conventional methods. For instance, studies have shown that most viral single-amplified genomes identified through SVG are more abundant in global ocean virome datasets than those identified through metagenome-assembled viral genomes or isolates48. Although its application in human gut virome research has so far been limited to a few studies, SVG offers distinct advantages, including minimal sample volume requirements (typically 1ml), making it practical for clinical applications in which sample availability is limited48,49. Applied to human microbiome samples, SVG has successfully revealed abundant uncultured viruses, with studies in saliva demonstrating that most naturally abundant viruses remain uncharacterized by traditional cultivation methods50. The technique overcomes common metagenomic assembly challenges in gut viromes, such as high microdiversity and uneven abundance patterns, which often result in fragmented assemblies and poor recovery of complete viral genomes. By facilitating the analysis of viral microdiversity, SVG enables a deeper understanding of viral evolution, ecology and their interaction dynamics with bacterial hosts, which are crucial for elucidating the biological roles of viruses in various ecosystems51. Given these advantages, we anticipate that SVG will see broader adoption in human virome studies in the coming years.

The application of particle sorting technologies has also allowed viral tagging to pair uncultivated phages with their bacterial hosts by fluorescently labelling virions, enabling their attachment to host cells and subsequent isolation via fluorescence-activated cell sorting. Amplification and sequencing of these host–phage complexes has revealed unique pairings in the human gut, predominantly subject-specific, and has demonstrated cross-reactivity between phages and bacteria from different donors52,53.

Advances in proximity ligation assays, such as meta3C and meta-HiC, have also revolutionized the study of phage–host interactions in complex microbial communities such as the human gut. These methods leverage physical DNA–DNA contacts to reconstruct bacterial genomes and assign phage sequences to their bacterial hosts with high specificity36,54. Conformation capture techniques crosslink DNA within microbial cells, digest it with restriction enzymes, and ligate proximal fragments. Sequencing these ligation products enables de novo assembly of bacterial genomes and scaffolding of phage sequences54. In a pilot study of ten human gut samples, metaHiC mapped over 6,000 phage–bacteria interactions, distinguishing dormant prophages from actively replicating phages based on coverage ratios36.

Non-sequence-based approaches are also useful for analysing gut viromes. For example, live imaging-based approaches enable tracking of phage replication and spread in situ with single-virion resolution. This method can reveal how intestinal tissues rapidly take up virions and quickly disseminate them to extraintestinal sites, thereby enhancing our understanding of phage dynamics55.

Methodological choices shape interpretation

Before examining virome alterations across disease states, we must first establish a critical interpretive framework: the apparent contradictions in virome research often reflect methodological choices rather than biological reality. This framework is essential for understanding why studies of the same disease can reach opposite conclusions.

Methodological choices in viral detection fundamentally influence biological interpretation of gut virome dynamics. VLP-enriched metagenomics captures primarily extracellular viruses and actively replicating phages, with studies showing markedly higher viral abundance and species richness than bulk metagenomics35. This approach emphasizes lytic viral activity and supports ecological models of phage-mediated bacterial control through ‘kill-the-winner’ dynamics. Conversely, bulk metagenomics preferentially detects integrated prophages and captures both extracellular and intracellular viruses, revealing viral–host mutualism and lysogenic conversion as mechanisms of bacterial adaptation56. Studies comparing paired samples demonstrate that VLP and bulk approaches recover distinct viral communities. Bulk metagenomics has been shown to generate an average of 50% more viral contigs in controlled experiments57, and, in some larger cohorts, showed higher recovery, though not significantly different from VLP samples35. By contrast, VLP samples tend to produce viral contigs with higher estimated completeness, averaging 10% complete genomes, whereas bulk samples typically yield 2–3% complete genomes in both infants and adults58. This difference is probably due to the lower proportion of viruses in bulk samples41. Proximity ligation approaches such as metaHiC represent a methodological advance by directly capturing phage–host DNA contacts in situ, enabling dormant prophages to be distinguished from actively replicating phages based on coverage ratios and the establishment of specific causal relationships rather than correlative associations36. On the other hand, SVG reveals low-abundance viruses that constitute a genetic reservoir that is potentially critical during environmental perturbations or dysbiosis, as these rare taxa might become dominant under variable conditions53 (Table 1). The choice of method therefore shapes the biological narrative: VLP enrichment frames viruses as predators, bulk metagenomics emphasizes symbiosis, whereas proximity ligation and single-virus approaches reveal the full spectrum of viral–host interactions (Fig. 1). Future studies should explicitly acknowledge these methodological constraints when interpreting viral ecological roles and employ complementary approaches to construct comprehensive understanding of gut virome dynamics.

Although molecular approaches markedly shape analytical results, the bioinformatics pipelines used for viral genome prediction, taxonomic identification and annotation can drastically influence outcomes. Recognizing these methodological influences enables more accurate interpretation of virome alterations in disease. The most robust findings and promising therapeutic targets are obtained from studies showing consistent patterns across multiple methodologies, whereas methodology-dependent observations might reflect different aspects of the same biological process (Box 1 and Tables 2 and 3).

Virome–bacteria interactions

The complex interplay between bacteriophages and the bacterial microbiota is a critical but often overlooked component of microbial ecology with major implications for human health and disease. Bacteriophages regulate the microbiota through multiple processes, including direct bacterial predation59,60, horizontal gene transfer61 and lysogenic conversion62. Bacteriophages have also been associated with non-hostderived immunity when adhering to the mucus layer and interacting with mucin glycoproteins63 (Fig. 2a). Through predation, lytic phages actively infect and lyse bacterial hosts, depleting specific bacterial populations and modulating bacterial abundance, thereby altering microbial diversity and stability64. However, disruptions in this balance, particularly during blooms of specific lytic phage populations, can also be associated with disease states such as ulcerative colitis65 (Fig. 2b,c). The relationship is bidirectional — whereas phages shape bacterial communities, bacterial evolution to resist phage infection is simultaneously driven by phage predation pressures, leading to dynamic co-evolutionary processes66–68.

Temperate phages are another crucial component of virome–bacteria interactions. These phages integrate into bacterial genomes as prophages, providing bacteria with potentially advantageous genes such as virulence factors, antibiotic resistance genes and metabolic genes, ultimately influencing bacterial fitness and community structure69 (Fig. 2d,e). During intestinal inflammation, prophage induction can substantially alter gut bacterial community composition, exacerbating dysbiosis by shifting phage replication dynamics from lysogenic to lytic cycles, as shown in mouse models70. Multiple environmental and physiological triggers, such as bacterial DNA damage, oxidative stress, antibiotic treatment, inflammation-associated compounds and dietary changes, can influence the transition of prophages from lysogenic to lytic cycles71,72. Common medications, including antibiotics and non-antibiotic pharmaceuticals, such as anti-inflammatory drugs, can trigger prophage induction, altering the gut microbiome composition73. The consequences of prophage induction include a cascade of microbial and immunological effects, notably bacterial cell lysis, reduced microbial diversity, increased horizontal gene transfer, and modulation of immune responses74. These effects collectively contribute to a more dysbiotic state and heightened inflammatory responses in conditions such as IBD, where increased prophage induction correlates with exacerbated disease severity in mouse models70. Understanding these complex interactions could provide new therapeutic avenues for managing microbial dysbiosis in various disease states.

The evolutionary plasticity of gut phages markedly influences their ecological roles and clinical relevance. Phages undergo rapid evolution through multiple mechanisms including point mutations, recombination and modular gene exchange, particularly in RBPs that determine bacterial host specificity75. This genetic flexibility enables phages to adapt to changing bacterial populations and potentially jump between bacterial hosts. Studies have demonstrated that virulent phages accumulate mutations facilitating host jumping in the complex gut microbiota of conventional mice, but not in simpler in vitro systems or mice with limited bacterial diversity76. Single amino acid substitutions in tail fibre genes can be sufficient to enable phages to infect previously insensitive bacterial strains77. Host jumping, facilitated by phages with a broad host range or through intermediate hosts, can dramatically alter microbial community dynamics and might explain sudden shifts in virome composition associated with disease states. Metagenomic analyses show that around 36% of viral clusters in the human gut are associated with multiple bacterial species, facilitating gene flow across phylogenetically diverse bacteria12. The modular structure of phage RBPs supports horizontal transfer as a major evolutionary driver, allowing exchange of functional modules between phages to generate new host specificities78. Furthermore, the acquisition of auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) through horizontal gene transfer further enhances phage adaptability, allowing them to modulate host metabolism and stress responses79,80. Understanding these evolutionary dynamics is crucial for predicting phage behaviour in therapeutic applications and assessing their role in microbiome stability.

Virome–host Interactions

Eukaryotic virus–host interactions

The gut immune system maintains a delicate balance through integrated innate and adaptive mechanisms to manage frequent environmental exposure to viruses that infect eukaryotic host cells81. Eukaryotic viruses are directly sensed by host pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like receptors (TLRs)82. This recognition triggers immediate innate immune defences. A critical barrier-specific innate response in the intestine involves intestinal epithelial cell (IEC) production of antiviral cytokine interferon-λ (IFNλ)83. Upon viral infection, IFNλ signals locally to induce antiviral genes, thereby fortifying the epithelial barrier against viral invasion in mice and enteroid models84. Uninfected IECs can also detect viral infections in neighbouring cells and release IFNλ, implicating intercellular communication in the gut epithelial barrier as part of the early antiviral defence85.

The sensing of eukaryotic viruses by PRRs not only activates innate defences but also drives the development of adaptive memory responses. The adaptive immune system, organized within gut-associated lymphoid tissues (GALT), generates highly specific responses involving T cells and B cells86. B cells within GALT produce secretory IgA (sIgA), the primary antibody isotype in the lumen responsible for neutralizing pathogens and coating commensal microorganisms and viruses87. Evidence from mice shows that an essential role of sIgA is in maintaining immune homeostasis by neutralizing enteric viruses and preventing antigen-driven expansion of CD8αβ+ intraepithelial lymphocytes88. Moreover, studies involving selective IgA deficiency (SIgAD) have shown that patients with SIgAD lacking both serum and faecal IgA exhibit higher concentrations of serum IgG reactive to gut bacteria than patients with SIgAD retaining some faecal IgA. These findings support the conclusion that combined loss of systemic and sIgA amplifies systemic exposure to gut bacteria, leading to a higher incidence of systemic immune dysregulation89. Likewise, chronic viral colonization in IgA-deficient hosts can exacerbate intestinal inflammatory conditions, such as colitis, particularly when secondary genetic or environmental factors are present88. Although the immune response to eukaryotic viruses enhances the host’s ability to respond to subsequent infections, it could also contribute to chronic inflammatory states90. Notably, enteric viruses can serve as functional substitutes for commensal bacteria in specific contexts. In germ-free or antibiotic-treated mice, murine norovirus infection restores intestinal morphology, lymphocyte populations, and IFNγ production through type I interferon signalling91,92. Collectively, these findings highlight that eukaryotic viruses, although often associated with pathology, can also confer benefits on the mammalian host by supporting immune function and maintaining gut homeostasis under specific conditions.

Bacteriophage–host interactions

Bacteriophages can indirectly influence immune responses by modulating bacterial communities in the gut, thereby altering the production of microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids that regulate regulatory T cell differentiation93. Bacteriophages can directly modulate mammalian immunity by phage adherence to mucosal surfaces via exposed immunoglobulin-like domains on phage structural proteins63. This binding is thought to reduce bacterial colonization and dampen epithelial inflammation94,95. Furthermore, gut bacteriophages stimulate TLR9-mediated recognition of phage DNA in dendritic cells, eliciting phage-specific IFNγ production via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II-dependent antigen presentation and non-specific immune response in murine models96.

Viral translocation, defined as the movement of viral components or particles across epithelial barriers into the systemic circulation, is increasingly linked to chronic diseases97. Bacteriophages have been shown to interact directly with mammalian cells via glycan-binding proteins, influencing epithelial proliferation and immune signalling98. Phage transcytosis across epithelial layers occurs via apical-to-basal trafficking, with phage DNA potentially activating intracellular TLR9 pathways99. Moreover, extracellular vesicles secreted by mammalian host cells can shuttle viral particles or nucleic acids into neighbouring cells, facilitating non-lytic viral dissemination100.

Viral communities in a healthy gut

Viral communities in the healthy gut are characterized by high diversity and stability, forming a resilient ecosystem that contributes substantially to host health. The human gut virome is highly individualized yet maintains structural consistency over time, even as bacterial populations fluctuate101,102. A key feature of this ecosystem is the personal virome, a numerically prevalent and enduring fraction of the viral community predominantly composed of virulent bacteriophages, including crAss-like phages and members of the Microviridae family102.

Bacteriophages are critical regulators of bacterial populations and maintainers of microbial balance in the gut. Although strictly lytic phages cause bacterial cell death upon infection, lysogenic phages employ stable replication strategies that allow them to coexist with their bacterial hosts without causing extensive lysis103. This replication stability promotes ‘piggyback-the-winner’ dynamics, wherein prophages regularly undergo spontaneous induction at low abundance levels, maintaining equilibrium rather than causing large-scale disruptions104. Temperate phages frequently harbour AMGs or CRISPR elements, enhancing bacterial fitness by conferring antibiotic resistance or improved stress tolerance, therefore supporting microbial community resilience and overall gut health105.

The complex co-evolutionary relationships between phages, bacteria and the human immune system are central to gut homeostasis. Studies of centenarians have revealed uniquely diverse gut viromes with increased lytic activity106. This enhanced diversity could be linked with the known metabolic health profile of centenarians, including optimized short-chain fatty acid production and bile acid metabolism107. Centenarian viromes are also enriched in AMGs involved in sulfate metabolic pathways and homocysteine metabolism, creating a more metabolically robust ecosystem that could contribute to the health advantages106.

Beyond their metabolic contributions, gut viruses substantially contribute to gut barrier integrity and systemic immunity. Phage-mediated modulation of bacterial populations helps maintain mucosal integrity, whereas viral interactions with immune cells fine-tune inflammatory responses. In centenarians, the metabolic synergy of the virome with beneficial bacteria, particularly Bifidobacterium and Akkermansia, has been linked to reduced oxidative stress and delayed age-related dysbiosis, highlighting the potential role of the virome in promoting longevity and health span106, suggesting that viral communities might be as integral to gut health as their bacterial counterparts.

Early-life virome and clinical implications

The infant gut virome, established during a critical window for microbial–immune crosstalk, begins forming at birth through maternal vertical transmission and environmental exposures108,109. Pioneer bacteria first colonize the infant gut, with prophages released from these bacteria forming the primary population of VLPs110. Metagenomic studies show that bacteriophages (Caudovirales and Microviridae) and eukaryotic viruses (Anelloviridae) are detectable within days of birth, with diversity expanding rapidly during the first year of life111. Maternal contributions markedly shape this composition, with persistent bacteriophage strains shared between mothers and infants during breastfeeding112,113. Early dynamics follow a predator–prey pattern, transitioning from high phage–low bacterial diversity to low phage–high bacterial diversity with viral communities stabilizing by age 3 years, when approximately 9% of initial phage colonizers persist, highlighting potential co-evolutionary relationships with bacterial hosts114.

The infant virome influences immune development and disease risk. In preterm infants, substantial virome shifts — characterized by a loss of interpersonal viral diversity, driven by an increase in abundance of low-prevalence viruses — precede the onset of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC)115. These viral shifts coincide with bacterial dysbiosis, characterized by blooms of Enterobacteriaceae, suggesting that bacteriophage-driven bacterial lysis could disrupt gut barrier integrity and contribute to NEC pathogenesis116. Prophages also play a part in infant gut microbiome development, with active induction patterns remarkably unaffected by environmental factors such as antibiotic use, birth mode, exposure to pets, gastrointestinal infections or living environments117. The extensive induction of prophages suggests a highly dynamic virome during infancy, characterized by frequent shifts between lysogenic and lytic states, which could substantially influence the functionality and stability of the gut microbiota. Notably, a virome-specific asthma risk signature has been identified, in which enrichment of temperate families targeting beneficial gut bacteria at 1 year of age significantly predicts preschool asthma, independent of bacterial community composition118. In cystic fibrosis, viromes depleted in key bacteriophages such as Myoviridae and Gokushovirinae are linked to bacterial dysbiosis that potentially exacerbates inflammation119. Importantly, infants demonstrating stable colonization by beneficial bacteriophage groups such as Microviridae exhibit increased resistance to infections and antibiotic-associated diarrhoea later in life120.

These findings underscore the critical role of the virome as an independent factor in early-life microbiome development and immune programming. The temporal patterns of virome establishment, maternal contributions and prophage dynamics create a foundation for lifelong health, with perturbations potentially contributing to various disease states from infancy through later life. Understanding these complex virus–bacteria–host interactions might offer new preventative and therapeutic approaches for conditions ranging from inflammatory disorders to infectious diseases.

The virome in disease states

Inflammatory bowel disease

IBD, encompassing Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, is characterized by chronic inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract, influenced by complex interactions among which are included genetic predisposition, environmental factors and the gut microbiota121. The latest research has highlighted the critical role of the gut virome in IBD pathogenesis. Patients with IBD consistently exhibit increased relative abundance and diversity of tailed phages alongside a reduced Microviridae abundance, suggesting viral dysbiosis as a contributing factor8,122,123.

In Crohn’s disease specifically, metagenomic analyses identified dysbiosis in both DNA and RNA viromes, highlighting elevated eukaryotic viruses such as Torque teno virus and inflammation-associated phages targeting Staphylococcus, Faecalibacterium and Escherichia. These viral community alterations potentially exacerbate inflammation and bacterial dysbiosis, creating a feedback loop that worsens disease progression124. Conversely, mucosal samples from patients with ulcerative colitis exhibit higher abundances of Escherichia and Enterobacteriaceae phages than healthy individuals as controls (Table 4). Moreover, extensive disruption of interkingdom interactions between mucosal bacteria and viruses has been observed, potentially exacerbating gut inflammation and microbial imbalance65.

The spatial distribution of viral communities seems to be important in IBD pathology. Distinct mucosa-associated viromes have been documented compared with stool viromes, underscoring the importance of sampling location. Multiomics spatial analyses in humans have revealed that mucosal viral populations are enriched with unique crAss-like phages not typically detectable in stool, suggesting mucosa-specific roles in disease pathology and bacteriome interactions, possibly contributing to localized inflammatory responses125,126. A prominent feature of IBD viromes is the increased presence of temperate phages, which could disrupt bacterial communities through lysogenic-to-lytic cycle shifts. These viral life cycle alterations probably affect bacterial community stability and gut inflammation127.

Eukaryotic viruses are also associated with IBD. For instance, increased Anelloviridae and Hepeviridae and decreased Virgaviridae (viruses of plants, so probably due to dietary changes) are observed in patients with early-onset Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, suggesting direct involvement or indirect modulation of inflammation and barrier function disruption128,129. Causal evidence comes from virome transplantation experiments in human microbiota-associated mice, which have demonstrated that phages isolated from patients with ulcerative colitis can exacerbate colitis severity by altering bacterial diversity and inflammatory responses, confirming a causal role of virome dysbiosis in IBD pathogenesis130,131.

The functional implications of virome dysbiosis in IBD extend beyond ecological disruption to altered viral gene activities, including increased viral DNA replication, integration and modification functions. These changes suggest that these viruses could directly modify host bacterial genomes or disrupt mucosal integrity124. The complex interplay of bacteriophages and bacterial communities, particularly shifts in mutualistic interactions with beneficial taxa such as Bifidobacterium and Lachnospiraceae, further emphasizes the regulatory influence of the virome on gut ecology and inflammation in Crohn’s disease125. Furthermore, it has been described in vitro that viromes isolated from non-IBD colon tissues induce predominantly anti-inflammatory immune responses in human macrophages. By contrast, ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease viromes trigger pro-inflammatory responses132. This immunological divergence suggests that specific viral community compositions directly influence host immune activation and disease phenotype, potentially offering new therapeutic targets for IBD management.

The relationship between viral diversity and IBD is complex and often inconsistent across studies. Early findings indicate increased diversity of tailed phages in IBD123, but a later re-analysis of the same dataset, incorporating previously unclassified viral sequences, found no statistically significant difference in overall viral richness between patients with IBD and healthy individuals as controls8. Later work using unamplified sequencing methods, which avoid the biases introduced by multiple displacement amplification, further suggests that differences in viral α-diversity between IBD and control groups are less pronounced than previously reported127. These findings highlight the substantial interpersonal variability of the gut virome and its dynamic nature, both of which complicate efforts to identify consistent, disease-specific diversity patterns. Discrepancies in the literature probably arose from methodological differences, including the use of amplification techniques, whether unclassified viruses are included, variations in cohort composition and disease activity, sampling sites and the inherently individualized virome (Box 1).

Metabolic disorders and the gut virome

Obesity and metabolic syndrome.

Research on obesity and metabolic syndrome reveals distinctive patterns in gut viral communities. For instance, specific subfamilies of crAssphages show divergent roles: the alpha subfamily, which seems to be beneficial for metabolic homeostasis, is notably decreased in individuals with obesity and metabolic syndrome, whereas the beta and delta subfamilies are elevated, correlating with an increase in bacilli, a known biomarker for obesity133 (Table 4). Similarly, other research identified the Candidatus Heliusviridae, a widespread gut bacteriophage lineage, as significantly enriched in metabolic syndrome. These phages predominantly infect bacterial families Streptococcaceae and Bacteroidaceae, disrupting microbial balance and possibly exacerbating metabolic dysfunction134. Obesity and food addiction are also linked to specific phages from the Microviridae family, whose abundance alters tyrosine and tryptophan metabolism in mice and increases BMI135.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus.

In type 2 diabetes mellitus, distinctive gut virome changes have also been documented, with individuals with diabetes displaying a marked decrease in viral diversity and richness. By contrast, there is an increase in Escherichia coli and Lactobacillus phages136. These virome changes correlate with reduced microbial diversity and compromised gut barrier integrity, which promote systemic inflammation and metabolic disruptions characteristic of diabetes137. Supporting this finding, type 2 diabetes-associated gut viromes have been shown to have considerable genetic novelty and plasticity, along with virulence factors, potentially shaped by horizontal gene transfer40.

Liver diseases.

Liver diseases, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and alcohol-induced hepatitis, further highlight the role of the gut virome in metabolic health. Patients with MASLD exhibit a marked decrease in intestinal viral diversity, specifically bacteriophages, correlating with disease severity and liver fibrosis138. A model based on faecal viral diversity accurately predicted severe MASLD, emphasizing the clinical relevance of virome profiling as a diagnostic tool138.

In alcohol-induced hepatitis, virome dysbiosis is associated with increased viral diversity, notably of pathogenic phages such as those infecting Escherichia, enterobacteria and Enterococcus. Additionally, elevated levels of mammalian viruses, including Herpesviridae, were associated with higher disease severity and mortality, indicating direct and indirect contributions to hepatic inflammation and damage139.

Cancer

The gut virome influences colorectal cancer (CRC) pathogenesis through direct viral oncogenesis and indirectly via chronic inflammation and bacterial dysbiosis. Studies have revealed distinct virome alterations associated with CRC, notably increased viral diversity and specific shifts in composition compared with healthy individuals140,141. Temperate phages, are prominently expanded in CRC, targeting bacterial taxa implicated in colorectal carcinogenesis such as Enterococcus faecalis. These phages frequently carry AMGs, potentially linked to tumour-promoting dynamics, including enhanced bacterial virulence and metabolic dysregulation142. In mouse models, Helicobacter pylori infection exacerbates these effects by inducing prophage activation, amplifying the inflammatory environment and enhancing carcinogenic potential through bacterial dysbiosis and immune modulation142. Furthermore, CRC-associated bacteriophages disrupt interactions with oral bacterial commensals found in the human gut, suggesting that phage-induced dysbiosis contributes substantially to CRC progression141 (Table 4).

Eukaryotic viruses also have critical roles in CRC pathogenesis. For instance, increased abundances of Anelloviridae, specifically Torque teno virus, have been observed within colorectal tissues, correlating with carcinogenic pathways and supporting a direct oncogenic role in CRC140. Additionally, human papillomavirus types 16 and 18 have been implicated in colorectal carcinogenesis, integrating their genomes into mammalian host cells, modulating cell cycle progression and promoting genomic instability143. Persistent viral activity, particularly from bacteriophages, can perpetuate inflammatory states, exacerbating epithelial damage and dysbiosis, critical conditions conducive to colorectal carcinogenesis144.

Common patterns

Analysis of virome alterations across diverse disease states reveals striking similarities that suggest fundamental principles of viromeassociated pathologies. Cross-disease analysis identifies temperate phage expansion as a shared feature across inflammatory conditions, with temperate viral clusters significantly increased in patients with IBD compared with healthy individuals in the same household as controls8. This pattern suggests that prophage induction might contribute to bacterial dysbiosis in inflammatory contexts, creating a positive feedback loop between inflammation and prophage induction, which could explain the observed bacterial dysbiosis in these conditions.

Two distinct paradigms of virome dysbiosis emerge from comparative analysis: dysbiosis by loss-of-function, characterized by reduced viral diversity and depletion of beneficial viral activities, and dysbiosis by gain-of-function, marked by expansion of specific viral groups with pathogenic potential. Loss-of-function dysbiosis is exemplified in metabolic disorders, in which children with obesity and metabolic syndrome show significantly decreased abundance of the beneficial Alpha crAssphage subfamily (particularly Alpha_1 and Alpha_4 genera) alongside increased Beta and Delta subfamilies133. Conversely, gain-of-function dysbiosis is demonstrated by experimental evidence showing that VLPs from patients with ulcerative colitis exacerbate colitis severity when transplanted into mice, confirming causative rather than merely correlative relationships130.

The mechanism underlying temperate phage expansion seems to be linked to inflammatory signals that trigger prophage induction, as demonstrated in studies showing 105-fold higher prophage transfer in inflammatory versus healthy contexts145. This prophage induction specifically affects bacteria typically depleted in IBD, such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, where enhanced temperate phage-mediated mortality might contribute to dysbiosis146. Notably, the crAssphage system provides a specific example of beneficial virus loss, whereby decreased Alpha crAssphages correlate significantly with overabundance of bacilli in metabolic syndrome, suggesting precise phage–bacteria regulatory relationships133.

These shared patterns indicate that virome dysbiosis converges on common mechanisms of bacterial community destabilization and dysregulated immune responses, providing targets for therapeutic intervention that transcend individual disease categories.

Distinguishing causative from correlative evidence

Critically, the evidence supporting these patterns varies dramatically in quality, with only selected studies establishing causality beyond correlation. The strongest evidence of causality comes from virome transplantation experiments demonstrating that VLPs from patients with ulcerative colitis directly exacerbate colitis severity in human microbiota-associated mice, with shortened colon length and increased pro-inflammatory cytokine production130. Similarly, faecal virome transplantation (FVT) studies have shown that viral communities are sufficient to drive metabolic phenotypes, with mice receiving high-fat diet-derived viromes gaining more weight even when fed normal chow, whereas those receiving chow-derived viromes showed reduced weight gain despite high-fat diet consumption147. In metabolic syndrome, modified FVTs successfully alleviated symptoms in obese mice148, whereas in Clostridioides difficile infection, sterile faecal filtrates containing primarily bacteriophages have proven as effective as full faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in clinical settings149. These interventional studies satisfy adapted Koch’s postulates for microbial communities: isolation of the viral component, disease induction or amelioration upon transfer, and restoration of the original phenotype.

By contrast, most disease–virome associations remain correlative, based on cross-sectional metagenomic surveys or longitudinal observations without intervention. This evidence hierarchy has profound implications for therapeutic development: conditions with established causal mechanisms (IBD, C. difficile infection) are ready for clinical translation through phage therapy or FVT, whereas those with only correlative evidence (most metabolic disorders, early-life conditions) require mechanistic validation before therapeutic targeting.

Predictive framework for therapeutic intervention

This framework generates testable predictions about therapeutic efficacy based on both dysbiosis pattern and evidence quality. For gain-of-function dysbiosis with causal evidence (IBD, C. difficile infection), phage reduction strategies, including targeted antimicrobials against specific phages or competitive exclusion through beneficial phage cocktails, should prove effective. Indeed, autochthonous FVT has been shown in mice to reshape bacteriomes after antibiotic treatment more effectively when viable phages were present than when using heat-inactivated FVT150. For loss-of-function dysbiosis, the framework predicts that phage supplementation would restore microbial balance, although this aspect remains largely untested due to the absence of causal evidence in this category. This highlights a critical gap suggesting either that loss-of-function dysbiosis is rarely causative or that current methodologies fail to capture these mechanisms. Notably, FVT designed to enhance beneficial bacteria such as Akkermansia muciniphila has shown unexpected effects, including improved fertility rates in mice151, indicating that virome modulation might have broader physiological effects than currently appreciated. Future studies should employ selective phage elimination techniques, should be longitudinal intervention trials, and should involve the careful dissection of temporal relationships between viral loss and disease onset to resolve this critical uncertainty.

Therapeutic potential

Restoring microbial balance

Phage therapy has emerged as a promising strategy to restore microbial balance without disrupting commensal communities. Phage consortia targeting Klebsiella pneumoniae and E. coli reduce intestinal inflammation in mouse models of IBD, underscoring their precision in suppressing pathobionts152. Similarly, novel E. coli-specific phages with lytic activity against antibiotic-resistant strains have been isolated from the human gut, suggesting their utility in treating infections153. Importantly, in vitro studies have shown that using a single phage for clearing enteric infections has a minor effect in bacterial clearance and actually increases phage-resistant bacteria, highlighting the need to use phage cocktails154. Studies using synthetic microbiomes (bioreactors) have revealed that reintroducing virulent phages can reshape bacterial populations, offering a controlled approach to modulate dysbiosis155. Novel therapeutic protocols include phage-derived lytic enzymes, such as endolysins, which degrade bacterial cell walls and can selectively target pathogens, with demonstrated protection against antibiotic-resistant bacteria156–158.

FVT and the virome in FMT

FVT, or faecal filtrate transplantation, is a targeted therapeutic approach to modulate the gut microbiome by transferring filtered viral components from a healthy donor. This approach is different from FMT, which involves transferring the entire microbial community148,159. FVT offers several distinct therapeutic advantages over conventional FMT: reduced risk of transferring antibiotic-resistant bacteria or pathogenic organisms, as the filtration process removes intact bacterial cells while retaining bacteriophages160,161; potentially lower immunogenicity and reduced risk of adverse reactions, as studies in animal models have demonstrated that FVT might not substantially influence host immune cells to induce mucosal immunity compared with whole-microbiota transplants162,163; and the ability to target specific bacteriophage-mediated mechanisms of microbiome modulation, including phage predation, prophage induction and cascading effects on bacterial communities164. Additionally, FVT enables more precise manipulation of bacterial populations through phage predation without introducing competing bacterial species, potentially offering a more controlled therapeutic intervention. FVT has been successfully applied to treat C. difficile infection160,161, and has been shown to reduce symptoms of type 2 diabetes mellitus159, and promote the proliferation of commensal bacteria165 in mice. Furthermore, FVT can alter the microbiota and the condition of obese or lean outcomes in mouse models147. Limited human trials have shown transient improvements, such as modified phage composition and effects on glucose levels in individuals with metabolic syndrome166. Evidence now demonstrates that timing of FVT administration critically influences therapeutic efficacy. In IBD, transplantation during disease remission successfully alleviates intermittent intestinal inflammation and confers resistance to subsequent inflammatory challenges, whereas administration during active flare states may exacerbate inflammation167. This temporal specificity, combined with the ability of FVT to durably reshape the gut bacteriome towards beneficial taxa such as Lachnospiraceae and Muribaculaceae, positions FVT as a promising approach for IBD management167. Despite this potential, FVT carries notable risks and limitations, including the possible transmission of uncharacterized or harmful eukaryotic viruses not removed by standard filtration and the risk of exacerbating inflammation if the gut barrier is compromised148,165.

Challenges in gut virome research

Methodological limitations

Despite rapid advances, the study of the gut virome faces notable constraints. DNA extraction methods, amplification steps and enrichment protocols can introduce biases that complicate accurate characterization and comparison across studies35,44. The potential exclusion of integrated prophages or specific viral types during enrichment further hinders comprehensive analysis41. Moreover, the distinction between active and inactive viral particles remains challenging to determine through current sequencing approaches.

The viral dark matter problem

A primary challenge remains the vast viral dark matter — the large proportion of viral sequences that cannot be classified due to a lack of reference genomes and universal marker genes8,9,11. Despite extensive metagenomic mining efforts, most viral sequences from human gut samples remain unclassified, highlighting the immense diversity and novelty of the virome. Reference databases for viral taxonomy and functional annotation remain incomplete. Novel viral entities, particularly from non-model organisms, remain under-represented, complicating taxonomic and functional classification. The absence of standardized approaches for viral genome annotation further hinders comparative analyses across studies. Furthermore, the functional annotation of newly discovered viral genes, including AMGs, remains a bottleneck in understanding their precise ecological roles105.

Causality versus correlation

Disentangling the complex, multidirectional interactions between viruses, bacteria, the host immune system, diet and other environmental factors is inherently difficult. Establishing causality versus correlation in disease states requires sophisticated experimental designs beyond associative studies, which remain the predominant current approach in the field.

Future perspectives

The gut virome is a complex and dynamic community that actively shapes the gut ecosystem through predation, lysogeny and horizontal gene transfer64,69. This viral collective directly and indirectly interacts with the host immune system, modulating both innate and adaptive responses, contributing to immune training and potentially substituting for bacterial functions under certain conditions63,91,168. A stable and diverse virome, characterized by persistent personal components102 and potentially enriched in beneficial phages such as specific crAss-like phages133 or those seen in centenarians106, is increasingly associated with gut homeostasis and overall health. Conversely, alterations in virome composition and function (dysbiosis), including shifts in phage diversity, abundance of specific viral types (for example, Caudovirales in IBD8,123), increased prophage induction70 or changes linked to eukaryotic viruses128, are implicated in a growing list of conditions, including IBD, metabolic disorders and adverse early-life outcomes115,118. The early-life establishment phase seems to be particularly critical, setting trajectories for long-term host–microorganism interactions113,114. Moving forward, establishing clear interpretative frameworks (Boxes 1 and 2), implementing advanced analytical techniques, and integrating multiomics, mechanistic and longitudinal approaches will be essential for developing more effective virome-based therapeutic and diagnostic strategies.

Methodological advances

Future research should focus on integrating advanced methodologies to overcome current limitations. Continued development and application of long-read sequencing43, amplification-free protocols39, SVG51 and in situ visualization techniques55 will be crucial for achieving a higher resolution view of virome composition, function and dynamics. Combining multiple methodological approaches within single studies will help mitigate individual biases and provide more comprehensive insights. However, the development of diagnostic markers faces substantial challenges due to the inherent characteristics of the gut virome. The highly dynamic nature of viral communities, their constant evolution and high interindividual variability could limit the universal applicability of virome-based diagnostics. Phages undergo continuous mutation and adaptation within the gut environment, making it difficult to establish stable biomarkers across populations. These features collectively necessitate careful consideration when developing diagnostic applications, and could require personalized or population-specific approaches rather than universal markers.

Mechanistic studies

There is a critical need for more mechanistic studies utilizing gnotobiotic animal models, organoid systems and carefully designed human interventions, including FVT studies130,131 to dissect the causal roles of specific viruses and community structures in health and disease. These controlled experimental systems allow manipulation of individual variables and detailed characterization of effects, thereby establishing causality rather than mere association.

Longitudinal studies

Longitudinal studies, particularly those tracking individuals from early life through adulthood, are essential for understanding virome development, stability and long-term health implications113. Such studies can reveal how early virome establishment influences later health outcomes and identify windows of opportunity for intervention.

Multiomics integration

Integrating viromics data with other ‘omics’ approaches (metagenomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, proteomics) and detailed host immunological data will provide a more holistic understanding of the place of the virome within the complex gut ecosystem and its interplay with the host. This multilayered approach can reveal functional networks and causal pathways that are not apparent from single-omics studies.

Computational tool development

Advanced computational tools designed specifically for viral metagenomic analysis are needed to address the unique challenges of virome data, including high sequence diversity, lack of conserved marker genes and complex ecological interactions. Machine learning approaches hold particular promise for feature characterization within these complex datasets. Such tools are currently one of the main options for viral pipelines in the context of viral prediction, protein structure annotation and lifestyle prediction (Table 2). However, these approaches can be more comprehensively integrated and specifically tailored to address the unique challenges of virome analysis, particularly for complex ecological interaction mapping and host–phage relationship inference.

Standardization efforts

Standardized protocols for sample collection, processing, sequencing, and analysis would benefit the field by facilitating comparison across studies and enabling meta-analyses. Consensus guidelines for reporting virome data and metadata would likewise enhance the reproducibility and integration of findings. Recognizing this critical need, the National Institutes of Health common fund has launched the Human Virome Program, which aims to establish specific centres to organize and standardize efforts to characterize the human virome across diverse populations and health states169.

Therapeutic interventions

Phage therapy is a promising strategy for precision modification of the gut ecosystem. Unlike broad-spectrum antibiotics, bacteriophages can target specific pathogenic bacteria without disrupting commensal communities152,153. Developing engineered phages or cocktails tailored to individual microbiome profiles could revolutionize treatment approaches for conditions associated with bacterial dysbiosis155. Novel therapeutic protocols include phage-derived lytic enzymes, such as endolysins, which degrade bacterial cell walls and can selectively target pathogens156–158.

FVT offers a novel therapeutic approach that could complement or replace traditional FMT in specific contexts. Early preclinical161 and clinical160 studies suggest potential applications in C. difficile infection, metabolic disorders159 and inflammatory conditions. However, rigorous safety protocols and standardized preparation methods are needed before widespread clinical application148,165.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the human gut virome is a complex and dynamic ecosystem that fundamentally shapes microbial communities, immune responses and host health outcomes. This Review examines the composition and dynamics of gut viral communities, from the dominant bacteriophages to emerging viral entities, and evaluates methodological approaches that influence biological interpretation. We synthesize evidence linking virome alterations to diverse disease states, distinguishing correlative associations from demonstrated causal mechanisms, and explore emerging therapeutic applications including phage therapy and FVT. Although substantial challenges remain, ongoing methodological advances and interdisciplinary collaborations promise to unlock the full potential of this field for enhancing our understanding of disease pathogenesis and developing novel therapeutic approaches.

Glossary

Auxiliary metabolic genes Genes carried by viruses that encode metabolic functions and can supplement or modify host cell metabolism during infection.

CrAssphages The most abundant bacteriophage discovered in human gut metagenomes through cross-assembly analysis.

Dysbiosis An imbalance in microbial community composition and function characterized by altered abundance of key taxa or disrupted ecological interactions with potential effects on health.

Lysogenic conversion The process by which prophage genes alter the phenotype of the bacterial host, potentially conferring new capabilities such as toxin production or antibiotic resistance.

Lysogeny The viral life cycle state where phage DNA integrates into the bacterial chromosome and replicates passively with the bacterial host without causing immediate lysis.

Prophage induction The process by which integrated viral DNA (prophage) exits the bacterial chromosome and initiates viral replication, leading to bacterial cell lysis.

Temperate phages Bacteriophages capable of both lytic (immediate bacterial host lysis) and lysogenic (chromosomal integration) life cycles.

Viral dark matter The large fraction of viral sequences in metagenomes that cannot be taxonomically classified due to lack of similarity to known viral genomes.

Fig. 1 | Molecular approaches to study the human gut virome.

a, Components of the gut virome ecosystem, including free viruses, bacteria carrying prophages, active prophages and virus–bacteria interaction pairs. b, Bulk metagenomics involves sequencing total DNA from gut samples and recovers mainly bacterial genomes and their prophages, with low recovery of free viruses. c, Proximity ligation (meta3C and metaHiC) maps phage–host associations by capturing physical DNA–DNA contacts, enabling detection of both active and dormant prophages, as well as replicating viruses. d, Virus-like particle (VLP) metagenomics enriches and sequences free viruses, including low-abundance taxa, but misses integrated prophages. e, Single-virus genomics (SVG) isolates and sequences individual viral particles, enabling the recovery of rare, and otherwise difficult-to-assemble, viral genomes.

Fig. 2 | Gut virome dynamics: protective roles, lytic and lysogenic cycles, and immune interactions.

Phage-mediated mucosal defence in the healthy gut (part a): in a healthy gut, bacteriophages adhere to mucosal surfaces, forming a protective barrier that prevents pathogenic bacterial adhesion. Secretory IgA (sIgA) antibodies produced by plasma cells further enhance this defence by neutralizing bacteria and limiting their colonization. Lytic cycle and inflammatory consequences (part b): phages initiate the lytic cycle, infecting bacteria and replicating via new virions synthesis (part c). Subsequent bacterial lysis releases pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), which are recognized by Toll-like receptors (TLRs) on dendritic cells (DCs). This interaction triggers pro-inflammatory cytokine production (tumour necrosis factor (TNF)), amplifying inflammation and potentially contributing to tissue damage and reduced bacterial diversity. Concurrently, released bacterial antigens can stimulate plasma cells to produce additional antibodies (IgA and IgG), which further modulate microbial colonization and contribute to mucosal immune responses. Environmental stressors can exacerbate these effects by inducing prophage activation. Lysogenic cycle and bacterial pathogenesis (part d): in the lysogenic cycle, temperate phages integrate their genomes into bacterial chromosomes as prophages. These prophages can encode virulence factors, including exotoxins and antibiotic resistance genes, enhancing bacterial pathogenic potential (part e). Horizontal gene transfer mediated by phages further disseminates antibiotic resistance within bacterial populations. sIgA, secretory IgA.

Box 1 | Methodological considerations for interpreting contradictory findings in gut virome research

When evaluating conflicting results across virome studies, systematic consideration of methodological factors often reveals that contradictions reflect technical rather than biological differences. This framework provides a structured approach for interpreting discordant findings.

Conflicting viral diversity measurements

When studies report opposing diversity patterns in the same disease state, consider:

Viral enrichment method

Virus-like particle (VLP) enrichment: preferentially captures extracellular and actively replicating (induced) phages; typically yields higher observed diversity

Bulk metagenomics: captures integrated prophages and cellular viruses; may show lower free viral diversity

No enrichment: bacterial DNA dominates, resulting in decreased viral detection sensitivity

Sequence classification approach

Analysis of classified viruses only: produces lower diversity estimates with clearer disease-associated signatures

Inclusion of unclassified ‘viral dark matter’: increases diversity measurements, potentially obscuring disease-specific patterns

Database selection: reference database completeness significantly affects classification rates

Diversity metrics employed

α-Diversity (within-sample) versus β-diversity (between-sample) measurements

Species richness versus evenness indices

Taxonomic level of analysis (strain, species, genus, family)

Variable viral abundance patterns

When viral loads appear inconsistent across studies, consider:

Disease activity during sampling

Active inflammation: enhanced prophage induction leading to apparent viral blooms

Remission or controlled disease: predominantly lysogenic state with lower viral detection

Treatment status: therapy-naive versus post-treatment samples represent distinct viral landscapes

Quantification and normalization methods

Virus-to-bacteria ratio (VBR): influenced by bacterial population fluctuations

Absolute viral counts: requires specialized quantification protocols

Relative abundance: may mislead if total microbial load varies

Sample type and location

Faecal samples: primarily represent luminal viral populations

Mucosal biopsies: capture tissue-associated or mucosal-embedded viral communities

These populations may differ substantially in composition and abundance

Inconsistent phage–host relationship assignments

When the same phage is linked to different bacterial hosts across studies:

Methods of host prediction and confidence levels

See Table 2

Host range considerations

Assumed narrow (species-specific) versus demonstrated broad host range

Environmental conditions affecting host specificity

Evidence of in vivo host-jumping events

Presence or absence of specific viral taxa

When viral families appear dominant in some studies, but absent in others:

Nucleic acid extraction protocols

Kit-specific viral recovery biases

Single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) viruses (for example, Microviridae) require specialized extraction/amplification

RNA viruses excluded in DNA-only protocols

Sequencing methodology

Short-read sequencing: superior diversity detection but may fragment large genomes

Long-read sequencing: improved genome completion but potentially misses low-abundance taxa

Amplification methods (multiple displacement amplification (MDA), whole genome amplification (WGA)): introduce substantial compositional bias

Bioinformatic analysis pipeline

Tool-specific detection biases for different viral families

Reference database version and completeness

Quality filtering stringency affecting retention of specific viral types

Conflicting functional interpretations

When the same viral community appears beneficial in one context but detrimental in another:

Experimental system differences

Germ-free versus conventional animals: distinct colonization dynamics

Humanized versus native microbiota: different ecological network structures

Single phage versus community transplantation: isolated versus network-mediated effects

Outcome measurements

Inflammatory markers versus bacterial diversity versus metabolic parameters

Acute versus chronic effects

Local (intestinal) versus systemic effects

Box 2 | Key principles for interpretation of gut virome research

Methodological complementarity

No single method captures the complete virome. Each approach reveals different aspects of viral ecology that should be considered complementary rather than contradictory.

Technical versus biological variation

Apparent contradictions between studies often reflect methodological differences rather than true biological variability. This framework should be applied before accepting conflicting results as biological reality.

Therapeutic implications

Accurate therapeutic targeting requires a precise understanding of which viral populations are captured by different methods. The causative viral agents may only be detectable through specific methodological approaches.

Study design considerations

Future investigations should employ multiple complementary methods to construct comprehensive pictures of virome dynamics. Meta-analyses must ensure methodological consistency when synthesizing results across studies.

Application guidelines

When evaluating any virome study, systematically address: which viral population does the employed method preferentially capture? what viral components might be systematically excluded or under-represented?; could alternative methodological approaches yield different or opposing results?; how do methodological constraints limit biological interpretation?

Key points

The gut virome, primarily composed of bacteriophages and eukaryotic viruses, substantially influences gut microbiota dynamics, immune responses and metabolic health.

Next-generation methods and state-of-the-art bioinformatics tools have identified numerous newly characterized viral entities with potential associations to health and disease.

Individual virome composition exhibits high variability, shaped by early-life exposures and sustained environmental interactions, and alterations in gut virome composition correlate with diverse diseases, including inflammatory bowel diseases, metabolic disorders, neurological conditions and cancer.

Prophage induction, driven by environmental factors, profoundly influences microbial community structure and host immune responses.

Therapeutic strategies such as phage therapy and faecal virome transplantation represent targeted approaches to restore gut microbial equilibrium.

Addressing methodological biases and ‘viral dark matter’ is critical for elucidating the role of the gut virome in health and disease, as disease states are commonly linked to high prevalence uncharacterized viral entities.
