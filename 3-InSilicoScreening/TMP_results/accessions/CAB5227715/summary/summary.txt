Paper: PMC13037465.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:

- The study analyzed temporal shifts across major climatic transitions, including the last glacial period (characterized by extremely cold conditions) and the Holocene interglacial (warmer conditions).
- The abundance of certain taxa showed correlations with temperature shifts: Casjensviridae were detected in all lakes except one, and their elevated abundance during the glacial period suggested infection might be favored under environmental stress conditions.
- The abundance of Salvovirus (infecting a plant pathogen) was positively associated with higher temperatures.
- The abundance of Phycodnaviridae (alga-infecting) increased in the Holocene, likely reflecting enhanced aquatic productivity associated with warming.
- The abundance of Mimiviridae showed a pattern of increasing significantly in one lake after a period of warming (post-13.5 ka BP), contrasting with its stability or decline in other lakes during Holocene warming.
- The general discussion notes that temperature correlates with viral community composition and infection dynamics in both marine and freshwater systems, and that viral communities are susceptible to global warming.
- The study discusses the impact of temperature on host distribution, noting that certain host strains (e.g., *Pelagibacter*) are associated with polar waters and that their decline after 9 ka BP may be linked to increasing sea surface temperatures.
- The persistence of virions in sediments is influenced by physical properties, but the text does not detail temperature-dependent stability mechanisms for the viruses themselves.

Environmental Context:
- Sedimentary ancient DNA (sedaDNA) from five Siberian lakes and three Subarctic/Antarctic marine cores, spanning the late Pleistocene and Holocene glacial–interglacial transitions.

Concise Summary:
The analysis utilized sedaDNA time-series from glacial to interglacial periods to infer long-term viral community structure. While direct physiological temperature data for the viruses or hosts are absent, the study documents clear ecological responses to major climatic shifts. Viral community composition is shown to be sensitive to temperature gradients, with shifts observed between glacial and interglacial periods. Specific temporal patterns include the increase of algal viruses (Phycodnaviridae) linked to enhanced productivity during warming (Holocene) and the association of certain phage families (Casjensviridae) with cold glacial periods. Furthermore, the abundance of key host lineages and their associated viruses tracked major climatic events, such as the decline of cold-adapted host strains following post-glacial warming. These observations confirm that ancient viral assemblages reflect environmental forcing, including temperature changes, over millennial timescales, even if the specific thermal adaptations of the viral components are not quantified.

Paper: 42129249.txt
Thermal Evidence:
- No specific optimal, growth, or survival temperature ranges were provided.
- No mention of thermophilic, psychrophilic, mesophilic, heat tolerance, cold tolerance, or temperature-adapted enzymes/membranes.

Environmental Context:
- Isolated from strongly sodic soils (pH 9.9, electrical conductivity 2.4 dS m-1).
- The organism is described as haloalkaliphilic.

Concise Summary:
An efficient elemental sulphur oxidiser was isolated from strongly sodic soils (pH 9.9). The organism exhibits haloalkaliphilic characteristics. The study details its role in enhancing sodicity reclamation by increasing elemental sulphur oxidation rates in alkaline conditions (pH 9.1-10.5). The metabolic activities observed include the production of titratable acidity, ammonia, and indole-3-acetic acid, alongside the solubilisation of P and Zn. The enhanced oxidation leads to a decline in soil alkalinity and an increase in $\text{Ca}^{2+}$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, and $\text{PO}_4^{3-}$, correlating with a significant reduction in the sodium adsorption ratio (SARe). The application of this strain significantly improved crop yields in sodic soil.
