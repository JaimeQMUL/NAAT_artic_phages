Paper: PMC13159264.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:
- **Temperature Gradient:** The study analyzed microbial communities across a natural thermal gradient ranging from $50^	ext{o}	ext{C}$ to $93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$.
- **Community Shifts:** Distinct community shifts were observed correlating with temperature: moderate-temperature springs ($50–70^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) were dominated by certain phyla; high-temperature springs ($70–90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) enriched specific taxa; and extreme-temperature springs ($	ext{≥}90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) were characterized by distinct, specialized lineages.
- **Thermophilic Adaptation:** Lineages in extreme-temperature environments exhibited clear signs of thermophilic adaptation, including genome streamlining, reduced regulatory complexity, and specialized metabolic strategies.
- **Phylum Specific Trends:** Certain phyla showed temperature-dependent abundance patterns: e.g., one phylum peaked at $85^	ext{o}	ext{C}$ before declining sharply at $93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$; another phylum showed gradual enrichment up to $93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$; and a third phylum dominated exclusively at $93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$, indicating strong adaptation to extreme thermal conditions.
- **Metabolic Adaptation:** Carbon fixation pathways shifted with temperature, moving from pathways associated with moderate temperatures (e.g., CBB cycle) towards specialized autotrophic pathways (rTCA, DH/HH cycles) in extreme high-temperature environments.
- **Genome Adaptation:** Hyperthermophilic lineages showed significantly smaller genome sizes compared to mesophilic phyla, alongside increased GC content in tRNA and rRNA, suggesting adaptations for thermal structural stability.

Environmental Context:
- **Habitat/Source:** Western Sichuan Plateau hot springs, representing a natural thermal gradient.

Concise Summary:
Metagenomic analysis across a $50–93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$ thermal gradient in Western Sichuan hot springs demonstrates that temperature is a primary driver of microbial community structure, metabolic adaptation, and genome evolution. Community composition shifts systematically: moderate temperatures ($50–70^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) favor certain phyla utilizing pathways like the CBB cycle; high temperatures ($70–90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) see enrichment of taxa employing the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway; and extreme temperatures ($	ext{≥}90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) are dominated by lineages exhibiting strong thermophilic adaptations. These extremophiles display genomic streamlining, reduced regulatory complexity, and specialized metabolic pathways (e.g., rTCA, DH/HH cycles) optimized for persistent thermal stress. The observed adaptations include genomic changes (e.g., increased GC content) and metabolic specialization, such as the shift in energy-conserving nutrient acquisition strategies (S/C/N cycles) towards highly efficient, autotrophic modes in the hottest environments. The findings establish a clear link between increasing thermal stress and increasing metabolic specialization and genomic reduction, consistent with niche narrowing in extreme thermal niches.

Paper: PMC13001362.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:
- Temperature emerged as the strongest driver structuring viral communities, outpacing the effects of salinity, depth, dissolved oxygen, chlorophyll-a, and geography.
- Viral diversity was consistently higher in the northern warmer waters (7.5–10.4 °C), while southern colder sites (4.0–7.4 °C) harbored less diverse and compositionally distinct communities.
- Giant viruses shift from Pandoravirales and AG_04 (Algavirales) at lower temperatures to increasing dominance of IM_01 (Imitervirales) and Prasinoviridae (AG_01) in warmer habitats.
- Prokaryotic virus communities also displayed strong temperature structuring, with additional influence from salinity.
- For giant viruses, 65.1% of total gvOTUs were detected exclusively in samples at higher temperatures, with the majority assigned to the Prasinoviridae (AG_01). In contrast, only 14.3% of gvOTUs were exclusive to colder temperatures, most of which belonged to the IM_01 family.
- Temperature shifts resulted in distinct community transitions: AG_04 family and Pandoravirales dominated below 6.1 °C, IM_01 family became prominent between 6.4 °C and 7.5 °C, and AG_01 family dominated above 8.9 °C.
- For prokaryotic viruses, community composition differed markedly between colder and warmer samples, and PERMANOVA confirmed that temperature had a statistically significant effect on beta diversity (R² = 0.30, p < 0.05).
- The overall analysis showed that temperature was the primary ecological driver of beta diversity in both viral communities, explaining a substantial portion of community variation.

Environmental Context:
- Subantarctic Chilean Patagonia fjords, spanning a latitudinal gradient with temperatures ranging from 4 to 10 °C.

Concise Summary:
Temperature is identified as the dominant ecological driver structuring both giant virus and prokaryotic virus communities in the subantarctic Chilean Patagonia fjords. Viral diversity is significantly higher in the warmer northern waters (7.5–10.4 °C) compared to the colder southern sites (4.0–7.4 °C). This temperature dependence dictates community composition: giant viruses shift from Pandoravirales/AG_04 at lower temperatures to increased dominance of IM_01 and Prasinoviridae (AG_01) in warmer conditions. Similarly, prokaryotic virus communities show clear structural differentiation between cold and warm habitats. The study confirms temperature's role as the primary determinant of beta diversity for both viral groups, suggesting that viral ecology is highly sensitive to warming trends.

Concise Summary:
The organism is a polyextremophilic cyanobacterium, originally isolated from thermal springs, exhibiting thermophilic characteristics. It can grow at high temperatures, with documented growth up to 50°C. Under high temperature stress (40°C), the organism adapts its membrane lipid profile by shifting acyl unsaturation from unsaturated to saturated fatty acids, and increasing the proportion of the anionic lipid SQDG. This adaptation is a common thermophilic response observed in related species, aimed at counteracting increased membrane fluidity. Furthermore, the organism's lipid metabolism is highly responsive to temperature, with changes in both membrane and nonpolar lipid pools (TAG accumulation) observed under thermal stress. The study details lipidomic changes across various stresses, including high temperature, high salinity, phosphate, nitrogen deprivation, and extended cultivation, highlighting the differential regulation of nonpolar lipid synthesis pathways (TAG and FAPE) compared to membrane lipid remodeling.
