Paper: PMC12827599.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:
- Optimal growth temperature: 30°C (for bacterial growth/phage assays) and 28°C (for host range assay).
- Growth temperature range: 30°C (primary incubation temperature).
- Survival temperature range: Stable over a wide range from 4°C to 45°C (for phages); significant reduction in infectivity detected above 55°C.
- Incubation conditions: Standard incubation at 30°C for bacterial growth and phage assays.
- Environmental source/habitat: Soil microenvironment.
- Thermophilic/Psychrophilic/Mesophilic: The conditions suggest mesophilic growth, with stability observed across a moderate range.
- Heat tolerance: Phages showed reduced infectivity above 55°C.
- Cold tolerance: Phages remained stable at 4°C.

Environmental Context:
- Habitat/Source: Soil microenvironment.

Concise Summary:
Two novel virulent bacteriophages were isolated from a soil microenvironment. Bacterial growth and phage assays were primarily conducted at 30°C, while host range assays utilized 28°C. The phages demonstrated thermal stability across a broad range, remaining stable from 4°C up to 45°C, though infectivity significantly decreased above 55°C. The stability profile suggests mesophilic adaptation. The bacterial host was characterized as an emerging nosocomial pathogen, and the phages were tested for stability under various temperature and pH conditions, confirming their potential utility in variable environmental settings.

Concise Summary:
Two lytic bacteriophages were isolated from influent sewage samples. The phages exhibited optimal lytic activity and stability at 37 °C, consistent with mesophilic organisms. Thermal stability testing revealed that the phages maintained viability at 25 °C and 40 °C, but showed significant loss of viability at 55 °C and above, with no viable phages remaining after incubation at 70 °C. The phages were isolated from sewage wastewater, indicating an environmental source. The study details the use of 37 °C as the standard incubation temperature for assessing lytic activity and biofilm disruption, confirming their physiological relevance to human-associated infections.
