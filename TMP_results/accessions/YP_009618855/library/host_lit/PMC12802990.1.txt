ABSTRACT

Shigellosis represents a major global health challenge, significantly contributing to mortality among children under five, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Advances in molecular techniques have underscored the extensive global impact of Shigella, and the spread of multi- and extensively drug-resistant strains has sharply escalated the threat, emphasizing the urgent need for innovative interventions. Developing a broadly protective vaccine with a positive benefit-risk profile remains a critical and challenging objective due to the diversity of Shigella serotypes mediated by the immune dominance of the O-antigen. This review analyzes the distribution of Shigella serotypes in both endemic and non-endemic regions and examines the impact of O-antigen diversity on cross-reactivity. Various vaccine strategies, with a particular focus on the O-antigen as a key antigenic target, will be presented, highlighting the need for an effective vaccine especially due to the increased antimicrobial resistance associated to this pathogen. Overall, this review aims to provide a thorough understanding of different aspects of Shigella epidemiology with the aim to support development of effective control strategies against Shigella thus contributing to efforts against the escalating antimicrobial resistance crisis.

Introduction

Diarrheal diseases constitute an important global health problem, causing more than 1 million deaths annually, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia1 (Figure 1). In 2016, Shigella was the second leading cause of diarrheal deaths after rotavirus.3 Recent molecular techniques have revealed a severe underestimation of the global burden of Shigella infections.4 Multi- and extensively drug-resistant (MDR and XDR) strains are becoming increasingly prevalent both in endemic5 and non-endemic countries.6–9 However, there is still no widely available vaccine against Shigella. Shigellosis occurs when epithelial cells in the colon, ileum, and rectum are invaded.10 The infection leads to inflammation, diarrhea, and frequently bloody diarrhea, depending on the serogroup,11 along with fever and abdominal cramps.10 Shigella invades the host intestine through M cells located above the lymphoid follicles, where it is engulfed by macrophages. It quickly escapes from the macrophages by initiating pyroptosis, releasing the bacteria at the basal surface of the epithelial cells. It then enters epithelial cells via macropinocytosis. Once inside the cytosol, Shigella replicates and uses actin-based motility to evade host defenses and spread to adjacent cells.12 Shigella species are typically classified as waterborne but can also be foodborne pathogens, as the most common transmission vehicles are water and contaminated food, even though they are essentially found in stool of infected people or primates.13

Risk factors for Shigella infection include poor water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) standards,14 and the disease is indeed prevalent in areas with inadequate sanitation and limited healthcare access. Shigella has the potential to cause large outbreaks across all age demographics, but the burden especially affects vulnerable groups such as children, in whom infection can lead to a cycle of malnutrition and diarrheal disease,15 resulting in significant long-term complications such as growth faltering and cognitive impairment.16 While traditionally associated with low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), Shigella infections also pose a growing concern in high-income countries (HICs), particularly due to the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR).17 In HICs, certain populations, such as men who have sex with men (MSM), are disproportionately affected.18

The complexity of fighting diarrheal diseases is due to their multifactorial nature, and while improved sanitation significantly reduces diarrhea incidence, sustained progress necessitates additional public health measures like appropriate surveillance systems and immunization programs.19

Protection through vaccination against Shigella is achievable, as early studies on natural exposure demonstrated that exposed subjects had serotype-specific IgG and IgA antibody responses that offered protection from reinfection.20,21 A threshold of 1:1600 IgG anti-S. sonnei lipopolysaccharide (LPS) titer in Israelian adults was associated with a 74% vaccine efficacy (VE). In children aged 3–4 y, a threshold of 4.5 Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) Units corresponded to a predicted VE of 63%, and for children aged 2–4 y, the same threshold predicted a VE of 49%.22 Studies have shown that the bactericidal activity of antibodies in sera is associated with reduced clinical disease and may predict vaccine efficacy against Shigella infection.23 Findings also suggest that specific immune markers, such as Serum Bactericidal Assay (SBA) titers, IgG avidity, and IgA levels, are associated with a lower risk of shigellosis and could potentially serve as correlates of risk. Controlled human infection models have further confirmed the plausibility of achieving immunity through vaccination.24

Serotype specificity is linked to the O-antigen (OAg), a component of the outer membrane LPS of Shigella, which is the primary target of the host’s humoral immune response. Several vaccines in development aim to induce an immune response that can recognize and neutralize Shigella bacteria based on their OAg.

However, developing a broadly protective vaccine against Shigella is challenging due to the diversity of Shigella serotypes, each characterized by a different OAg structure. The Shigella genus comprises four principal serogroups: Shigella dysenteriae (group A), Shigella boydii (group B), Shigella flexneri (group C), and Shigella sonnei (group D), with more than 50 serotypes. Among them, the predominant and widespread species are S. flexneri (more than 15 serotypes) and S. sonnei (1 serotype).25

Despite the diversity of serotypes, cross-reactivity could potentially be leveraged to develop a vaccine that provides protection against multiple serotypes. If antibodies induced against the OAg of one Shigella serotype can cross-react with the OAg of another serotype, a vaccine incorporating the former OAg could potentially provide protection against both serotypes.26

In this review, we will explore the distribution of S. sonnei and flexneri in both endemic and non-endemic countries, unraveling the factors that influence the variation in prevalence of different serotypes. We will delve into the implications of OAg diversity on cross-reactivity between serotypes and the potential for cross-protection. The urgent need for a vaccine, especially in the face of rising antimicrobial resistance, will be discussed by examining various vaccine strategies for Shigella, with an emphasis on the OAg as an antigenic target. We aim to provide a thorough understanding of different aspects relevant to the development of effective control strategies against Shigella.

Distribution of Shigella

The prevalence of S. flexneri and S. sonnei species can vary across regions due to multiple factors including hygiene practices, sanitation, and healthcare infrastructure. Numerous large studies conducted in countries of the Asian continent undergoing economic transition illustrate where the spread of S. flexneri has been progressively replaced by S. sonnei as health conditions have improved.27–30 Concurrently, there has been an increase in antibiotic-resistant strains of S. sonnei, possibly contributing to its raised prevalence.31 Generally, literature indicates that S. sonnei tends to be more prevalent in industrialized countries or regions with better sanitation and hygiene practices, while S. flexneri is often more prevalent in developing countries or regions with poor sanitation and hygiene facilities (Figure 2). However, many developing countries face challenges such as under-resourced healthcare systems, that may struggle to diagnose, monitor, and control infectious diseases such as shigellosis, resulting in an inaccurate understanding of Shigella prevalence.32 The World Health Organization (WHO) has established treatment guidelines for Shigella that do not require routine diagnosis for treatment.33 This approach is primarily because Shigella infections often present with distinctive symptoms, such as dysentery, allowing for empirical treatment. The goal is to quickly initiate treatment and control the infection, thereby reducing the risk of complications and the spread of the disease.

The underestimation of Shigella cases is further exacerbated by nonspecific symptoms that can overlap with other gastrointestinal illnesses. Consequently, many cases go undiagnosed or misdiagnosed, hindering the compilation of accurate epidemiological data. Addressing this knowledge gap requires collaborative efforts to implement effective surveillance systems and increase awareness about Shigella in both developed and developing regions.

Indeed, efforts like the Global Enteric Multicenter Study (GEMS),34 the Vaccine Impact on Diarrhea in Africa (VIDA),5 and Enterics for Global Health (EFGH) Shigella surveillance study, have played pivotal roles in enhancing our understanding of Shigella distribution in endemic countries. While large-scale initiatives like GEMS, VIDA, and EFGH provide valuable insights, smaller studies can offer region-specific details and variations in Shigella serotypes and strains. Shigella in shigellosis-endemic countries

In delving into the examination of the prevalence of Shigella, it becomes evident that a substantial proportion of research and data is centered around Sub-Saharan Africa and Asia. These regions, being hotspots for Shigella, draw heightened attention due to their unique challenges, encompassing factors like inadequate sanitation, dense urban populations, and distinct socioeconomic dynamics. Thus, the concentration of studies on Sub-Saharan Africa serves as a focal point in unraveling the complexities of Shigella distribution and impact on a global scale. In addition to the extensive research focused on Sub-Saharan Africa and Asia, it is crucial to acknowledge the presence of Shigella in South America, which also experiences endemic levels of this pathogen.

The most impactful contribution to the epidemiology of Shigella in endemic countries came from the GEMS case–control study that monitored moderate-to-severe diarrhea, elucidating its causes, for 3 y (from Dec, 2007 to Mar, 2011) in seven sites both in Africa and in Asia (Bangladesh, Pakistan, India, The Gambia, Mali, Kenya, and Mozambique).72 The subsequent study from Livio et al. focused the attention on Shigella isolates gathered in the GEMS study in all the seven countries.34 This analysis confirmed the prevalence of S. flexneri and S. sonnei that accounted respectively for 65.9% and 23.7% of the cases of shigellosis in children below 60 months of age. Also, a better understanding of the distribution of the serotypes of S. flexneri was possible in this context: of 1130 Shigella isolates of children presenting with moderate to severe diarrhea, 20.2% were diagnosed with S. flexneri 2a, 11% with S. flexneri 6, the 10.9% with S. flexneri 2b, the 9.4% with S. flexneri 3a and 7.5% with S. flexneri 1b.0, and confirming these serotypes as the most widespread.34

Africa

A recent VIDA investigation evaluated the burden of Shigella in children below 59 months of age, focusing on three Sub-Saharan African sites analyzed in the GEMS study (The Gambia, Mali, and Kenya) spanning from 2015 to 2018, yielded substantial insights into the prevailing patterns of Shigella distribution in Africa.5 Particularly noteworthy were findings regarding the percentage of prevalence of the most prominent serotypes, principally including S. flexneri 2a (40.6%), 1b (18.8%), 6 (17.5%), 3a (9.0%), and 4a (5.1%).5 Additional data concerning serotype distribution are available from various African countries. For instance, research by Breurec et al. in the Central African Republic revealed that five S. flexneri serotypes comprised 54.9% of all isolates: 6 (14.4%), 1b (13.3%), 2a (9.0%), 3a (8.6%), and 4a (5.6%) within that specific region from 2002 to 2013.73 Similarly, in South Africa, the National Institute for Communicable Diseases’ GERMS-SA annual surveillance review for 2023 identified S. flexneri 2a as the predominant serotype (224 S. flexneri 2a isolates/705 serotyped isolates; 32%). This was followed by S. sonnei (135/705, 19%), S. flexneri 3a (83/705, 12%), and S. flexneri 1b (81/705; 11%).55 In a smaller-scale study conducted in Malawi in children below 5 y of age by Stenhouse et al. from 2012 to 2015, only three S. flexneri serotypes were identified: S. flexneri 3a, 4a, and 6.50

Asia

In a multicenter study conducted between 2000 and 2004, encompassing a collective cohort of over 600,000 patients spanning Bangladesh, China, Pakistan, Indonesia, Vietnam, and Thailand, S. flexneri emerged as the predominant Shigella species (68%) across all sites except Thailand, where S. sonnei prevailed (85%). Among the isolated S. flexneri serotypes, the majority comprised S. flexneri 2a, 3a, 1a, 1b, 2b, 1c, 6, and X, with considerable variation in their distribution across distinct locations.56 The latest significant studies examining Shigella distribution in the Asian continent have primarily centered on Bangladesh, specifically in the urban center of Dhaka and the rural Matlab subunit within the Chandpur District. Findings from these investigations confirm a higher prevalence of S. sonnei in urban regions and S. flexneri in rural regions.31,74 Similarly, data collected from the rural community of Mirzapur between 2007 and 2010 also corroborate S. flexneri as the most widespread species, particularly noting the presence of S. flexneri 1b, 2a, 2b, 3a, and 6.57 Substantial contributions to the understanding of Shigella species prevalence in Asia have emanated from the GEMS study spanning from 2007 to 2011 and insights from Livio et al.34 According to these findings, S. flexneri emerged as the prevailing species in the three Asian countries examined during the GEMS study (67.9% in Bangladesh, 65.1% in Pakistan, and 51.7% in India), with S. flexneri 2a being the most abundant serotype across Bangladesh, and India (20.8% in Bangladesh and 26.4% in India).34 Notably, two separate studies conducted in India (Kolkata) identified S. flexneri 2a and 3a as the predominant serotypes, followed by S. flexneri 4 and 1b.61,62 Additionally, a study spanning from 2008 to 2014 in China reaffirmed the dominance of S. flexneri 2a out of all the other S. flexneri isolates (36.7%), followed by 1b (12.9%), 2b (12.6%), and X (9.9%).75

South America

Laboratory data encompassing 19 countries of Latin America were gathered through the Latin American Network for Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance spanning from 2000 to 2015. The predominant isolated species included S. flexneri (49%) and S. sonnei (28%), notably highlighting an increase in resistance against ciprofloxacin.76 A recent retrospective substudy of the Malnutrition and Enteric Disease (MAL-ED) observational birth cohort study, which focused on the epidemiology of Shigella species and serotypes in children, examined the distribution of Shigella in Fortaleza, Brazil, and Loreto, Peru, from 2009 to 2012. The findings indicated a notable difference in the prevalence of Shigella species between the two sites. In Peru, S. flexneri was the most prevalent among the other Shigella species, accounting for 80.1% of cases, whereas in Brazil, it represented only 31.8% of cases.25 The most common S. flexneri serotype in Brazil was S. flexneri 2a, comprising 22.7% of cases, while in Peru, S. flexneri 6 was the most prevalent serotype at 30.1%, followed by S. flexneri 2a at 25.6%.

Regarding studies focusing on endemic regions, particularly those that contain substantial sample sizes, an undeniable trend emerges: the pervasive presence of S. flexneri as the primary Shigella species in most cases. This consistent dominance underscores the significance of S. flexneri in the Shigella infections distribution across diverse geographical settings. Among the serotypes observed, S. flexneri 2a has always been found and indicated as a recurrent and widely distributed strain, highlighting its remarkable prevalence and adaptability. Moreover, the recurring identification of serotypes such as S. flexneri 1b, 3a, and 6 in numerous studies further emphasizes their significant role in Shigella epidemiology. Their presence serves as a critical indicator of their widespread dissemination and reinforces the importance of including antigens from these serotypes in vaccine formulations, as endorsed by WHO guidelines.77

Many areas facing Shigella lack robust tracking systems and data, leading to an underestimation of its true impact. Changes in public health strategies and evolving antibiotic resistance patterns contribute to fluctuations in the distribution of Shigella species at different places and different times, add complexity, and highlight the need for adaptable surveillance strategies. To support this claim of epidemiologic transition, it is beneficial to delve deeper into the differences between the GEMS study and VIDA study. The latter was specifically designed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the epidemiology of Shigella species over time and following introduction of rotavirus vaccine utilizing the same protocol as the one utilized for the GEMS study.

Shigella in shigellosis non-endemic countries

While the development of vaccines against Shigella has rightly been focused on LMICs in Sub-Saharan Africa and Asia, due to the significant disease burden, it is important to broaden our perspective. Reports on Shigella in non-endemic settings are relatively scarce, yet there is an emerging trend indicating a significant disease burden in these regions as well. This is particularly noteworthy as Shigella is routinely notifiable in these non-endemic countries, presenting an opportunity for more comprehensive surveillance and understanding.

Through the contribution of national surveillance systems and studies carried out in non-endemic regions to analyze epidemics or isolate cases of shigellosis, there is evidence in Europe, the United States, and other industrialized regions. Shigella, though less prevalent, presents its own set of implications, especially considering outbreaks in certain settings (i.e MSM) and outbreaks caused by antimicrobial-resistant pathogens.

Europe

All European Union/European Economic Area countries annually submit epidemiological surveillance data to the European Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network. The most recent available report on shigellosis cases recorded during surveillance pertains to the year 2022, encompassing 30 countries and documenting 18,426 instances of shigellosis from 2018 to 2022.78 France, Spain, and the Netherlands emerged as the nations with the highest caseloads in 2022, accounting for 31%, 14%, and 10% of all cases, respectively, with a considerable proportion of cases linked to travel. Furthermore, S. flexneri 2a, 1b, and 3a were identified as the most prevalent serotypes, while S. sonnei was the predominant species.78

United Kingdom

Since 2015, Public Health England (PHE) has integrated whole-genome sequencing into their surveillance program in England and Wales to monitor Shigella cases. Data collected from 2015 to 2018 by the Gastrointestinal Bacterial Reference Unit (GBRU) of PHE revealed a prevalence of S. sonnei (54.4%) totaling 2695 cases, and S. flexneri (39.2%) totaling 1943 cases, with S. flexneri 2a (1113/1943, 57.2%), 1b (254/1943, 13.1%), and 3a (206/1943, 10.6%) emerging as the dominant S. flexneri serotypes.79 England witnessed several outbreaks in recent years attributed to S. sonnei and S. flexneri 2a affecting principally MSM.8,80,81 XDR S. sonnei and XDR S. flexneri 2a exhibited similarities in plasmid-mediated antimicrobial resistance and epidemiology, hinting at a phenomenon of horizontal antimicrobial resistance transfer.8 Previous outbreaks in England and Wales affecting MSM were also linked to S. flexneri 3a. Interestingly, in the work of Charles et al.,82 an in-depth analysis was conducted using samples retrieved from laboratory-confirmed Shigella cases in England during the period from 2016 to 2023. This analysis revealed shifts in serotype distribution. S. flexneri 2a serotype dominated prior to the implementation of lockdown restrictions for the COVID-19 pandemic. S. flexneri 1b, 2a, and 3a serotypes drove increases by late 2019 and persisted during the pandemic, primarily among adult males. Post-lockdown, S. flexneri 1b and 2a were the most prevalent, with a sharp rise of S. flexneri 2a observed in early 2023, further confirming that S. flexneri 2a did not decrease but instead increased in prevalence in the post-lockdown period.

United States of America

The National Enteric Disease Surveillance report, compiled by the National Center for Emerging and Zoonotic Infectious Diseases issues annual assessments of shigellosis monitoring in the USA. The latest report, pertaining to the year 2016, documented 12,597 cases of shigellosis across 52 states and regional public health laboratories. S. sonnei emerged as the predominant species (80.5%), followed by S. flexneri (12.6%), with the highest incidence observed among children aged 1 to 4 y. While the distribution of S. flexneri serotypes is only available for a subset of states, evidence suggests the presence of various serotypes, including S. flexneri 2a/2 unspecified, 1b/1 unspecified, 3a/3 unspecified, 4a/4 unspecified, and S. flexneri 6, in order of prevalence. Furthermore, a study conducted across 10 states focusing on individuals above 9 y of age also reported a higher prevalence of S. sonnei relative to S. flexneri cases.83 Notably, cases of XDR or MDR Shigella strains causing outbreaks have been reported in the USA. A recent investigation screening stool samples collected from 2017 to 2020 in San Diego, California, revealed a slightly higher frequency of S. flexneri (55%) compared to S. sonnei (45%) identifications and associated with resistance to a wide range of antibiotics.84 Another study analyzed samples collected in Minnesota from 2012 to 2015, comprising culture-confirmed cases of shigellosis by the Minnesota Department of Health Public Health Laboratory. Among the 882 infections identified, 85% were attributed to S. sonnei and 14% to S. flexneri, with half of the infections occurring in children.85 Moreover, an outbreak of shigellosis in New York, analyzed alongside sporadic samples collected over 26 months from 2013 to 2015, revealed that nearly all infections were caused by either S. sonnei (65%) or S. flexneri (34%), both exhibiting high rates of antimicrobial resistance to ampicillin and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole.86

Other

A multinational study included the United Kingdom (UK), Australia, Belgium, France, and the USA involving 943 Shigella isolates. This investigation unveiled an internationally linked outbreak of XDR S. sonnei strains, indicating a recent common ancestor. Shigellosis has been emerging as a growing cause of gastroenteritis in Australia, with notable outbreaks in remote First Nations communities and among MSM in urban centers. From 2001 to 2019, 18,363 shigellosis cases were documented in the National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System. Among the reported cases included in the analysis (18,327), 42% were attributed to S. sonnei and 29% to S. flexneri.87 In Australian metropolitan areas, shigellosis predominantly affects returning travelers from endemic regions and MSM. A study conducted in Victoria between 2016 and 2018 identified two significant MSM-associated lineages: one of S. sonnei and another of S. flexneri 2a.88 Analysis of isolates from 2013 to 2023 revealed that S. sonnei was the most frequently cultured species (61.9%), followed by S. flexneri (33.5%). The incidence of shigellosis among men rose until 2020, decreased during the initial period of the COVID-19 pandemic, and surged again in 2022–2023. There was a significant rise in MDR Shigella species, particularly S. sonnei, with cases increasing from 2022 to 2023. While S. flexneri maintained susceptibility to at least one oral antibiotic, S. sonnei showed escalating resistance to third-generation cephalosporins, azithromycin, and ciprofloxacin, indicating a troubling trend toward MDR and XDR strains.89 The emergence of XDR and MDR Shigella strains, particularly in non-endemic settings such as the UK, Europe, the USA, and others, is a stark indicator of a global trend toward increasing antimicrobial resistance. These findings lend credence to the hypothesis of a global phenomenon of antimicrobial resistance facilitated by the horizontal transfer of genes, a mechanism highlighted by Baker et al.90 Both endemic and non-endemic regions are experiencing these trends, albeit with variations in the specific strains and resistance patterns.

In non-endemic and industrialized countries, S. sonnei often emerges as the predominant species, aligning with previous observations. S. flexneri appeared consistently in 100% of the examined studies, often with a frequency comparable to that of S. sonnei, particularly evident in extensive surveillance programs. Similarly to endemic countries, when reported, the prevailing serotype of S. flexneri was always identified as S. flexneri 2a. Other prevalent serotypes included S. flexneri 1b and 3a, with S. flexneri 6 less frequently encountered in industrialized nations compared to endemic regions.

The genetic basis for these antigenic variations lies in the genes responsible for the biosynthesis and modification of the OAg. These genes are typically located on the bacterial chromosome or on plasmids.100 Horizontal gene transfer, mutations, and genetic recombination contribute to the diversity of S. flexneri serotypes by introducing new genetic variations that alter the antigenic properties of the OAg.92,101,102 While each serotype may exhibit unique variations in OAg modifications such as glycosylation and O-acetylation,103 certain regions of the genome encoding enzymes involved in OAg synthesis may share sequence homology, resulting in structurally similar OAg.104

For a broadly protective vaccine, leveraging the similarities between the OAg structures of different serotypes can be a strategic approach. By identifying common antigenic determinants shared among multiple serotypes, a vaccine can be designed to elicit an immune response that provides cross-protection against a wide range of serotypes.77

However, it is important to note that the cross-reactivity observed in serological assays may not solely be attributed to type or group similarities between OAg structures.105 Instead, conformational similarity of epitopes, the exposure of specific substituents within the three-dimensional structure, and the flexibility of OAg structures likely play a more critical role in mediating cross-reactivity. These factors may influence how the immune system recognizes and responds to OAgs, ultimately affecting the ability of a vaccine to provide broad protection.106,107 The phenomenon of cross-reactivity between serotypes of S. flexneri has been observed in both pre-clinical and clinical contexts, supporting the claim of cross-protection.91,105,108–111

In one of the earliest studies, Simmons (1958) investigated how OAg-mediated cross-reactivity works among different S. flexneri serotypes. This was done by observing the agglutination reactions between whole bacteria from S. flexneri 1a, 2a, 3, 4a, 5, 6, X, and Y serotypes and various antisera from the same serotypes. The results showed that the 2a strain could cross-react with antisera from S. flexneri 1a and Y and the S. flexneri 3 strain could cross-react with antisera from S. flexneri 1a, X, and Y.

After these very early observations of a possible cross-reactivity between S. flexneri strains, a study from Noriega et al. postulated that the Shigella antisera cross-reactivity could be mainly mediated by group specificities (3(4), 7(8), 9, and 10). In the study, attenuated S. flexneri 2a and S. flexneri 3a strains were used in a single formulation to immunize guinea pigs. The researchers measured geometric mean titers (GMT) of LPS-specific IgG and IgA against various S. flexneri serotypes, including 1a, 1b, 2a, 2b, 3a, 3b, 3c, 4a, 4b, 4c, 5a, 5b, 6, X, and Y. Results from ELISA testing showed high IgG titers against the vaccine serotypes, with significant cross-reactivity observed particularly against S. flexneri 1b and 1a. For IgA, a broader cross-reactivity was observed, with responses also against S. flexneri 2b, 4b, 5b and Y. In a subsequent experiment, the immunized animals were challenged with wild-type S. flexneri bacteria of various serotypes (S. flexneri 1a, 1b, 2a, 2b, 3a, 3b, 3c, 4a, 4b, 4c, 5a, 5b, 6, X, and Y), demonstrating cross-protection against all serotypes tested, except for S. flexneri 6.26

One of the pioneering studies demonstrating this concept was conducted by Van De Verg et al., which revealed different patterns of cross-reactivity between serotypes in humans vaccinated with an hybrid E.coli K12-S. flexneri 2a live-attenuated vaccine and humans and non-human primates challenged with S. flexneri 2a. To measure IgG and IgA titers, ELISA was conducted using a broad panel of LPS from S. flexneri 1a, 1b, 2a, 2b, 3a, 3b, 4a, 4b, 5a, X, and Y strains. What was possible to observe was that both the vaccinated or challenged human patients, gave a high percentage of IgG cross-reactivity against S. flexneri 5a, X, and Y serotypes. In terms of IgA titers, vaccinated subjects exhibited modest responses across all tested strains, with particularly higher titers against serotypes X, Y, 5a, 2b and 1a. For challenged human subjects, these individuals demonstrated elevated IgA titers for serotypes Y, 5a, 1b and 1a, with modest responses also against 4a, 3b, 3a and 2b that showed low or no IgG titers.108 In challenged non-human primates, high IgG titers were observed against all tested strains, and particularly strong IgA responses against Y, 5a, 4b, 4a, 3a and 1b.108

One of the studies on Shigella cross-reactivity showed evidence of cross-reactivity between S. flexneri strains when evaluating a S. flexneri 2a OAg conjugate vaccine. The study showed unexpected cross-reactive IgG antibodies against S. flexneri 6 LPS in adult, 4–7 y old and 1–4 y old patients but failed to replicate this cross-reactivity in mice, responding almost exclusively to the type 2a conjugate. The study suggests that the O-acetylated disaccharide shared by S. flexneri 2a and 6 OAgs is likely responsible for the cross-reactivity observed in humans.109 However, a precedent work identified that the O-acetylation of the rhamnose III sugar at positions 3 or 4 (referred to as 3/4-O-acetylation) is common to all known S. flexneri 6 and some of the S. flexneri 2a isolates.100

These observations are underscoring the complexity of cross-reactive immune responses and the differences that can be observed in these responses between different animal species make it difficult to predict results in humans.

Cross-reactivity between S. flexneri serotypes was also studied for the rational design of a broad-coverage Shigella vaccine based on Generalized Modules for Membrane Antigens (GMMA) technology. GMMA from 14 S. flexneri subtypes were used to immunize mice and rabbits and to generate antisera against S. sonnei and S. flexneri. It was found that some immunogens induced broadly cross-reactive antibodies, while others had narrower specificity. For example, S. flexneri 1b GMMA antisera were able to bind to and very efficiently kill in vitro S. flexneri 1b, 2a, 3a, 3b, 4a and 6. Also, S. flexneri 3a GMMA-raised antibodies that showed the ability to efficiently kill in vitro S. flexneri 1b, 2b, 3b, 4a, and 5b strains. On the other hand, GMMA from S. flexneri 2a, 4b, X and Y, and to a lesser extent S. flexneri 6, generated antisera that reacted with relatively few bacterial isolates. Results from this study confirmed that the observed cross-reactivity cannot always be attributed to group specificities. Some examples of this are antisera raised against S. flexneri 1b and 1c GMMA showed a particularly high binding to S. flexneri 2b, that have no group specificities in common.105

The cross-reactivity across various serotypes and species using multivalent Outer Membrane Vesicles (OMV)-based vaccines has been corroborated by additional studies. For example, the presence of IgG subclasses and secretory IgA specific to both outer membrane proteins (OMP) and LPS, and bactericidal activity were detected against the four strains included in a vaccine composed of antigens from S. sonnei and S. flexneri 2a, 3a, and 6. A OMP- and LPS-specific immune response was observed as well against S. dysenteriae type 1 and S. boydii type 4. Furthermore, the protection of the vaccine was assessed by challenging vaccinated mice with the target strains and S. dysenteriae type 1 and S. boydii type 2, yielding promising results.112

Vaccine strategies for Shigella: O-antigen as an antigenic target for broad protection

The Shigella genus requires a vaccine that can elicit a broadly reactive immune response, particularly targeting the most prevalent species such as S. flexneri and S. sonnei.34 According to WHO guidelines on preferred product characteristics for Shigella vaccines, a vaccine incorporating OAg structures from S. sonnei and various S. flexneri serotypes, specifically S. flexneri 2a, 3a, 6, and possibly 1b, could directly protect against 72% of circulating, widespread, and clinically significant Shigella strains. Additionally, through cross-protection mechanisms, such a vaccine could provide coverage for approximately 89% of circulating strains77; this estimate is based on the finding that five serotypes/subserotypes (S. flexneri 2a, 6, 3a, 2b, and 1b) account for 89% of S. flexneri isolates in the study by Livio et al.34 Additionally, the inclusion of these serotypes can provide indirect protection against other less common serotypes due to the common structure of the OAg.34

Other estimates based on data on prevailing Shigella species and serotypes in eight settings with a high burden of disease from 2009 to 2012 suggested that a vaccine containing S. flexneri 2a, 3a, or 6, or S. sonnei would cover 80.4% of the circulating strains in the examined areas and a vaccine containing S. flexneri 1b, 2a, or 3a, or S. sonnei would cover 59.4% of the circulating strains.25

Efforts to develop an effective Shigella vaccine have primarily concentrated on stimulating antibody responses, particularly IgG against the Shigella OAg since these antibodies have been proposed as a correlate of protection for Shigella and play a vital role in defense mechanisms by facilitating opsonization, phagocytosis, and intracellular cytotoxicity, as explained by Cohen et al.20

For instance, LimmaTech Biologics AG has developed a vaccine composed of bioconjugates targeting S. sonnei and S. flexneri 2a, 3a, and 6.113 The cross-reactivity of this quadrivalent (4 V) Shigella bioconjugate vaccine and the corresponding monovalent formulations against serotypes that are not targeted by the vaccine was assessed in rabbits. The vaccines were administered intramuscularly, alone or with an adjuvant. Serum from rabbits immunized with the 4 V vaccine showed bactericidal activity against S. flexneri 2a, S. sonnei, and S. flexneri 3a isolates, but not against S. flexneri 6 isolates. In contrast, serum from rabbits immunized with the adjuvanted 4 V vaccine exhibited bactericidal activity against all targeted serotypes and additional non-targeted serotypes (S. flexneri 2b, 4a, 4b). The adjuvanted vaccine particularly enhanced bactericidal antibodies against S. flexneri 2b, with moderate activity against S. flexneri 4a and 4b.111

Another approach in vaccine research that involves the utilization of OAg includes the one used by the Institut Pasteur that has formulated a vaccine comprising a conjugate of S. flexneri 2a OAg chain with tetanus toxoid.114 The Bill & Melinda Gates Medical Research Institute and the Institut Pasteur have formed a partnership to co-create a four-component synthetic carbohydrate-based conjugate vaccine targeting S. sonnei and Shigella flexneri serotypes 2a, 3a, and 6.115

A bivalent OAg glycoconjugate vaccine was also developed by Beijing Zhifei Lvzhu Biopharmaceutical, targeting S. flexneri 2a and S. sonnei. It has progressed through phase II trials and is currently in phase III clinical trials in China.116

OMVs present another avenue, offering easily obtainable solutions containing both OAg and protein epitopes capable of inducing cross-reactive immune responses. An example currently on clinical trial are GMMA-based vaccines. An initial GMMA-based candidate vaccine, 1790GAHB, targeting S. sonnei, elicited functional antibodies in adults against S. sonnei.117–119 This formulation paved the way to a quadricomponent vaccine candidate, altSonflex1-2-3, consisting of GMMA from S. flexneri 1b, 2a, and 3a, and S. sonnei, currently undergoing phase 2 clinical trials.110 Studies conducted on altSonflex1-2-3 showed how OAg mediated cross-reactivity can be assessed and demonstrated how sera from mice, rats, or rabbits immunized with the 4-component GMMA-based vaccine were able to bind to and have a bactericidal activity against S. flexneri 4a, 5b,6, X, and Y heterologous serotypes.91,110 As GMMA mimic the structure and composition of a bacterial outer membrane, they include soluble periplasmic proteins, lipoproteins and outer membrane proteins (Omps). However, their contribution to triggering functional antibodies against S. sonnei and S. flexneri is very limited compared to OAg, which serves as the primary factor driving bactericidal activity.105,120 Preclinical results were then compared to clinical results, confirming the ability of the vaccine to induce functional cross-reactive antibodies against the same panel of heterologous strains of Shigella also in humans, even with different patterns and specificities.91 Alternative strategies that leverage the immunogenicity of Shigella OAg include whole-cell killed121,122 and live-attenuated vaccines featuring genetically weakened Shigella strains.123,124 Indeed, multivalent killed vaccines have shown promise in early clinical development.125 One method focuses on expressing Shigella OAgs on live-attenuated vectors from the Shigella genus or similar pathogens.126,127

Other than the OAg, conserved components of the Shigella cell envelope, such as Ipa proteins and Omps, have been explored as potential vaccine targets. Ipa proteins, critical for Shigella invasion of host cells, have shown efficacy in preclinical studies when targeted alone or in combination with Shigella polysaccharides.128–130 Moreover, Ipa proteins exhibit a degree of conservation among different Shigella serotypes, making them attractive candidates for broad-spectrum vaccines. Indeed, there is evidence of cross-reactivity elicited by some of these protein-based vaccines. For example, an S. flexneri 2a OAg-IpaB vaccine administered in mice showed 56% protection against heterologous S. sonnei challenge In the same study, a group of mice was vaccinated with S. flexneri 2a OAg-mutant diphtheria toxoid cross-reacting material 197 (CRM) and showed lack of protection (11% survival), indicating that the cross-protection against S. sonnei was conferred by the IpaB protein in the case of OAg-IpaB vaccine.

A different application of the use of OAg and Ipa proteins together entails formulating a mixture of invasion plasmid antigens (IpaB, IpaC, and IpaD) and LPS extracted from genetically attenuated S. flexneri 2a, forming a complex used as a vaccine, as demonstrated in studies on the Invaplex (AR Detox) vaccine product. Initially, the vaccine comprised native product isolated from wild-type Shigella, but it evolved into a formulation composed of purified LPS and recombinant IpaB and IpaC.130 Evidence showed that this vaccine, currently under clinical development, could also offer protection against S. flexneri 3a and 6, since a bactericidal antibody response was observed against these two heterologous serotypes, even if it was attributed to the presence of IpaB and IpaC antigens.130

Likewise, Omps, like OmpA and OmpC, have garnered attention due to their role in bacterial adhesion and invasion.131–133 Additionally, the Omp IcsP has been identified as a common antigen among different Shigella serotypes, suggesting its potential for vaccine development.134

The synergy between the immune responses elicited by Shigella OAgs, LPS, and Omps presents a promising approach for vaccine development aimed at conferring protection against various serotypes of Shigella. While OAgs typically induce a robust immune response upon vaccination, Omps, although individually less potent, can broaden the vaccine’s protective efficacy due to their conservation across serotypes. By incorporating these elements into a vaccine formulation, synergistic responses could be achieved.

In the absence of a single validated correlate of protection, Shigella vaccine trials employ multiple immunological assays. ELISA for anti-LPS/OAg IgG remains the gold standard humoral endpoint.135,136 However, functional assays like serum bactericidal assays (SBA) are increasingly used to quantify complement-mediated killing, with high titers being associated with protection.23,135 Beyond these, a comprehensive, holistic approach is critical: techniques such as systems serology, which profiles antibody subclass distribution, Fc receptor interactions, and effector functions, are emerging as promising tools to define integrated immune signatures predictive of protection.137,138 This broad evaluation is essential to identify the composite immune responses needed for broadly protective Shigella vaccines.

The clinical development of Shigella vaccines faces significant challenges, ranging from Shigella OAg diversity to the establishment of reliable correlates of protection.22,139,140 To overcome these challenges, substantial efforts are underway to optimize Shigella detection methodology, and immune responses evaluation and to carefully design Phase 3 trials to test efficacy of Shigella vaccines141,142 to maximize the success of such trials and minimize the time to licensure.

Conclusion

This review highlights the significant global health challenge posed by Shigella infections, emphasizing the need for enhanced surveillance and refined methodologies to better understand its distribution and develop effective vaccine strategies. Shigella vaccine development is even more urgent due to the growing problem of antimicrobial resistance. Indeed, the EFGH multicounty surveillance study has been recently performed, providing critical data on Shigella incidence, disease severity, serotype distribution and antimicrobial resistance, laying essential groundwork for future vaccine trials.143 While regional epidemiological studies and surveillance initiatives offer invaluable insights into the prevalence and distribution of Shigella species, their utility is inherently limited by underestimation challenges and the lack of advanced techniques for precise serotype identification, particularly in endemic regions. Advanced molecular techniques have played a pivotal role in identifying Shigella in developed non-endemic countries, whereas culture-based methods remain predominant in endemic areas. Indeed, the study’s limitations include a lack of consistent data on Shigella distribution in some countries and the varied methodologies used for serotyping. This inconsistency means available information may not accurately reflect the true epidemiological situation, thereby complicating vaccine development. Additionally, the overuse of antibiotics in LMICs is not only posing significant diagnostic challenges but also driving the spread of antimicrobial-resistant strains. Also, attenuated clinical signs in most industrialized nations due to prompt medical care complicate the diagnostic process, highlighting the need for improved diagnostic capabilities.

The review underscores the importance of developing a broadly protective vaccine, focusing on OAgs as key targets for inducing robust immunity against multiple Shigella serotypes. While animal models offer insights into cross-reactivity, integrating these findings with human clinical trial data is essential for a comprehensive understanding of vaccine performance. Therefore, while the structural similarities of OAg are key considerations in designing a broadly protective vaccine, it is crucial to conduct comprehensive studies to understand the full spectrum of immune responses elicited by the vaccines. Researchers are exploring various vaccine strategies, including live-attenuated, conjugate, and subunit vaccines, with OAg-based formulations showing promise for enhancing cross-protection. Indeed, while cross-protection mediated by proteins might appear simpler due to conserved epitopes, OAg-based vaccines achieve cross-reactivity by leveraging structural similarities between OAg variants, which is a more complex but highly effective approach for addressing pathogen diversity. Many of the OAg-based platforms utilized for vaccine development allow to preserve OAg integrity, thus supporting induction of antibodies specific for conformational epitopes that can contribute to cross-protection. The importance of developing a vaccine that could protect against the majority of the Shigella strains causing a burden in endemic countries is paramount. Additionally, a vaccine targeting resistant strains would be a significant breakthrough, particularly for the traveler market, to prevent the global dissemination of MDR and XDR Shigella strains.

Despite these advancements, gaps remain in our understanding of Shigella cross-reactivity. Addressing these gaps requires the development of refined methodologies to generate comprehensive data on the serotype coverage of current vaccines. A deeper understanding of cross-reactivity, including the mechanisms beyond group-specificities that may drive this phenomenon, and the potential of antibodies elicited to cross-react with heterologous OAgs, is essential. Furthermore, current approaches for evaluating cross-reactivity are limited, emphasizing the need for advanced high-throughput assays to facilitate a more robust analysis of sera against multiple serotypes. The implementation and optimization of these tools have the potential to greatly enhance our knowledge and accelerate progress in vaccine development.

In summary, concerted efforts are needed to combat Shigella infections by leveraging advances in vaccine development and epidemiological surveillance. The findings and identified research gaps presented in this review pave the way for new research directions, inspiring further innovation in diagnostic technologies, vaccine design, and global health strategies. While acknowledging that numerous initiatives, studies, and national surveillance efforts are already in place, and despite existing attention on the antimicrobial resistance (AMR) crisis and the burden of Shigella, this review nevertheless advocates for the development of cross-disciplinary research initiatives that bring together experts from epidemiology, infectious diseases, and data science. By fostering partnerships between research institutions in high-income countries and those in low- to middle-income regions, future studies can address local challenges while generating globally relevant solutions and succeed in the identification of a correlate of protection for Shigella and the development of an effective vaccine. Furthermore, the prospect of translating preclinical studies into clinical settings offers a promising horizon, urging researchers to pursue integrated studies that combine laboratory insights with real-world epidemiological data. By addressing this, morbidity and mortality associated with Shigella infections may be reduced.
