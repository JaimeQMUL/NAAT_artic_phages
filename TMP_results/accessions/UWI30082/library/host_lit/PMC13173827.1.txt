Cerebral vasculitis affected 22% of comatose patients with bacterial meningitis, predominantly Streptococcus pneumoniae, despite high dexamethasone usage (85%). Vasculitis was associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation, extended ICU stays, and increased mortality. Further research is required to refine therapeutic strategies and clarify the impact of corticosteroid therapy.

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the study by Baron et al. [1] on the outcome of community-acquired meningitis in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) over a 15 year-period. They report on 166 patients with an overall case fatality rate of 18.1% and no improvement over time despite a statistically significant increase in the use of dexamethasone. They also found that only 22% of the patients received antibiotics within 1 h and 42% within 3 h following hospital admission, which is an unacceptable timeframe, yet reflects real-life practice. Interestingly, the proportion of patients who survived with severe neurological disability (defined as Glasgow Outcome Scale 2–3) declined over time (from 9.8% to 0%), in parallel with an increased use of dexamethasone (from 43% to 77% in the most recent period). Those observations support recent meta-analysis where the use of steroids in patients with acute bacterial meningitis is associated with reduced neurological sequelae [2, 3]. Neurological complications identified during acute bacterial meningitis have critical prognostic consequences. In the study of Baron et al., death was due to neurological complications in 25 out of 30 patients : brain herniation in 6, withdrawal of care because of poor neurological prognosis in 11 patients, and cerebrovascular complications in 8 patients (ischemic stroke, veinous thrombosis). In the context of acute bacterial meningitis, stroke is classically attributed to vasculitis, vasospasm, endocarditis or intra-arterial thrombosis [4]. Acute cerebral vasculitis is a well-known but poorly described complication of acute bacterial meningitis. It is known that vascular alterations play a key role in brain damage, particularly in meningitis caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae. In a pathological study of 31 patients who died from bacterial meningitis, all cases exhibited vascular inflammation, leading to narrowing and obstruction of the vascular lumen [5]. Other contributing factors include local dysregulation of coagulation and fibrinolysis, and endothelial cell dysfunction.

On the basis of our experience, we would like to draw attention to the clinical relevance of brain vasculitis in patients with acute bacterial meningitis. We conducted a retrospective analysis on patients admitted to the medical ICU of Nantes University Hospital, between January 2013 and Decembre 2023. We report on 58 comatose adult patients diagnosed with acute bacterial meningitis who required invasive mechanical ventilation. In our case series of comatose ventilated patients, all patients had an imaging of the brain within a few hours following ICU admission. The diagnosis of brain vasculitis and the exclusion of differential diagnoses were based on the formal neuroradiological report of the brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT), in accordance with established neuroradiological criteria [6, 7]. The diagnosis of brain vasculitis was based on the presence of direct (vessel wall thickening with contrast enhancement) or indirect signs (cerebral perfusion deficits, ischemic brain lesions, intracerebral or subarachnoid haemorrhage, vascular stenoses unlikely to be atherosclerotic). Vasospasm was distinguished from vasculitis primarily by the clinical context and temporal evolution. Embolic Stroke was distinguished by the distribution of lesions and differentiation from thrombosis or atherosclerosis was based on the location and characteristics of the stenosis (Atherosclerotic lesions predominantly occur at vascular branching points, whereas vasculitic lesions do not show this prevalence and are often associated with concentric vessel wall enhancement, as opposed to the eccentric enhancement in atherosclerosis).

Table 1 report their main characteristics. The median age of the study participants was 64 years (IQR 53–70) and 42 (72%) were male. At ICU admission, most patients presented with coma (88%), while 21% had at least one episode of seizure, and 31% displayed a focal neurological deficit (motor or sensory). All but 2 patients had a microbiological identification of a pathogen, with Streptococcus pneumoniae accounting for 60% of the isolates. We found that 13 out of 58 patients (22%) had evidence of brain vasculitis on brain imaging reports. Of the 13 patients with vasculitis, 11 had a brain MRI and 2 had a brain contrast-enhanced CT.

There was no difference in age, gender, comorbidities, and level of consciousness at ICU admission between patients with and without vasculitis. However, the motor component of the Glasgow Coma Scale was significantly lower in patients with vasculitis, which may be explained by the higher prevalence of ischemia observed on brain imaging in this group. Streptococcus pneumoniae meningitis was associated with an increased incidence of brain vasculitis, despite all affected patients received appropriate initial antibiotic therapy. Patients who developed cerebral vasculitis were more likely to receive prolonged corticosteroid therapy, a common clinical practice despite limited supporting evidence. Cerebral vasculitis was associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation (11 vs. 4 days) and longer ICU stays (13 vs. 7 days). These patients also exhibited higher ICU mortality (62% vs. 24%) and a greater rate of poor functional outcomes (mRS 3–5: 75% vs. 3.6%) compared to those without vasculitis. Ultimately, the primary cause of death in patients with cerebral vasculitis was irreversible brain damage, a finding less frequent in those without vasculitis.

Our study as a number of limitations. First, because we included only ventilated patients, we cannot assess the prevalence of vasculitis in the less severe forms of meningitis. Second, the limited number of patients with vasculitis precluded a multivariable analysis designed to identify independent predictors of mortality and poor outcomes. Finally, the study design prevented us from evaluating how the specific treatments used in the ICU may have affected patient outcomes.

Conclusions

In the dexamethasone era, more than one-fifth of patients with comatose meningitis developed evidence of cerebral vasculitis. Since this complication is difficult to predict at ICU admission, a low threshold for appropriate brain imaging—specifically MRI or contrast-enhanced CT—should be maintained in this high-risk population. Its occurrence identifies a subgroup of patients at high risk of poor functional outcomes and mortality. Finally, while high-dose or prolonged corticosteroid courses are widespread in clinical practice, this approach remains largely empirical. Interventional studies are therefore warranted to establish evidence-based guidelines.
