Here, we report selective covalent assembly of Gram-negative bacteria and synthetic polymers into functional living materials. We discovered that triblock polymers decorated with vinyl sulfone (VS), a motif that forms a stable covalent bond with cysteine residues on surface proteins, yielded stable covalent assembly with bacterial cells. Notably, we found that these assemblies were uniquely cell-type-specific, occurring only in Gram-negative bacteria, highlighting differences in surface structures and the macromolecular diffusion barrier across bacterial species. We also demonstrated that assembling engineered cells into materials results in situ melanin production from living materials, with robust biocontainment and mechanical reinforcement. Spontaneous enrichment of tyrosine-derived red pigments in the supernatant showcases the effect of confinement in a complex biochemical pathway. This work establishes a platform for encoding complex, engineered, and evolved functions of Gram-negative bacteria into synthetic materials, enabling the development of a wide range of material-based bioreactors.

Introduction

Gram-negative bacteria are a group of prokaryotes characterized by their double-membrane structure sandwiching a thin peptidoglycan layer (Figure a). This dynamic structure provides a functional advantage in terms of adaptability to various environments and resistance to external stressors. Many Gram-negative bacteria play key roles in biogeochemical cycles, including denitrification processes crucial for nitrogen management in soil, freshwater, and marine environments. − Their ability to form spatially organized bacterial communities, collectively carrying out these metabolic functions, makes them attractive targets for creating scalable bioreactors capable of performing specialized bioremediation applications. At the same time, Gram-negative bacteria are indispensable tools in biotechnology, − as exemplified by Escherichia coli, a workhorse organism in synthetic biology due to its exceptional genetic tractability. ,

Incorporating Gram-negative bacteria into synthetic scaffolds opens the possibility of leveraging their wide range of genetically encoded or naturally evolved complex functionalities to program materials. These materials can aid in the control of function, application, and biocontainment of synthetic microbial communities, providing sustainable solutions to large environmental problems. Recent advances in engineered living materials (ELMs) have expanded ways to combine the programmable functionality of living cells with the structural integrity of synthetic scaffolds. Traditionally, many ELMs have been created by physically encapsulating engineered living microbes, such as E. coli, within polymer matrices, including alginate, − agarose, gelatin, , or Pluronic hydrogel networks. ,

We recently reported a unique type of ELM by covalently assembling microbes with reactive polymers that specifically form well-defined chemical bonds with cell-surface moieties. − This approach offers potential advantages for achieving high cell density compared to other encapsulation methods and biocontainment, as it chemically integrates cells with polymer chains. Our previous work has utilized dynamic covalent bond formation with phenylboronic acid, which targets the abundant surface diols on Gram-positive bacterial cell surfaces, such as Bacillus subtilis and Staphylococcus epidermidis, − as teichoic acid species in their exposed peptidoglycan cell wall are heavily decorated with carbohydrate residues (Figure a). However, our labeling study using a fluorescent phenylboronic acid probe also revealed a key chemical difference between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria in their cell surface structure: a lab strain of E. coli, a model organism for Gram-negative bacteria, has a much lower abundance of available surface diols compared to Gram-positive bacteria. Moreover, the alkaline conditions (pH ∼ 9.5) typically required for effective bond formation of diols with 3-acetamidophenylboronic acid motifs in polymer chains result in substantial loss of metabolic activity in a model Gram-negative bacterium, E. coli, with 60–70% reduction in cellular metabolic activity under these conditions (Figure b). This pH incompatibility, combined with the limited availability of surface diols, suggests a limitation of the phenylboronic acid–based covalent integration approach for a wide range of Gram-negative bacteria. , This limitation has motivated us to explore alternative bond formation chemistries and assembly strategies for Gram-negative bacteria.

In this study, we report selective covalent assembly of macroscopic living materials comprising Gram-negative bacteria and synthetic polymers. We identified vinyl sulfone (VS) as an effective molecular handle for targeting cysteine residues on the surface proteins of Gram-negative cells. A systematic investigation of polymer architecture revealed design rules for covalent assembly with a model Gram-negative bacterium, E. coli. Strikingly, we observed unprecedented cell-type-specific molecular assembly of bulk materials with Gram-negative bacteria, highlighting differences in macromolecular diffusion barriers between Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. We demonstrated the formation of functional materials that produce melanin and preferentially enrich tyrosine-derived red pigments in the supernatant, showcasing the potential application of this technology to encode complex functions of Gram-negative bacteria into synthetic materials and to create material-based bioreactors.

Results and Discussion

Bond Formation of Vinyl Sulfone with Available Cysteine Residues on the Surface of a Model Gram-Negative Species, E. coli

Gram-negative bacterial cell surfaces present nucleophilic amino acid residues on outer membrane proteins. Cysteine residues are attractive targets for covalent modification due to their highly nucleophilic terminal thiol groups. − While maleimides demonstrate high selectivity toward thiols, they also exhibit fast reactivity under physiological conditions, which can introduce material heterogeneity due to uneven cross-linking during assembly processes. In contrast, VS can form stable thioether-sulfone bonds with thiols with slower reaction rates than maleimides, − which may allow more uniform cross-linking during material assembly. − We reasoned that this slower kinetics could be advantageous for achieving stable covalent assembly of living materials with homogeneous bulk material properties and consistent cellular distribution throughout the assembled structure. To assess the intrinsic reactivity of vinyl sulfones with accessible cysteines, we conducted a model reaction using ethyl vinyl sulfone and l-cysteine methyl ester (PBS buffer, pH 8.1), with maleimide as a positive control, and monitored the reaction by 1H NMR spectroscopy. As expected, the rate of reaction between l-cysteine methyl ester and vinyl sulfone was much slower than that with maleimide (Figure S4). Maleimide reacted instantaneously, with complete conversion occurring within minutes, while vinyl sulfone exhibited slower kinetics, reaching 65% conversion in 2 h and 100% conversion by 24 h at 25 °C. The reaction with vinyl sulfone proceeded faster at 37 °C than at 25 °C.

We first validated the availability and reactivity of the surface cysteine residues by labeling experiments using fluorescent maleimide conjugated to sulfoCy5. A robust fluorescence localized on the E. coli (DH10B) cell surface after multiple washes indicated the availability of the surface-exposed thiols for covalent conjugation (Figure c). A fluorescent vinyl sulfone probe incorporating a boron-dipyrromethene (BODIPY) fluorophore with a vinyl sulfone reactive handle, BODIPY-VS, was synthesized (Figure S2.3). We also employed BODIPY–OH (Figure S2.2), which lacks a vinyl sulfone handle, as a negative control. Labeling experiments using a laboratory strain of E. coli (DH10B) were conducted by incubating cell suspensions (OD600 1.6) with fluorescent probes (50 μM, pH 8.1, 37 °C, and 500 rpm) and subsequent washes with PBS buffer. Fluorescence microscopy images revealed a surface localization of the fluorescence with BODIPY-VS, but not with BODIPY–OH, and the gradual increase in fluorescence intensity over time, confirming covalent attachment of vinyl sulfone probes to the bacterial surface (Figures d and S5.1). We also measured the bulk fluorescence intensity of these suspensions at various time points, which showed a similar kinetic profile (Figure g) as the model reaction traced by 1H NMR (Figure S4). We also tested the BODIPY-VS labeling with a model Gram-positive bacterium, S. epidermidis. While the Gram-positive bacterial surface is covered with a thick peptidoglycan cell wall mesh, there are protein species anchored to the cell wall, , including LPXTG-anchored surface proteins and lipoproteins, , as well as membrane proteins beneath the cell wall. , Robust surface labeling of BODIPY-VS on S. epidermidis suggests the accessible free thiol in these available protein species to small molecules (Figures f and S5.2).

Polymer Designs to Enable Macroscopic Assembly of Living Materials with E. coli

We set out to design synthetic polymers appended with VS motifs for assembling E. coli cells. Initially, linear polymers of 2-hydroxyethyl acrylamide (HEAA) were synthesized via RAFT (LC, Figures a–b and S2.4), and the subsequent postpolymerization modification with divinyl sulfone (DVS) resulted in statistical VS linear polymer (L, Figures a–b and S2.5). 1H NMR analysis revealed the extent of VS modification below 30% for L polymer (Figure b). When 0.33 g/mL (based on dry weight) of cell suspension is mixed with L polymer (10–50 w/v%), it does not yield macroscopic assemblies with E. coli (Figures c and S6.1). However, cellular aggregates were observed under optical microscopy, indicating that the linear L polymer could promote cell clustering at a microscopic scale but lacked the ability to produce bulk materials.

We then changed the architecture of the polymer backbone to a triblock design. We reasoned that the limited VS modification is due to the solubility of the resulting polymers and incorporated a solubilizing polyethylene glycol (PEG) spacer between pHEAA blocks. RAFT polymerization of the PEG-modified chain transfer agent (CTA) and subsequent RAFT yielded triblock polymers, TC1–TC3 (Figures a–b and S2.6–2.8). Subsequent postpolymerization modification with excess DVS in 0.1 M sodium hydroxide solution yielded VS-functionalized triblock polymers, T1–T3, with VS modification ranging 40–60% based on 1H NMR analysis (Figures a–b and S2.9–2.11). In contrast to linear polymers, the triblock design of all three polymers successfully produced the macroscopic assembly of E. coli into a self-standing hydrogel (Figures c,i and S10) upon mixing 20% w/v polymer solution in a PBS buffer (pH 8.1) with 0.33 g/mL of E. coli.

Rheological analysis was performed to quantify the mechanical properties of assembled materials. First, we compared the viscoelastic properties of the assembly with bacterial cell pellets and T1 polymer solutions without bacteria (Figure d). The significant difference across all the tested frequencies between assembled materials and these controls verifies the strong interaction between polymers and cells. In addition, when a cell pellet is mixed with unmodified polymer TC1, it exhibits low modulus values, distinct from cellular assemblies with T1 (Figure e). This clear difference confirmed the covalent nature of the assembly, and the bond formation is essential for developing useful mechanical properties.

Mechanical properties of materials assembled at room temperature (25 °C) and physiological temperature (37 °C) revealed the thermal effects on the assembly (Figure f). Consistently higher modulus values of materials assembled at 37 °C compared to those at 25 °C suggest that the difference in kinetics and degree of bond formation observed in small molecule experiments (Figure S4) is translated into the mechanical properties of assembled materials. Time-dependent rheological measurements revealed a gradual increase in the storage modulus over several hours at 25 °C (Figure g), highlighting the effect of cross-linking time on material stiffness. Higher polymer concentrations (5–20 w/v%) for the assembly correlated with increased material stiffness (Figures h and S10).

Selective Covalent Assembly of VS-Functionalized Polymers with Gram-negative Bacteria

Fluorescent probe labeling with BODIPY-VS suggests that VS small molecules are readily accessible to the surface proteins of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (Figure c–f). To our surprise, T1 polymer was unsuccessful in assembling Gram-positive bacteria (Figure a–d) while yielding reliable macroscopic assembly of Gram-negative bacteria (Figure e–i). Consistent with this observation, additional polymers (T2 and T3) also failed to induce gel formation with B. subtilis and S. epidermidis (Figure S11), suggesting that the lack of assembly is not unique to T1. The rheological measurements of the assembled materials with the other Gram-negative bacteria, Shewanella oneidensis, Serratia quinivorans, Pantoea agglomerans, and Serratia proteamaculans, demonstrated comparable mechanical strength (Figure j) to those of E. coli assemblies prepared under analogous conditions (Figure d). Representative cell pellet images of all Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains prior to assembly are provided in Figure S12.

With this observation, we attempted to investigate possible selective interactions at a microscopic level by incubating RFP-expressing Gram-positive bacterium B. subtilis and GFP-expressing E. coli with T1 under a more dilute condition (OD600 ∼ 1.1, cell suspension mixed with 20% w/v polymer, 37 °C, 500 rpm, 24 h). Optical microscopy revealed that the clusters of coassemblies are predominantly composed of E. coli cells (Figure m) with few red B. subtilis cells adhering to the structures. Incubating T1 with the RFP-expressing B. subtilis cells (OD600 ∼ 1.1, cell suspension mixed with 20% w/v polymer, 37 °C, 500 rpm, 24 h) showed minimal cluster formation (Figure S6.2). Collectively, these studies demonstrate the selective assembly preference of the vinyl sulfone polymers for Gram-negative bacteria.

Gram-positive bacteria possess a thick, highly cross-linked peptidoglycan cell wall decorated with teichoic acids, in contrast to double membranes with membrane proteins in Gram-negative bacteria. In our previous studies, , we reported macroscopic covalent assemblies of Gram-positive bacteria B. subtilis and S. epidermidis using phenylboronic acid–based multivalent polymers by targeting the abundant carbohydrate residues on the wall teichoic acids. Using these phenylboronic acid polymers, we also demonstrated a stable assembly with another Gram-positive bacterium, S. salivarius (Figure S6.3). We speculate that the accessibility of carbohydrates and proteins for macromolecular species in Gram-positive bacteria is the primary reason for the observed difference between VS small-molecule labeling results and polymer assembly. The thick, peptidoglycan mesh structure of the cell wall in Gram-positive bacteria imposes a steric constraint on polymer penetration, which is necessary to form covalent bonds with membrane proteins or other protein species residing in and beneath the cell wall. While small-molecule VS can penetrate the inner part of the peptidoglycan mesh and potentially reach the membrane, polymeric VS cannot form enough covalent bonds required for stable network formation due to the diffusion barrier. In contrast, exposed surface proteins on the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria are more accessible to VS polymers, allowing for multivalent cross-linking and yielding stable macroscopic assemblies.

Cell Viability and Containment in Engineered Covalent Assemblies

In prior studies examining E. coli within polymeric hydrogels, cellular viability, growth, colony formation, and containment have been shown to depend on the mechanical properties of the polymeric network and the initial cell densities. ,,− We sought to investigate cell viability and biocontainment in our engineered covalent assembly, which differs from previously reported material systems in that it involves direct covalent bond formation between polymeric and cellular components and has a higher cell density. We first performed erosion tests to assess the passive containment capabilities of the vinyl sulfone polymers in a buffer without nutrients (150 rpm, 25 °C). We did not observe significant cell leakage over time with the covalently assembled materials, whereas the control mixture prepared with TC1 dissolved completely (Figures b and S7). This clear distinction in containment showed the effectiveness of covalent cross-linking in preventing bacterial escape. The structural integrity of the materials was also preserved throughout the agitation process. The macroscopic appearance was retained after extended immersion and agitation, confirming the mechanical stability of the covalent assembly (Figure S7).

Immersion of the covalently assembled materials in LB and M9 minimal media revealed clear nutrient-dependent differences in bacterial growth. When immersed in nutrient-rich LB medium (150 rpm, 25 °C), we observed growth of E. coli outside the materials (Figure c). While the materials maintained their structural integrity (Figure a), a visible increase in optical density in the medium (Figure S7) indicated that some bacterial cells near the surface were able to break free and proliferate. While no bacterial cell proliferation was observed in M9 minimal medium, with OD600 values remaining near baseline throughout the experiment, supplementing M9 with a low concentration of yeast extract (0.1%) triggered cell escape and growth, albeit with lower turbidity than in LB medium (Figure c). These results demonstrate that biocontainment effectiveness in this covalent assembly of Gram-negative bacteria is significantly influenced by the nutrient content of the surrounding media.

Unlike dynamic covalent bond assemblies in our prior studies, − once the covalent connection between VS and the E. coli surface is broken, there is no mechanism for reattachment. In addition, Dekker and co-workers have demonstrated that E. coli can undergo significant deformation to pass through microfluidic channels that are smaller than its size. Taken together, we speculate that the unique deformability of E. coli and the irreversible nature of covalent bond breaking are the reasons for the observed nutrient-dependent release profile of the covalently assembled materials with E. coli.

We assessed the viability of assembled cells. Due to the irreversible nature of the covalent assembly and the limited penetration of fluorogenic probes, we opted to test cell viability by dissecting assemblies after 24 h incubation at room temperature. We performed the 5-cyano-2,3-ditolyl tetrazolium chloride (CTC) assay, which measures the respiratory activity of viable cells by forming an insoluble, fluorescent formazan product. Dissected materials in a 5 mM CTC solution developed an orange-red color and fluorescent formazan product, indicating the presence of metabolically active cells throughout the material (Figure S8). To further quantify cell viability in these assemblies, we stained the micron-sized clusters with a similar cell and polymer composition to that of the macroscopic materials, but assembled under a more dilute condition. Bacterial metabolic activity was maintained for 20 h with uniform distribution of insoluble, fluorescent product within the assembly (Figures d and S8).

Functional Living Materials Producing Melanin and Enriching Tyrosine-Derived Red Pigments in the Supernatant

To demonstrate functional output from the engineered living material, we utilized engineered E. coli expressing the HpaBC hydroxylase complex to biosynthesize melanin from exogenously supplied tyrosine. − In this pathway (Figure a), HpaBC catalyzes the conversion of tyrosine into L-DOPA, which spontaneously oxidizes to dopaquinone. Dopaquinone rapidly oxidizes to dopachrome and subsequently rearranges to form 5,6-dihydroxyindole (DHI) or 5,6-dihydroxyindole-2-carboxylic acid (DHICA). These intermediates polymerize to form melanin. , Melanin biosynthesis in living materials can be useful for encoding bioinspired thermoregulatory, UV-protective, and heavy-metal chelating functions. −

(f) Frequency sweeps of hydrated assembly producing melanin and control.

Melanin accumulation was evident in both macroscopic assemblies and in free-floating cells suspended in M9 buffer supplemented with tyrosine, with clear differences in color development and material deposition over time. We monitored tyrosine conversion to downstream DOPA species by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm of cell-free supernatants from both assembly and free-cell suspensions, which indicated the conversion of tyrosine to melanin intermediates over time (Figure b). To our surprise, the assembly derived supernatants were red in color, in contrast to the characteristic dark brown color of the free-floating, melanin-producing cell culture (Figure c). On the other hand, assemblies transitioned from light brown to dark black color over multiple days (Figures d and S9), consistent with gradual oxidative polymerization of tyrosine-derived intermediates. The assemblies exhibited predominantly surface-localized pigmentation with minimal coloration in the supernatant, indicating that melanin was largely deposited on or within the assembly.

UV–visible spectra further validated the melanin-like nature of the pigments produced. The supernatant from free-floating cell culture displayed broad, featureless absorption from UV to visible wavelengths (Figure e), a characteristic of melanin polymers. On the other hand, the red supernatant from the assembly shows absorbance peaks between 320–340 nm and 450–500 nm, which suggest characteristic oligomeric behavior of DHI/DHICA and DOPA intermediates. Rheological measurements on materials producing melanin in a buffer show significantly higher stiffness than the control materials immersed in the same buffer condition but not producing melanin (Figure f), highlighting that melanin formation within the material is reinforcing the network and preventing further bacterial escape at 30 °C (Figure S9). Further, high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) analysis of the red supernatant revealed a prominent ion at m/z 512.1069 in positive ion mode, likely a melanin intermediate (Figure S9). These results not only showcase in situ melanin production from living materials but also the effect of spatial confinement on enzymatic functions, as the melanin production pathway is strongly influenced by oxidative conditions and tyrosine availability.

Conclusion

This work presents a strategy for covalently assembling Gram-negative bacteria with synthetic polymers using VS motif to yield macroscopic living materials. Importantly, we found that this assembly process exhibits a remarkable selectivity for Gram-negative bacteria. Given that small-molecule VS probe labels both Gram-negative and Gram-positive cells, the observed selectivity is likely due to bacterial cell-surface structures and diffusion barriers for polymeric species. The mechanical properties of macroscopic assemblies were characterized by varying VS reaction parameters, including polymer structure, polymer concentration, cross-linking duration, and temperature. These assemblies remained stable under prolonged agitation, limiting cellular escape and maintaining material integrity. The assembled cells exhibited nutrient-dependent proliferation and high viability. We further demonstrated the utility of these materials for melanin production. Deposition of melanin on living materials imparts new optical and physical properties. Moreover, we observed enrichment of red pigment derived from oligomeric intermediates of the melanin synthesis pathway in the supernatant, highlighting the confinement effect on biochemical transformation. This finding suggests new opportunities for creating functional coatings using living materials.

Considering the significance of 3-dimensionally arranged functional Gram-negative bacterial communities in nature, − we envision our vinyl sulfone–mediated assemblies providing a path to a versatile platform for spatial engineering of stable, metabolically active, and functional living materials capable of performing complex biochemical transformations.
