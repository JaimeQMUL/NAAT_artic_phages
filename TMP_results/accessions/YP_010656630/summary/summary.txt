- **Maximum Growth Temperature ($	ext{T}_{	ext{max}}$):** Warm-temperature isolates exhibited a significantly higher mean $	ext{T}_{	ext{max}}$ ($35.6 	ext{ }^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) compared to cool-temperature isolates ($33.4 	ext{ }^	ext{o}	ext{C}$), with the warm isolate reaching $35.3 	ext{ }^	ext{o}	ext{C}$.
- **Minimum Growth Temperature ($	ext{T}_{	ext{min}}$) and Niche Width:** $	ext{T}_{	ext{min}}$ and niche width ($	ext{T}_{	ext{max}} - 	ext{T}_{	ext{min}}$) did not show significant differences between the two groups.
- **Thermal Performance Curves (TPC):** The TPC analysis revealed distinct thermal response curves, with the warm phenotype showing a clear advantage at higher temperatures.
- **Photophysiology:** At $28 	ext{ }^	ext{o}	ext{C}$, the warm-temperature isolate maintained significantly higher photosynthetic efficiency ($	ext{Fv/Fm}$) compared to the cool-temperature isolate. Furthermore, the warm isolate showed lower fluorescence at physiologically relevant temperatures, suggesting better maintenance of functionality under thermal stress.
- **Enzymatic/Molecular Adaptation:** Methylation patterns were observed in coding regions of photosystem-related genes (e.g., phycobilisome linker proteins, ATPase subunits, photosystem I/II components) that differed between the warm and cool isolates, suggesting an epigenetic mechanism potentially linked to thermal specialization.
- **Growth/Survival:** The warm phenotype demonstrated a growth advantage above the maximum historical summer surface water temperature recorded at the collection site, suggesting potential adaptation to future warming.

Environmental Context:
- **Habitat/Source:** Isolated from a coastal marine environment (Narragansett Bay). The study compared strains enriched at ambient/cool temperatures ($18-22 	ext{ }^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) against strains enriched at elevated/warm temperatures ($30 	ext{ }^	ext{o}	ext{C}$). The warm phenotype was tested at a temperature exceeding current site records.

Concise Summary:
This study demonstrates the coexistence of distinct, cryptic thermal phenotypes within a single, genetically highly similar population of a marine cyanobacterium. Thermal selection experiments separated the population into distinct cool- and warm-temperature thermotypes. Phenotypic differences were pronounced: warm-adapted isolates exhibited significantly higher maximum growth temperatures ($	ext{T}_{	ext{max}}$) and optimal growth temperatures ($	ext{T}_{	ext{opt}}$) compared to cool-adapted isolates. These thermal differences were correlated with superior photosynthetic efficiency ($	ext{Fv/Fm}$) and maintenance of function under thermal stress in the warm phenotype. Crucially, these functional differences were not attributable to detectable genomic variations (ANI $>99.99%$). Instead, the study identified differential cytosine methylation patterns within genes related to the photosynthetic machinery (e.g., phycobilisome components) as the only observed molecular difference correlating with thermal specialization. The findings suggest that epigenetic mechanisms, rather than core genome changes, may underpin thermal microdiversity, providing a potential mechanism for population resilience to projected ocean warming.

- Post-harvest temperature abuse provided favorable conditions for *Vibrio* proliferation directly within oyster tissues.

Environmental Context:
- The organisms were studied in the context of aquaculture systems (bottom and floating cages) in coastal bay waters, and in post-harvest handling scenarios (temperature abuse).

Concise Summary:
The study investigated the impact of aquaculture practices and post-harvest handling on microbial dynamics. Key thermal observations include the simulation of temperature abuse at 25°C for 24 hours, which was noted to favor *Vibrio* proliferation. General findings indicate that *Vibrio* species exhibit rapid growth potential, with concentrations increasing significantly (up to 100-fold) within 10 hours at ambient temperatures. The stability of bottom cage environments was associated with metabolically active *Vibrio* populations, while temperature abuse was shown to favor proliferation in floating cage oysters. The research emphasizes that post-harvest temperature abuse is a critical factor influencing pathogen load, suggesting that thermal stress responses are highly relevant to the observed microbial dynamics.
