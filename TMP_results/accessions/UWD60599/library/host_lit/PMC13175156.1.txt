Introduction:

In response to the opioid epidemic, our practice substantially reduced the number of opioid pills prescribed or dispensed to patients after fracture surgery. This study explored patient outcomes under this protocol.

Methods:

In this survey of 125 adult patients from one level I trauma center combined with retrospective chart review, patients had undergone surgery for distal radius, proximal humerus, femoral neck, tibial shaft, or lateral malleolus fracture (n = 25 patients per location). The pain management protocol reduced the total number of opioid pills dispensed at discharge to 42 (oxycodone 5 mg or hydromorphone 2 mg, taken as needed every 6 hours), advising discontinuation within 3 days. Patients were evaluated at least 3 months postoperatively. Patient-reported outcome measures, cross-referenced with chart data, were assessed using the Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire (PSEQ) and the Return-to-Work Self-Efficacy questionnaire and were compared. Pain was assessed using verbal and numeric rating scales.

Results:

Median pain scores for all five groups corresponded to mild pain on both rating scales. PSEQ and Return-to-Work Self-Efficacy responses demonstrated high confidence, with median scores at or exceeding 4 on a 0 to 6 Likert scale. Two PSEQ questions regarding the ability to enjoy activities and gradually increase movement despite pain showed notable differences across surgical groups.

Conclusion:

Fewer opioid pills effectively managed postoperative pain and aligned with high patient self-efficacy. Consistency between patient-reported outcome measures and medical records supported these findings. These observations are exploratory and should be interpreted in the context of a descriptive design.

Level of Evidence:

III

The opioid epidemic in the United States is a critical public health challenge, with the notable contribution of prescription opioids. Over the past two decades, prescription opioid overdose deaths have nearly quintupled, leading to approximately 45 fatalities a day in 2021 alone.1 Even after low-risk procedures, 80% of patients fill opioid prescriptions.2 Despite broad state and institutional interventions,3 few reports describe optimal prescribing practices that balance effective pain management with minimization of opioid dependency harms.

Orthopaedic surgery has been a focal point in the ongoing opioid crisis.4 Orthopaedic surgeons are the third-highest prescribers of opioids in the United States, and four of the top five procedures for postoperative opioid dispensing in adults aged 45 to 64 years are orthopaedic.5,6 Commonly prescribed opioids, including hydrocodone and oxycodone, are used to manage acute pain after both elective and trauma-related surgeries. A 2019 study found that 42.3% of orthopaedic surgeons acknowledged prescribing opioids to patients who later developed opioid use disorder, yet 35.3% did not perceive opioid overprescribing as a concern within their practice.7 Orthopaedic surgeons prescribe opioids at a rate disproportionately greater than that of other specialties,8 with a greater tendency in the United States than in international peers, who favor nonopioid analgesic alternatives.9,10

Despite increasing awareness of the opioid epidemic and changing prescribing patterns,11,12 substantial variability in pain management practices persists within orthopaedic surgery, particularly in outpatient settings.13 Patients are amenable to multimodal pain control, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, acetaminophen, gabapentinoids, and even nonpharmacologic strategies, after orthopaedic trauma.14,15 Although some orthopaedic postoperative guidelines exist,16 few are tailored to orthopaedic trauma surgery. The “Lopioid” protocol, which uses tramadol, meloxicam, gabapentin, and as-needed acetaminophen, has been shown to reduce opioid consumption.17 However, that study addressed less common injuries and not the more prevalent fractures typically associated with higher surgery volumes and greater pain management needs. Emerging evidence supports institutional opioid-sparing protocols after joint arthroplasty and general polytrauma (adjusted analgesia by age, creatinine clearance, and specific medical conditions) may maintain effective pain management and patient satisfaction while lowering dependency risk.18-20 Such findings highlight the need to identify specific opioid-prescribing patterns that both optimize outcomes and maintain patient safety by preventing both overprescription of opioids and undertreatment of pain.

In 2018, our orthopaedic trauma department implemented a protocol that reduced the number of postoperative opioid pills dispensed at discharge from 120 to 42 pills, on average.21 This study evaluated the association of this reduced prescribing protocol with patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), including pain levels and patient self-efficacy, after orthopaedic trauma surgery. By quantifying opioid prescribing patterns and correlating these with patient-reported measures of pain and self-efficacy, the goal of this research was to provide actionable, evidence-based guidelines for safer prescribing practices.

Methods

Design and Setting

This study used a phone survey (Supplemental File 1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A525) and retrospective chart review design to evaluate postoperative pain management and self-efficacy following the implementation of a reduced, 42-pill opioid protocol consisting of oxycodone 5 mg or hydromorphone 2 mg taken as needed every 6 hours, with discontinuation within 3 days advised. The study was approved by the Johns Hopkins Medicine Institutional Review Boards (IRB00443764). Details of multimodal pain management are available in Supplemental File 2 (http://links.lww.com/JG9/A526). At hospital discharge, patients were instructed to start or continue a multimodal pain management regimen unless contraindicated. This included scheduled aspirin 81 mg every 12 hours (84 pills), gabapentin 300 mg every 8 hours (42 pills) or a reduced dose of gabapentin 300 mg daily for those with renal insufficiency (14 pills), acetaminophen 1 g every 8 hours (maximum 4 g/d; 42 pills of 500 mg each), and ibuprofen 400 mg every 8 hours (42 pills). Opioids—either oxycodone 5 mg or hydromorphone 2 mg—were dispensed at discharge, to be taken as needed every 6 hours, with 42 pills provided. Patients were advised to use opioids sparingly, taking oxycodone only as needed and no more often than every 6 hours, with a strong recommendation to discontinue opioid use within 3 to 5 days.

Patient Selection Criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 18 years or older, had undergone surgical treatment for one of five specific fracture locations (distal radius, proximal humerus, femoral neck, tibial shaft, or lateral malleolus), and had completed at least 3 months of follow-up. These fracture locations were selected based on their high prevalence in orthopaedic trauma surgery, the variability in pain and recovery associated with each injury, and their frequent management with opioid analgesics. The inclusion of these fracture locations provided a comprehensive, representative cohort to evaluate the effects of opioid use on functional outcomes across diverse anatomic sites. Patients were excluded if they were younger than 18 years, were non-English speaking, resided outside the United States, or had psychiatric or neurocognitive conditions preventing consent and survey completion. A Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials diagram is available in Supplemental File 3 (http://links.lww.com/JG9/A527).

Data Collection and Outcome Measures

Baseline demographic and clinical data for all eligible patients were extracted from Electronic Patient Information and Clinical system, our institution's electronic health record system, and the Chesapeake Regional Information System for our patients, a Mid-Atlantic health-information exchange. Baseline demographic, social history, clinical characteristics, and opioid use data collected are detailed in Supplemental File 4 (http://links.lww.com/JG9/A528). Body mass index (BMI) was included to assess its potential association with postoperative recovery, pain levels, and pain self-efficacy, given its known links to surgical complications and opioid use.22-24 This allowed for a more complete evaluation of factors influencing postoperative outcomes.

Additional data collection was conducted through 10-minute phone surveys administered at least 3 months after surgery, consistent with the inclusion criteria. This timing was chosen to assess medium-term postoperative pain and functional outcomes, rather than acute pain immediately after surgery, and, thus, reflected pain experienced during the recovery and healing phases. Pain was assessed using a verbal rating scale in which No pain was classified as 0 and Pain as bad as it could possibly be was classified as four, alongside a numeric rating scale (NRS) from 0 (no pain) to 10 (pain as bad as it could possibly be). PROMs were assessed using (1) the validated Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire (PSEQ),25,26 with scores ranging from 0 (not at all confident) to 6 (completely confident), with higher scores indicating greater confidence in managing pain, and (2) the validated Return-to-Work Self-Efficacy (RTWSE) scale,27,28 scored from 0 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), reflecting confidence in returning to work despite pain. Retired patients completed only RTWSE questions 6 to 8. Survey data were cross-referenced with electronic medical records to verify accuracy and completeness.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were summarized as means with SDs, whereas non-normally distributed data were reported as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Group comparisons for pain levels, PSEQ, and RTWSE responses across the five fracture locations were done using the Kruskal-Wallis test, appropriate for nonparametric and ordinal data. With 25 patients per group (N = 125) and an assumed NRS SD = 2.0, the study has 80% power (α = 0.05) to detect a between-group difference of ≈1.62 NRS points (Cohen d ≈ 0.81). Post hoc pairwise comparisons were then done with multiple comparison adjustments to identify statistically significant group differences contributing to overall group effects. Associations for categorical variables (eg, opioid use, receipt of naloxone prescription) were assessed using Fisher exact test for small, expected counts <10 or the chi-squared test for expected counts ≥10. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Correlation analyses were done to assess the concordance between patient-reported data and electronic medical records for opioid use, opioid prescription refills, receipt of a naloxone prescription, and use of acetaminophen, gabapentin, ibuprofen, marijuana, and ice. Missing values were excluded from the analyses.

Results

Demographics and Social History

The study was designed to recruit a total of 125 patients, providing adequate power (approximately 80%) to detect clinically meaningful differences in overall pain and self-efficacy outcomes, while ensuring balanced representation across five common fracture types (n = 25 each): distal radius, proximal humerus, femoral neck, tibial shaft, and lateral malleolus. Demographic data are available in Table 1. The mean age did not vary markedly across groups (P = 0.11). Sex distribution differed markedly (P = 0.04), with men predominating in the femoral neck and tibial shaft groups (60.0% each) and women being more prevalent in distal radius (84.0%), proximal humerus (64.0%), and lateral malleolus (80.0%) groups (Table 1). BMI at the time of surgery (P = 0.01) and most recently recorded BMI (P = 0.02) varied markedly, with additional analysis indicating notable differences between the distal radius and lateral malleolus groups (most recent BMI, P = 0.01; BMI at time of surgery, P = 0.004). Other demographic characteristics, such as race, ethnicity, insurance type, and baseline physical activity, were generally comparable across fracture location groups. Social history data are presented in Table 2. Substance use and abuse patterns did not differ markedly among fracture groups.

Mechanisms of injury differed markedly across fracture groups (P < 0.0001, Table 3), with falls most commonly seen with fractures of the distal radius (60.0%), lateral malleolus (60.0%), and proximal humerus (48.0%), and motor vehicle accidents being the most common cause of femoral neck (60.0%) and tibial shaft (36.0%) fractures. Gunshot injuries were reported exclusively in the tibial shaft group (8.0%). Workers' compensation utilization was minimal across all cohorts, with only six patients reporting its use, four of whom had tibial shaft fractures (16.0%, P = 0.08; Table 3).

Patient-Reported Outcome Measures

Survey results are presented in Table 4. Pain level was similarly mild across all fracture location groups, with a median verbal rating scale score of 1 (mild pain) out of 4 (P = 0.20) and NRS scores ranging from 1 to 3 out of 10 (no to mild pain; P = 0.11). PSEQ responses demonstrated high confidence in pain management, with all median scores at or exceeding a score of 4 on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (no confidence) to 6 (greatest confidence; Table 4). Two PSEQ items revealed notable group differences:PSEQ question 1, “I can enjoy things, despite the pain” (P = 0.004): Scores were markedly lower in the lateral malleolus group than in the distal radius (P = 0.01) and proximal humerus (P = 0.02) groups.

PSEQ question 10, “I can gradually become more active, despite the pain” (P = 0.047): Scores were markedly lower in the tibial shaft group than in the distal radius group (P = 0.049) .

At the time of the phone survey, 72 patients (57.6%) had returned to work, 22 patients (17.6%) were retired, and five patients (4.0%) were receiving disability benefits. Twenty-three patients (18.4%) were unable to return to work, whereas three patients (2.4%) were not employed before surgery and remained unemployed at the time of the survey. RTWSE scores exceeded the neutral benchmark (3.0) across all groups, with no notable differences among fracture locations (Table 4). These results reflect uniformly high patient confidence in their ability to manage various aspects of returning to work, including discussing physical limitations with supervisors and coworkers, managing pain effectively while working, and avoiding reinjury.

Opioid Use and Pain Management Practices

Postoperative opioid use rates did not differ markedly among fracture location groups (Table 4). Numbers of leftover opioid pills were similar across fracture locations (P = 0.33) at 41.2% to 68.8% of cases, with the highest rates in the distal radius and proximal humerus (68.8%) groups. Refills were infrequent (61.9% to 81.0% requiring none); the tibial shaft group had the highest proportion, needing three or more refills (23.8%; Table 4). Negative opioid effects did not vary markedly across cohorts (P = 0.07). No notable differences were observed in the use of acetaminophen, ibuprofen, gabapentin, or ice among fracture locations (Table 4). Postoperative marijuana use differed markedly among groups, with the highest rate seen in tibial shaft fractures (40.0%, P = 0.049).

Discussion

In this study, fewer opioid pills were perceived by patients to manage postoperative medium-term pain effectively beyond the acute recovery period. Across all fracture locations evaluated, median pain scores remained within the mild pain range, and PROMs, as measured by PSEQ and RTWSE scores, exceeded neutral benchmarks, indicating strong confidence in managing pain and resuming daily activities. Importantly, opioid refills were infrequent, and a notable proportion of patients reported leftover opioids, suggesting the potential for further prescription reductions. These results support the hypothesis that effective postoperative pain management does not require heavy reliance on opioids, aligning with emerging literature that highlights the potential benefits of opioid-sparing protocols.7,8

The success of this protocol of a maximum of 42 opioid pills dispensed at discharge may be attributed to several factors. First, the inclusion of multimodal analgesia such as acetaminophen, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and ice as an adjunct likely contributed to effective pain relief while mitigating opioid-related risks. Previous studies have demonstrated that multimodal approaches enhance pain alleviation and reduce opioid consumption without compromising patient satisfaction.9,13,17 In the arthroplasty literature, studies have compared preprotocol (70.5% to 79.7%) and postprotocol (72.8% to 82.1%) pain-control satisfaction rates after reduced opioid prescribing.18,19 However, these measures were based on multiple-choice surveys assessing patient perioperative experience, designed primarily for the Value-Based Purchasing Program to inform reimbursement adjustments. This context limits the direct applicability of their findings to broader assessments of pain management effectiveness. Furthermore, the emphasis on early opioid cessation, within 3 days postoperatively, may have curtailed the transition from acute to chronic opioid use, an essential consideration in preventing opioid dependence.5

Despite notable variations in injury mechanisms and preoperative opioid use, no notable variations were observed in patient-reported pain or self-efficacy scores across the five fracture location groups. This underscores the robustness of the protocol applied in this study across diverse patient cohorts and injury types. However, specific group differences in PSEQ responses highlight areas for improvement. For instance, lower confidence scores in patients with tibial shaft and lateral malleolus fracture, particularly in “enjoying activities despite pain” (PSEQ question 1) and “gradually increasing activity despite pain” (PSEQ question 10) (Table 4), may reflect the greater severity of these injuries and the psychologic burden associated with prolonged recovery periods.16 Addressing these disparities through enhanced patient education, preemptive counseling, and tailored multimodal strategies could further optimize outcomes.29,30

Despite the reduced number of opioid pills dispensed, a notable proportion of patients, especially those with distal radius and proximal humerus fractures, reported having leftover opioid pills. In addition, opioid refills were infrequent across all fracture locations, although the tibial shaft group had the highest rate (three or more refills [23.8%]), likely reflecting greater injury severity and prolonged recovery. These findings suggest that, although fewer opioid pills are effective for many, some patients may require more individualized pain management. Personalizing prescriptions based on patient-specific factors, such as injury type, pain tolerance, and preoperative opioid use, could optimize pain management while minimizing surplus opioids.13,16

The high concordance between patient-reported and chart-based records strengthens the reliability of these findings. Agreement >50% in reporting opioid use, refill attainment, and marijuana use across all cohorts underscores the validity of these data sources. However, discrepancies in naloxone prescribing agreement between cohorts highlight potential gaps in documentation or patient recall. Addressing these inconsistencies through standardized recordkeeping and comprehensive patient education regarding multimodal options could further enhance the accuracy of future research.17,31

This study contributes to efforts to guide physicians and their teams in tailoring their pain management regimens—not only to provide patient comfort and satisfaction but also to reduce reliance on opioids and mitigate associated risks such as dependence, prolonged use, and opioid-related complications in orthopaedic surgical settings—thereby improving patient care and safety. Our findings reinforce the feasibility of reducing the number of opioid pills provided to patients with orthopaedic trauma at discharge, aligning with previous findings by Bloom et al,18 who demonstrated that reduced opioid prescriptions after total hip arthroplasty did not diminish patient satisfaction, and Manjunath et al,19 who reported outcomes in total knee arthroplasty similar to those of Bloom et al and this study. Importantly, our study extends these findings to a trauma cohort, emphasizing the generalizability of such protocols across subspecialties. However, the frequency of leftover opioid pills (ranging from 41.2% to 68.8%) and the infrequent need for refills (with up to 81.0% requiring none) are consistent with findings by Haddad et al,16 who advocated for refined, data-driven prescribing practices tailored to individual patient needs.32

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and reliance on self-reported data introduces potential biases, such as recall bias and underreporting of opioid use. Cross-referencing patient-reported outcomes with medical records improved data accuracy, and the use of validated tools (PSEQ and RTWSE) strengthened outcome reliability. The moderate agreement between patient reports and medical records reflects both documentation and recall limitations while highlighting the complexities of real-world clinical practice and reinforcing the external validity of our findings. Thus, the minimum follow-up period of 3 months may not adequately capture the immediate postoperative period or long-term outcomes, including delayed dependency or chronic pain. However, given the high attrition rates commonly observed in trauma cohorts, this time frame strikes a balance between feasibility and data collection, allowing for the inclusion of a larger patient sample despite inherent challenges in this subspecialty. Importantly, the 3-month time frame also situates pain ratings and self-efficacy measures within the context of patients' return to daily activities and work, while serving as a hypothesis of generating foundation for future investigations into short-term postoperative pain trajectories. Although the overall sample size (N = 125) created robust measures, subgroup analyses by fracture type (n ≈ 25 each) were exploratory and may be underpowered. The study was designed to ensure cohort-level power and representation across fracture categories, so individual subgroup results should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, opioid-intensive regimens were unavailable, precluding control comparisons, although findings may inform future efforts to improve quality. Future research should address these limitations by using larger, multicenter cohorts; extending follow-up periods; and incorporating real-time tracking of medication use to improve data accuracy and capture comprehensive long-term outcomes.33,34

Conclusion

In this exploratory study, allowing a smaller number of opioid pills after orthopaedic trauma was associated with generally mild pain, maintenance of self-efficacy, and infrequent refills at medium-term follow-up. These preliminary findings are consistent with the potential for safe opioid reduction. Larger, prospective studies are needed to confirm the results.
