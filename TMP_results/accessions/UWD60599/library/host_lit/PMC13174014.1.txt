Background

Existing self-administered, pre-participation screening questionnaires for physical exercise have not demonstrated high take-up rates in many populations. There is evidence that part of the reason for this may be the language of these questionnaires and difficulty in understanding these questions by lay persons, especially those with existing medical conditions that may be of concern if engaging in physical exercise. The medical referral rates for existing questionnaires appears excessive. There has also not been a demonstrable decrease in sports emergencies following current use of these questionnaires.

Main body

Based on the lessons learnt from previous surveys and the evidence behind existing questionnaires, an eight-question International Physical Exercise Screening Questionnaire (IPESQ) is proposed. This paper discusses the roles played by pre-participation screening questionnaires in preventing or minimizing the occurrence of sports emergencies, desired characteristics of pre-participation exercise screening questionnaires and describes the justification for each of the IPESQ questions and how the Questionnaire may be implemented. It proposes a tool that is based on studies that have actively sought public feedback, and used the level of evidence currently available in the scientific literature as proof of relevance and usefulness.

Conclusion

The proposed IPESQ will need to be actively implemented and its impact determined with monitoring of sports-related emergency rates. It is relevant to an international audience, since sports emergencies such as cardiovascular collapse, injuries and heat illnesses are universal phenomena. The IPESQ can be regarded as a promising but not yet proven instrument to minimize the occurrence of sports emergencies.

Introduction

There have been many studies, including randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses that have extolled the immediate, short-term, and long-term benefits of physical exercise (PE) in persons of many age-groups [1]. At the same time, it is important to recognise the risks of medical and surgical emergencies associated with undue physical exertion, not just for athletes, whether competitive or recreational, but also for the general public engaging in physical activity. Some of the stated benefits and risks of PE are as given in Table 1 [2–37].

On balance, though, the benefits of exercise are thought to far outweigh its risks. In an attempt to get people of all ages to engage in sporting activities and be physically active, and yet trying to minimize the occurring of sports emergencies, attempts have been made to identify those at risk of these during PE. Medical screening before PE, also known as pre-exercise screening, helps identify individuals at higher risk of adverse emergency events during exercise [38]. Pre-exercise screening is not only for competitive athletes but also for the bulk of members of the public who may be considered to be recreational athletes. It’s a safety measure to ensure the benefits of exercise outweigh potential risks. Exercise has also been the backbone for rehabilitation across a wide range of medical and physical conditions.

A variety of pre-exercise screening systems have been used around the world. Some of these include the guidelines issued by the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PARQ+) produced by the Health & Fitness Federation of Canada and that has been used in many countries around the world for many years and the more recent Get Active Questionnaire put out by the Canadian Society for Exercise Physiologists [39–41].

The ACSM provides guidelines and recommendations for exercise preparticipation health screening, which are used to help determine if individuals are safe to begin or continue exercising [39]. They are not mandated by law in most places, including in the USA, but they are generally regarded as good practice by healthcare professionals and fitness professionals in preventing exercise related emergencies. Because ACSM does not have a specific self-administered screening questionnaire, the actual application of these guidelines tend to be influenced by regional factors, including the availability of healthcare resources, cultural norms, and the type of sports or activities being pursued. ACSM emphasizes that pre-participation screening should consider an individual’s activity level, desired intensity, and known health conditions, rather than requiring blanket medical clearance for everyone. This is in line with moves towards a more liberal approach to pre-participation screening that results in fewer individuals needing to seek medical clearance before starting a physical activity program. The number of communities or individuals that adopt ACSM guidelines for pre-participation screening is not known.

The PAR-Q + is said to be designed to help one assess one’s own level of readiness for physical activity. It was originally designed based on expert opinion without systematic evidence-based support [42]. This has limited the use of the forms by members of the public. The PAR-Q has sometimes been seen as a barrier to physical exercise participation for those who may be likely to benefit from routine physical activity. The PAR-Q was also recognized as being conservative leading to many false positives. In addition, inconsistent and/or improper usage of the clearance forms were often noted. Most of those with a chronic medical condition that answer ‘YES’ to one or more of the questions also did not receive or seek medical clearance for physical activity. Doctors often found the exercise clearance process cumbersome and time-consuming. Successive revisions of the document attempted to address these issues. On 2 December 2023, the PAR-Q+ Collaboration announced that “it is been estimated that millions of individuals from around the world currently use the PAR-Q + and/or ePARmed-X + for pre-participation screening and risk stratification” [43]. However, there is no published literature as to how many countries have adopted the PAR-Q + or how many persons are actively using this. In spite of this, sports emergencies such as injuries and sudden deaths continue to occur in countries around the world with no obvious decrease in incidence.

The Get Active Questionnaire (GAQ), is primarily used in Canada as a pre-screening tool for physical activity and exercise, intended for Canadians of all ages [41]. It is also used in the UK, specifically for pregnancy and identifies those who need medical advice before or during physical activity. The depth of penetration in use of the GAQ in Canada and the UK is not known. It has been used sporadically in some other countries. Some communities have expressed concerns at the complexity and difficulty in understanding and self-administering the GAQ in their local populations [44]. The take-up rate of the GAQ in the countries in which it has been used has not been documented.

Trends in exercise-related injuries and cardiac arrest

In spite of the proliferation of many pre-participation screening guidelines and questionnaires around the world, the available reported evidence suggests mixed trends for sports emergencies such as injuries and cardiac arrest during physical exercise. A nearly 50% fall in sports-related emergencies over 10 years (2012–2021) has been reported in the USA, with over 1.1 million persons treated in emergency departments in 2021, down from over 2.1 million in 2012, with the bulk of the fall occurring during the initial years of the COVID-19 pandemic [45]. This fall may have been attributed to the severe decrease in competitive and recreational sport during that period. At the same time, however, the National Safety Council in the USA reported that after an initial fall in 2020, there has been a steady increase in sports and recreational-related injuries and medical emergencies by 20% in 2021, 12% in 2022, and 2% in 2023 [46]. However, in certain populations, such as the elderly, sports-related orthopedic injuries increased in both number and incidence over the same period (2012–2021) [47]. In Singapore with her use of multiple screening questionnaires over the years, especially the PAR-Q and GAQ, there was a notable increase in emergencies associated with individual sports like climbing, gym workouts, and cycling, while team sports emergencies decreased [48]. A recent report from the United Kingdom revealed that sports emergencies are on the rise, particularly among younger athletes, emphasising the necessity for effective prevention and management strategies. However, the incidence of sudden cardiac arrest during sports had not changed over a decade (2005–2016), remaining stable at around 6.9 cases per million inhabitants per year, suggesting a need for improvement in screening strategies [49, 50].

The authors propose an International Physical Exercise Screening Questionnaire (IPESQ) to address the issues identified with currently available such tools, suggest measures for wide implementation of this tool and follow up to determine its value in minimizing exercise-related emergencies in the community.

Would sports emergencies decrease with consistent use of Pre-participation screening questionnaires?

Rationally, the answer would be Yes. Consistent use of pre-participation screening questionnaires, (PPSQs), can help reduce the risk of sports emergencies. While they may not identify individual abnormalities, they could identify pre-existing conditions, injuries or imbalances that may make a recreational or competitive athlete more vulnerable to a sports emergency and, thus, identify those who require further detailed medical evaluation. For example, a history of ankle sprains might lead to targeted balance training to reduce the risk of re-injury. Early identification of conditions, such as heart abnormalities, that may put an athlete at risk during sports allows for appropriate medical attention and guidance. By identifying and addressing potential risks, these questionnaires should contribute to the overall health and safety of members of the public. However, it is important to note that while these questionnaires can help reduce the risk of injury, they are not a guarantee of injury prevention. In summary, PPSQs can play a vital role in reducing the risk of sports emergencies by identifying risk factors, enabling personalized prevention strategies, and promoting the overall health and safety of those participating in physically intense exercise.

Why have currently available questionnaires not had a major impact?

There can be many reasons for this. Previously, no studies had been reported that address whether the sets of questions in any of the available questionnaires were easily understood. The questionnaires were drawn up by experts in medicine and sports, who often resorted to use of technical terminology, which, sometimes, were not clearly understood by lay persons who would need to use them. In Singapore, an English speaking community, previous attempts at getting members of the public to use either the PAR-Q or the GAQ on a large scale failed because for those who answered Yes to any of the questions in these questionnaires, there were additional questions to be addressed. This appeared awkward for exercise participants. There was also no common understanding of the questions posed in each of the questionnaires. Courses had to be organized for members of sports associations so that they could be taught how to interpret these questions. In an attempt to better gauge the understanding of these questionnaires by lay persons, public surveys were conducted. The one seeking feedback on the PAR-Q + was answered by 1019 lay persons and the one on the GAQ by 1043 persons [44, 51]. These two surveys showed that:

For the PAR-Q+, 43.4% would have required referral to a medical practitioner for review before they could be told whether they would be fit to continue with PE. For the GAQ, it was 32.9%. These surveys, suggested that if the PAR-Q + had only referred those with uncontrolled medical conditions, the medical referral rate may have decreased to 10.9%, and with the use of the GAQ to 2.7%. These would likely have made the use of these tools more acceptable to the wider population.

Of the main 7 PAR-Q+ questions, 4 were easily understood and 3 with slight difficulty. Of the 44 follow-up questions, 12 were easy to understand, 24 with slight difficulty and 8 with moderate difficulty. For the 9 GAQ questions, 2 were understood with significant difficulty, 3 with moderate difficulty and 4 with slight difficulty. About 45% of the responses from the GAQ survey participants were answered with a range of difficulty ranging from slightly to extremely difficult.

For both the PAR-Q + and the GAQ, those persons with co-morbidities experienced more difficulty in understanding the questions.

The PAR-Q+ survey was felt to be too long by 50.1% of survey participants. The same feedback was given by 24.3% of GAQ survey participants. More than half the participants from both surveys asked for the questionnaires to be shortened.

The PAR-Q survey was thought to have too many technical terms, including medical jargon by 46.9% of participants, versus 23.1% for the GAQ survey.

Participants from both surveys commented that heat disorders were missing from the PAR-Q + and GAQ documents and suggested that questions pertaining to this be included.

Suggestions for improving the questions were received from up to 42.4% of PAR-Q+ survey participants and 36.5% of GAQ survey participants.

A third survey was carried out on a draft questionnaire consisting of seven questions for what was to be considered as a local Singapore Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (SPARQ) document [52]. This draft document was well received by 1137 survey participants with 90.0% commenting that the quality of the advice provided was adequate and 93.1% being happy to follow it. The length of the questionnaire and the use of technical terms were felt to be adequate by 93.4%. In addition, suggestions for improvement were received from 26.6% of respondents.

All three surveys received many comments and suggestions for improvement [44, 51, 52].

Preferred characteristics of a pre-participation readiness questionnaire for physical exercise

Generally, 10 well-accepted principles may be considered when creating such a questionnaire:

Ease of Understanding: using clear, non-technical language to better ensure that individuals of all education levels can understand the question.

Conciseness: shorter and more focused to improve completion rates and reduce participant burden so that it does not result in a disincentive to engage in PE.

Comprehensiveness: so that the questions address key areas like medical history, current symptoms, medications, and family history to identify potential risks.

Individualized Approach: so as to tailor to the specific activity level and age of the individual, where applicable, recognizing that different populations may have different needs.

Sensitivity to Risk Factors: so as to effectively identify individuals with uncontrolled pre-existing conditions or symptoms likely to be exacerbated by exercise, such as heart problems, joint issues, or dizziness and not create conditions that excessively increase medical referral rates.

Clear Guidance: so as to provide clear instructions on what steps to take if any questions are answered “yes,” such as consulting a physician.

Cost and Comfort Level: so that the test is inexpensive and with minimal discomfort and morbidity to the participant.

Psychometric Properties: to better ensure strong psychometric properties, including validity and reliability, to ensure accurate and consistent results.

User-Friendly Design: to make it easy to administer and complete, whether in paper or online format.

Consideration for Diverse Populations: to be culturally sensitive and applicable to diverse populations, including individuals with disabilities.

Developing an International Physical Exercise Screening Questionnaire (IPESQ)

Most guidelines on pre-participation screening are based on retrospective studies concerning competitive athletes. The recommendations for the components of these guidelines and pre-participation tools were heterogeneous across organisations and geographic regions and were rarely based on evidence from comparative studies [53]. There have been no studies on individuals who exercise in a recreational setting. The level of evidence on which most guidelines and screening questionnaires are based is low. There is no robust evidence for the effect of pre-exercise screening tools on health outcomes. Rational reasoning and consensus have been major tools used to derive these, which, based on the available science and evidence, appear reasonable options. For the first time, however, we have public feedback on some of the questionnaires used. This was the basis for drafting an international physical exercise screening questionnaire that would likely address the ten principles listed above. Approval for the review of the screening questionnaires with feedback from public surveys was provided by the Institutional Review Board of the Singapore Sports Institute (SSG-EXP-002 dated 21 September 2021). The 8 components of the IPESQ, explaining the rationale for each question and how the lessons from the previous public feedback surveys have influenced these, are outlined below. The proposed IPESQ is as given in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1 The proposed International Physical Exercise Screening Questionnaire

Q1. Do you have heart disease that is not well controlled (i.e. chest pain, chest ache, chest tightness, chest discomfort and shortness of breath, in spite of medication) over the last six months?

The theme for this question was heart disease which includes known active heart disease that is not well controlled (e.g. patient is still symptomatic with chest ache, tightness, chest pain and breathless after mild exertion). It is comprehensive, indicates inadequate control because of continuing symptoms, describes the condition, explains the symptoms and likely to be easily understood. It is reasonably concise with explanations. There are no technical terms. There is good evidence suggesting that pre-participation screening for heart disease can decrease instances of sudden cardiac arrest during physical activity [54]. However, the recurrent theme is that it is not those with stable heart disease, but those who continue to manifest with symptoms who may be more prone to adverse consequences.

Q2: Do you have difficulty in controlling your high blood pressure (having headaches with dizziness and blurring of vision) with medicines over the last six months?

A review of 50 papers on exercise interventions in hypertensive subjects did not report adverse events if the baseline systolic blood pressure was between 140 and 180 mm Hg (mid-point 160 mm Hg) and mean diastolic blood pressure 90 mm Hg [55]. The European Society of Cardiology 2020 guidelines define poorly controlled hypertension as a resting SBP exceeding 160/90 mm Hg and recommends holding off maximal exercise stress testing until below this reading [56]. Neither the European guidelines nor the ACSM define uncontrolled hypertension by symptoms. The guidelines suggest that hypertensive patients may be regarded as low-risk if medically asymptomatic and BP < 160/90 mm Hg. Symptomatic hypertension has not been well defined. While there may be a wide variety of symptoms in those with poorly controlled blood pressure, the more severe ones refer to the presence of headaches and dizziness with blurring of vision. The question is concise, states difficulty in controlling high blood pressure and clarifies with symptoms that are usually easily understood by lay persons.

Q3. Do you feel more breathless when doing physical exercise than you usually would for that level of activity?

The Oregon-SUDS study reported an 18% incidence of unusual breathlessness in the four weeks prior to sudden cardiac arrest [57]. Such unusual breathlessness may also suggest future morbidity following strenuous exercise [58]. Hence, there would be a need to pay attention to any presence of unusual breathlessness during exertion as that may suggest either cardiac, respiratory or other causes (e.g. anaemia), all of which may have bad consequences, if not investigated early. In patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, association with sedentary lifestyle and cardiovascular diseases can increase the risk of adverse events during exercise [59]. Since the occurrence of unusual breathlessness during prior physical activity, rather than at rest, appears to be an early warning symptom for adverse cardiac or pulmonary consequences during exercise, this should constitute an important component of pre-participation screening. This question is clearly stated, concise and easy to understand, with no technical terms used. The lay person is not asked to make a diagnosis, but only recognize symptoms that are out of the ordinary.

Q4. Have you felt dizzy or fainted either during or shortly after physical exercise over the last six months?

Dizziness during or after PE, is often seen as a pre-syncopal symptom of cardiac arrythmias. Patients with non-lethal arrhythmias are unlikely to present with symptoms of dizziness and can exercise without increased risk [55]. Amongst adults and children, 21.4% to 24.0% of cases of SCA following exercise over a five-year period had herald symptoms, including syncope [60, 61]. The feedback from the public had been good for the previous questionnaires, including the PAR-Q+, GAQ and SPARQ, except for the suggestion to add the word “shortly” or “immediately” before the words physical exercise for greater clarity on the relationship to activity. Therefore, with the concise statement and non-use of medical jargon, this question would be likely to be easily understood by laypersons.

Q5. Do you have a chronic medical condition that you find difficult to control with medicines, especially diabetes, kidney failure and thin bones?

This question combines the areas covered by two questions in the GAQ document and 1 of the 7 questions in each of the PAR-Q + and SPARQ documents. Duplication and overlap have been avoided with this question and the criticism of long-windedness that was in the PAR-Q + is also avoided, especially with some relatively common examples relevant to chronic medical conditions that may be a cause for concern prior to engaging in PE. In addition to being comprehensive, it adopts a personalized approach that directly addresses the potential exercise participant.

Q6. Do you have aches and pains in your bones, joints and muscles that severely limits ability to perform physical exercise?

With the very large global burden of musculoskeletal diseases and the relatively high numbers of patients with bone, joint and soft-tissue problems, especially with pain (either acute or chronic) that we encountered in the surveys carried out, the need to identify such persons was evident [58, 62]. While acute pain requires rest, recovery of chronic pain requires the patient to continue being physically active despite the pain [63, 64]. The International Olympic Committee recommends an element of musculoskeletal screening to detect current injuries because a previous injury is the most consistent risk factor for recurrent injuries [65]. Misunderstanding with this question will be minimized because the participant does not need to consider whether there is a difference between an ache or a pain or worry about the severity of the symptom when answering this question.

Q7. Have you had a head injury over the last one year with subsequent persistent headaches, or nausea or increasing loss of concentration?

It has been well established that athletes with recent concussion and persistent post-concussion symptoms who participate in high intensity sports have worse neurocognitive performance than those who undergo moderate levels of activity [66]. The duration of recovery after a concussion can vary tremendously depending on the active treatment measures that have been instituted for the patient. It is also known that early, graduated return to physical activity promotes early recovery to pre-morbid status [67]. The use of lay descriptors of the common symptoms of post-concussion syndrome will aid comprehension and highlight only those who would need further referral prior to engaging in increased physical exercise.

Q8. Do you feel unwell over the last one week with either fever, sore throat, cough, vomiting or diarrhoea?

Heat disorders occur with strenuous physical activity in many environments, both temperate and tropical and are more likely after mild illnesses such as viral infections and gastroenteritis in the few days prior to intense PE. A retrospective review of two large USA-based cardiovascular registries revealed that 7% of sudden deaths were due to myocarditis, of which 60% occurred during or just after physical activity and amongst whom 35% had documented viral illness and 15% nausea/abdominal pain [68, 69]. Heat illnesses were not covered in the PAR-Q + and the GAQ, but is relevant in both temperate and tropical environments. Again, the question is concise, uses only lay terms and is unlikely to cause difficulty in understanding.

Discussion

The objective of this paper was to use the lessons learnt from three previous surveys conducted, the high medical referral rates encountered with previously used pre-participation questionnaires, and the lack of clear evidence of extensive use of such tools, to propose the use of a document that, if well and actively publicized and implemented, has potential for demonstration of benefit. Previously used tools, have previously, not been tested for public feedback. Such feedback is extremely important if we intend to use tools that would be self-administered by lay persons. What’s special about this paper is that it proposes a tool that is based on studies that have actively sought public feedback, and used the level of evidence currently available in the scientific literature as proof of relevance and usefulness.

The three surveys referred to, show the following:

Medical referral rates exceeding 20% for those who undertake currently available questionnaires. This is also evident in previous studies with the PAR-Q + and with the GAQ [70, 71]. In any population, if one were to expect high usage of self-administered pre-participation tools, even once annually, prior to either engaging in or increasing one’s level of PE, such screening should be simple, convenient, and support their motivation and confidence to exercise [72]. The PAR-Q + was not found to be adequate in certain populations [73]. However, if use of the screening tool leads to a need for frequent medical referral, then such tools could become a disincentive to participate in PE. In addition, unnecessary referrals for diagnostic testing can lead to high rates of false-positives, which can result in additional costly noninvasive and invasive testing, and usually overwhelm the medical resources in most communities, especially when applied to general community screening [38]. The potential exists that if only persons with uncontrolled medical conditions were to be referred for further screening, the proportion of such referrals could fall to as low as 2.7% which would be less likely to overwhelm medical resources in most communities [52]. It is hoped that the focus on uncontrolled medical conditions in the IPESQ would be likely to result in a low medical referral rate.

Language of the questionnaires with medical jargon and concurrent difficulty in understanding. That understanding of the language of these questionnaires was an issue, especially owing to use of medical jargon, lack of clear definitions and ambiguities in use of words was clear in all three surveys done. Surveys on other similar questionnaires have also demonstrated sub-optimal readability [74]. The challenge for those developing guidelines and public participation questionnaires is to ensure that the questions designed are easily understood by those who should be using them, and especially by those with uncontrolled medical conditions likely to require medical referral. The three studies all showed that the difficulty of understanding the words used in the questionnaires was greater, for almost all the questions, amongst those with medical conditions than for those without co-morbidities. Much effort has been expended to simplify the language of the proposed IPESQ, including removal of technical medical terms. The document also underwent a process of user-acceptance testing, not by volunteers of high educational background, but by randomly accessed members of the public participating in a local brisk-walking session, so as to gain an appreciation of its language and understanding.

The usefulness of the questions in the IPESQ may only be determined by adopting the document over time amongst large numbers of sports participants and then determining rates of injury or medical emergency amongst those using the tool versus those not doing so. This requires active implementation of the tool in multiple communities and monitoring of outcomes to determine whether those who use the questionnaire had any lowering of sports emergency rates over time. The relevance of the themes on which these questions have been determined by authors of previously designed and used questionnaires have been accepted in general with refinements and suitable adjustments made to reflect areas not previously covered.

Limitations

The methodology used to derive the IPESQ had some limitations. The sample size for the user-acceptance testing was relatively small and the findings may limit its generalizability. However, the objective of that testing was to determine its usability in a general population. In addition, the study did not include a separate formal psychometric evaluation process for the following reasons:

Internal consistency was reasonably addressed through the user acceptance testing mechanism, though test-retest reliability was not carried out and could not have been done since such would have required a captive population.

Both content and construct validity were addressed by review of the relevant domains covered in previously developed questionnaires and their theoretical construct.

Factor analysis to evaluate the internal structure was already carried out since public feedback from three previously surveyed questionnaires was actively considered in drafting the IPESQ questions.

We ensured that there were no negative questions.

Item-total correlations were considered to ensure that each of the questions in the IPESQ were not outliers to be removed and were addressing issues that appeared to be relevant to the occurrence of sports emergencies.

We recognize and acknowledge that as a self-report instrument, the IPESQ, like other existing self-administered questionnaire is subject to response bias, under-reporting of symptoms, and potential misinterpretation of one’s own health status.

The study team also did not conduct a separate direct head-to-head comparison of the IPESQ with the PAR-Q and the GAQ within the same population. This would have been challenging in any population where previous population surveys on the PAR-Q, GAQ and a previous locally constructed questionnaire had already been conducted with the problems of questionnaire fatigue in the population surveyed.

Conclusions

The proposed IPESQ may be relevant to an international audience, since cardiovascular collapse, injuries and heat illnesses are universal phenomena and not just restricted to the local community. The causes of these would also tend to be similar. The lessons learnt from careful study of previously used international and local pre-participation questionnaires have been used to create the proposed IPESQ. The IPESQ can be regarded as a promising but not yet proven instrument to minimize the occurrence of sports emergencies.
