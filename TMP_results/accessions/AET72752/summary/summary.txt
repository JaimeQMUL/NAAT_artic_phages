Paper: PMC13159264.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:
- **Temperature Gradient:** The study analyzed microbial communities across a natural thermal gradient ranging from $50^	ext{o}	ext{C}$ to $93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$.
- **Community Shifts:** Distinct community shifts were observed correlating with temperature: moderate-temperature springs ($50–70^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) were dominated by certain phyla; high-temperature springs ($70–90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) enriched specific taxa; and extreme-temperature springs ($	ext{≥}90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) were characterized by distinct, specialized lineages.
- **Taxa Specificity:** Specific phyla showed temperature preferences: e.g., certain groups dominated at $50^	ext{o}	ext{C}$ to $90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$ but declined at $93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$; another phylum was noted to dominate exclusively at $93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$.
- **Adaptation Trends:** Thermophilic lineages in extreme-temperature environments exhibited genomic streamlining, specialized metabolic functions, and narrower ecological niches, consistent with adaptation to persistent thermal stress.
- **Metabolic Pathways:** Carbon fixation pathways shifted with temperature, moving from CBB cycle (moderate temps) towards rTCA, DH, and HH cycles (extreme temps).
- **Genome Stability:** Hyperthermophilic lineages showed adaptations like increased GC content in tRNA and rRNA, and shifts in amino acid composition towards stability-enhancing residues.

Environmental Context:
- **Habitat/Source:** Western Sichuan Plateau hot springs, spanning a broad thermal gradient ($50–93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$). The environment is characterized by geothermal activity and variable geochemical conditions.

Concise Summary:
Metagenomic analysis across a $50–93^	ext{o}	ext{C}$ thermal gradient in Western Sichuan hot springs demonstrates that temperature is a primary driver of microbial community structure, metabolic adaptation, and genome evolution. Community composition shifts systematically: moderate temperatures ($50–70^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) favor certain phyla utilizing CBB cycles; high temperatures ($70–90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) enrich taxa employing the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway; and extreme temperatures ($	ext{≥}90^	ext{o}	ext{C}$) are dominated by lineages utilizing specialized autotrophic pathways (rTCA, DH/HH cycles).
Thermophilic lineages in extreme environments exhibit clear signs of adaptation, including genome streamlining, reduced regulatory complexity, and narrower ecological niches. Genomic adaptations include increased GC content and amino acid shifts enhancing thermal stability. Metabolic specialization is evident in the transition of energy conservation strategies, moving from diverse heterotrophic pathways at lower temperatures to highly efficient, coupled autotrophic cycles (S/C/N) at higher temperatures. The overall pattern suggests that increasing thermal stress drives greater metabolic specialization and genomic reduction, consistent with adaptation to persistent thermal stress.

Paper: PMC13001362.1.txt
Thermal Evidence:
- Temperature emerged as the strongest driver structuring viral communities, outpacing the effects of salinity, depth, dissolved oxygen, chlorophyll-a, and geography.
- Viral diversity was consistently higher in the northern warmer waters (7.5–10.4 °C), while southern colder sites (4.0–7.4 °C) harbored less diverse and compositionally distinct communities.
- Giant viruses shift from Pandoravirales and AG_04 (Algavirales) at lower temperatures to increasing dominance of IM_01 (Imitervirales) and Prasinoviridae (AG_01) in warmer habitats.
- Prokaryotic virus communities also displayed strong temperature structuring, with additional influence from salinity.
- For giant viruses, 65.1% of total gvOTUs were detected exclusively in samples at higher temperatures, with the majority assigned to the Prasinoviridae (AG_01).
- In contrast, only 14.3% of gvOTUs were exclusive to colder temperatures, most of which belonged to the IM_01 family.
- Temperature shifts resulted in distinct community transitions: AG_04 family and Pandoravirales dominated below 6.1 °C, IM_01 family became prominent between 6.4 °C and 7.5 °C, and AG_01 family dominated above 8.9 °C.
- For prokaryotic viruses, community composition differed markedly between colder and warmer samples.
- PERMANOVA confirmed that temperature had a statistically significant effect on beta diversity in both viral communities (Giant viruses: R² = 0.30, p < 0.05; Prokaryotic viruses: R² = 0.21, p < 0.05), underscoring temperature as a key structuring force.

Environmental Context:
- Subantarctic Chilean Patagonia fjords, spanning a latitudinal gradient with temperatures ranging from 4 to 10 °C.

Concise Summary:
Temperature is identified as the primary ecological driver structuring both giant virus and prokaryotic virus communities in the subantarctic Chilean Patagonia fjords. Viral diversity is significantly higher in the warmer northern waters (7.5–10.4 °C) compared to the colder southern sites (4.0–7.4 °C). This temperature dependence is evident in the taxonomic composition of giant viruses, which shift from Pandoravirales/AG_04 at lower temperatures to increased dominance of IM_01 and Prasinoviridae (AG_01) in warmer habitats. Prokaryotic virus communities also show strong temperature structuring. The analysis confirms that temperature is the dominant factor explaining beta diversity for both viral groups. The study highlights the environmental sensitivity of these viral communities to temperature-driven changes, suggesting potential vulnerability to climate warming.
